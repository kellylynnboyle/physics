## Key concepts
| Concept | Statement / Equation |
|---|---|
| Momentum | p = mv (vector, kg·m/s) |
| Impulse | J = FΔt = Δp (vector, N·s) |
| Impulse–momentum theorem | F_avg Δt = m(v_f − v_i) |
| Conservation of momentum | In an isolated system: Σp_before = Σp_after |
| Perfectly inelastic | Objects stick: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁+m₂)v_f. KE lost. |
| Elastic | Momentum **and** KE conserved. |
| Explosion / recoil | Starts at rest: total p = 0, so pieces move in opposite directions. |
| Newton's 3rd law link | Forces in the interaction are equal and opposite, for equal times → equal and opposite impulses → momentum conserved. |

## Cornell notes

**Essential question:** *Why are some collisions more dangerous than others, and what quantity is conserved in all of them?*

| Cue | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is momentum? | "Mass in motion." p = mv. Vector, so direction (sign) matters. Heavy and fast = hard to stop. |
| What is impulse? | A force acting over time: J = FΔt. Equals the change in momentum. Units N·s = kg·m/s. |
| Impulse–momentum theorem | FΔt = mΔv. To get the same Δp, **increase time to reduce force** (airbags, crumple zones, bending knees) or decrease time to increase force (karate chop, hammer). |
| Force–time graph | Area under the curve = impulse. |
| Bounce vs. stick | Bounce reverses v so Δv is about 2v; stick gives Δv = v. Bounce → larger impulse (this is why bouncing off a wall hurts more). |
| Conservation of momentum | Isolated system (no net external force): total p constant. Internal forces only redistribute momentum. |
| Solving collisions | 1) Pick + direction. 2) Total p before (watch signs). 3) Total p after. 4) Set equal and solve. 5) Compare KE before/after. |
| Types of collisions | Elastic (KE conserved, e.g., billiard balls approx.). Inelastic (KE lost). Perfectly inelastic (stick; maximum KE loss). **Momentum is conserved in all of them.** |
| Explosions | Start at rest, p_total = 0 → m₁v₁ = −m₂v₂. Lighter piece moves faster. |
| Real-life design (HS-PS2-3) | Crumple zones, helmets, airbags, padded floors, catching with "give": all increase Δt. |
| Common mistake | Using speeds instead of signed velocities; forgetting to add masses after sticking. |

## Most-missed ideas (these appear in the quiz)
1. Units of momentum vs. force vs. energy.
2. Why airbags work (same Δp, longer Δt, smaller F).
3. Using combined mass after a sticky collision.
4. Momentum conserved ≠ kinetic energy conserved.
5. Signs in explosions and recoil.
6. Bounce gives twice the impulse of stopping.
7. Sign flip when a ball rebounds.

## Practice problems
1. Find the momentum of a 0.145 kg baseball at 40 m/s. *[5.8 kg·m/s]*
2. A 1500 kg car going 20 m/s stops in 5 s. Average braking force? In 0.1 s (crash)? *[6000 N; 300,000 N]*
3. A 3 kg cart at 4 m/s hits and sticks to a 1 kg cart at rest. Final speed? KE lost? *[3 m/s; 24 J − 18 J = 6 J lost]*
4. A 60 kg skater pushes off a 40 kg skater at rest; the 40 kg skater glides at 3 m/s. Speed of the 60 kg skater? *[2 m/s, opposite direction]*
5. A 0.2 kg ball hits a wall at 10 m/s and rebounds at 6 m/s. Impulse? If contact is 0.01 s, average force? *[3.2 N·s; 320 N]*
6. Two equal carts: A at 2 m/s hits B at rest, elastic. Final velocities? *[A stops, B moves at 2 m/s]*
7. **Design challenge (HS-PS2-3):** Build a package that keeps an egg safe from a 3 m drop using only paper, tape and straws. Calculate the impulse needed and the stopping time you achieved.

- **Collision cart lab (HS-PS2-2):** measure velocities before/after elastic (magnet or spring) and inelastic (Velcro) collisions. Compute total p and KE with % difference.
- **Egg-drop / helmet design (HS-PS2-3):** design, test, refine; document the force–time idea with a slow-motion video.
- **Rebound lab:** compare impulse from a bouncing ball vs. a clay ball dropped on a force plate or scale.

## Real-world connections
- Automotive safety: airbags, seat belts, crumple zones, guardrail attenuators on California highways.
- Sports: baseball bats, boxing gloves, catching a fast ball, football helmets and concussions.
- Space: rockets (momentum of exhaust), satellite docking, debris collisions.
- Forensics: accident reconstruction using skid marks and momentum.
- Nature: recoil of a gun, a squid's jet propulsion.
