## Key concepts
| Concept | Statement / Equation |
|---|---|
| 1st law (inertia) | An object keeps its velocity (rest or constant v) unless a **net** external force acts. |
| 2nd law | a = ΣF / m, or ΣF = ma (vector; same direction as net force). |
| 3rd law | If A pushes on B, B pushes on A with equal size and opposite direction. Pairs act on **different** objects. |
| Weight | F_g = mg (g = 9.8 m/s² on Earth; ≈ 1.6 on Moon). |
| Normal force | Contact force perpendicular to the surface. Not always equal to weight. |
| Friction | Static: f_s ≤ μ_s N. Kinetic: f_k = μ_k N. Opposes (relative) motion. |
| Tension | Pulling force transmitted through a rope. |
| Terminal velocity | Air drag grows with speed until ΣF = 0, then v is constant. |
| Newton (N) | 1 N = 1 kg·m/s² |

## Cornell notes

**Essential question:** *How do forces change the motion of objects, and how can we predict it?*

| Cue | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is a force? | A push or pull; a vector measured in newtons. Forces come from interactions between two objects. |
| Net force | Vector sum of all forces. Same direction → add. Opposite → subtract. Balanced forces: ΣF = 0 → a = 0 (constant v, not necessarily at rest). |
| 1st law | Inertia = resistance to change in motion; mass is the measure of inertia. No force needed to keep moving at constant v. |
| 2nd law | ΣF = ma. For the same force, bigger m → smaller a (inverse). For the same m, bigger F → bigger a (direct). |
| Free-body diagram (FBD) | Dot for the object. Arrows from the dot for each force **on** that object only: weight ↓, normal ↑ (⊥ surface), friction (parallel, opposite motion), applied, tension, drag. Never draw the object's own motion as a force. |
| 3rd law | Pairs: same type, equal size, opposite direction, **different objects**, so they never cancel. Example: Earth pulls you ↔ you pull Earth. Rocket pushes gas ↔ gas pushes rocket. |
| Mass vs. weight | Mass: amount of matter (kg), constant. Weight: gravitational force (N), depends on location. W = mg. |
| Friction facts | f = μN. Independent of contact area and (roughly) speed. Static > kinetic. Rolling is much smaller. |
| Elevator / scale | Scale reads the normal force. Accelerating up: N = m(g + a). Free fall: N = 0 (apparent weightlessness). |
| Inclined plane | Weight splits: parallel mg sin θ, perpendicular mg cos θ. N = mg cos θ. |
| Strategy | 1) Sketch. 2) FBD. 3) Choose + direction. 4) Write ΣF = ma. 5) Solve. 6) Check units/sign. |

## Most-missed ideas (these appear in the quiz)
1. Constant velocity means zero net force (no "force of motion").
2. Collision forces are equal and opposite regardless of mass.
3. Normal force and weight are not a third-law pair.
4. Mass ≠ weight.
5. Use net force in a = F/m.
6. Scale reading (normal force) changes in an accelerating elevator.
7. Friction depends on the normal force, not area.

## Practice problems (g = 10 m/s²)
1. A 2 kg cart is pushed with a net force of 6 N. Find a. *[3 m/s²]*
2. A 1200 kg car accelerates at 2.5 m/s². What net force acts? *[3000 N]*
3. A 60 kg student stands in an elevator accelerating up at 2 m/s². Scale reading? Accelerating down at 2 m/s²? *[720 N; 480 N]*
4. A 20 kg box on a floor with μ_k = 0.25 is pushed at constant speed. Required push? *[50 N]*
5. A 5 kg block is pulled by 40 N right; friction is 15 N. Find a. *[5 m/s²]*
6. Draw FBDs for: (a) skydiver at terminal velocity, (b) book on a table, (c) a car braking. *[Check: (a) weight = drag, (b) weight = normal, (c) friction backward, weight = normal]*
7. **Challenge (Atwood):** 3 kg and 2 kg masses over a frictionless pulley. Find a and tension. *[a = 2 m/s²; T = 24 N]*

## Lab / hands-on
- **Cart and hanging mass lab (HS-PS2-1):** keep total mass constant, vary pulling force; graph a vs. F (slope = 1/m). Then keep force constant and vary mass; graph a vs. 1/m. Write a claim–evidence–reasoning (CER).
- **Friction lab:** drag a block with a spring scale; vary mass and surface; calculate μ.
- **Egg-drop or crash-cart design (links to HS-PS2-3):** protect an egg using the idea of force, time and momentum (see Unit 4).
- **Balloon rocket:** demonstrate the third law and test variables.

## Real-world connections
- Automotive: seat belts, anti-lock brakes, traction on wet roads, towing capacity.
- Sports: sprint starts (pushing back on the ground), tug of war, trampolines.
- Aerospace: rocket launches, why astronauts feel weightless in orbit (free fall).
- Amusement parks: roller-coaster g-forces as normal force changes.
- California: earthquake-resistant buildings use inertia (base isolation, tuned mass dampers).
