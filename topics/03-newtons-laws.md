# Unit 3: Newton's Laws

**Pacing:** ~4 weeks · **Quiz:** [Newton's Laws](../quiz/index.html) · **Standards:** HS-PS2-1, HS-PS2-3 (also HS-PS2-4 gravitational force at the end of the unit)

> Forces explain why motion changes. Net force → acceleration. Always start with a free-body diagram.

## Learning objectives

- [ ] **N1.** State Newton's three laws and give an example of each (HS-PS2-1).
- [ ] **N2.** Draw a labelled free-body diagram for any situation (flat, incline, hanging, elevator).
- [ ] **N3.** Apply ΣF = ma in one dimension and along an incline.
- [ ] **N4.** Distinguish mass from weight and identify the normal, tension, friction and drag forces.
- [ ] **N5.** Identify action-reaction pairs correctly (two objects, equal and opposite).
- [ ] **N6.** Calculate static and kinetic friction using μ and N.
- [ ] **N7.** Design, build and evaluate a device that reduces the force on an object during a collision (HS-PS2-3).

## Key concepts and equations

| Concept | Statement / equation |
|---|---|
| 1st law (inertia) | An object keeps its velocity (including rest) unless a net force acts. ΣF = 0 ⇔ a = 0 |
| 2nd law | a = ΣF / m, or ΣF = ma (N = kg·m/s²) |
| 3rd law | If A pushes on B, B pushes on A with equal magnitude and opposite direction |
| Weight | W = mg (a force, in N) |
| Normal force | Perpendicular contact force; found from ΣF, not always equal to mg |
| Friction | Kinetic f_k = μ_k N; static f_s ≤ μ_s N |
| Tension | Force along a rope; same magnitude on both ends for an ideal rope and pulley |
| Incline (angle θ) | Parallel: mg sinθ; perpendicular: mg cosθ (N = mg cosθ with no other vertical force) |
| Elevator | N = m(g + a), a positive upward |
| Terminal velocity | Drag = weight, so a = 0 |
| Universal gravitation | F = Gm₁m₂ / r² |

**Free-body diagram recipe:** (1) isolate one object, (2) draw only forces **on** it, (3) label each force by its source, (4) pick axes, (5) write ΣF = ma for each axis.

## Cornell notes

| Cue / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What does the 1st law say? | No net force means constant velocity (maybe zero). Objects resist changes in motion: inertia, measured by mass. |
| Does motion require a force? | No. Changing motion requires a net force. Friction is why things stop in everyday life. |
| What does the 2nd law say? | a = ΣF/m. Acceleration points in the direction of net force; bigger mass means smaller a. |
| Mass vs. weight? | Mass: amount of matter, in kg, constant. Weight: gravitational force, mg, in N, changes with location. |
| What does the 3rd law say? | Forces come in pairs on **different** objects, same type, equal size, opposite direction. |
| Which forces are NOT a 3rd-law pair? | Weight and normal force on a resting object. Both act on the same object. |
| How do I handle friction? | Static friction matches the applied force up to μ_s N. Once sliding, it is f_k = μ_k N (usually smaller). |
| How do I solve an incline? | Tilt axes: along and perpendicular to the slope. a = g sinθ − μ g cosθ going down. |
| Why does a scale change in an elevator? | The scale reads the normal force. Accelerating up → N > mg. Accelerating down → N < mg. |
| What is terminal velocity? | When drag grows to equal weight, net force is zero and speed stops increasing. |

**Summary (write your own):** _Net force produces acceleration in proportion to 1/mass. Forces come in pairs that act on different objects. Free-body diagrams turn word problems into ΣF = ma equations._

## Common misconceptions

1. "Motion requires a continued force."
2. "The bigger object exerts a bigger force in a collision."
3. "Weight and normal force are an action-reaction pair."
4. "Weight and mass are the same."
5. "A scale shows weight." *(It shows the normal force.)*
6. "Friction = μN always." *(Not for static friction below its maximum.)*
7. "A falling object at terminal velocity still has a net downward force."
8. "Tension equals the weight of the hanging mass." *(Only if a = 0.)*

## Practice problems (g = 10 m/s²)

| # | Problem | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A 1500 kg car feels a net force of 3000 N. Find its acceleration. | 2 m/s² |
| 2 | A 10 kg crate is pulled with 50 N against 20 N friction. Find a. | 3 m/s² |
| 3 | A 70 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating at 2 m/s² (a) up, (b) down. Find the readings. | (a) 840 N; (b) 560 N |
| 4 | A 2 kg and a 3 kg mass hang over a frictionless pulley. Find a and the tension. | 2 m/s²; 24 N |
| 5 | A frictionless 30° incline: find a for a block released from rest. | 5 m/s² |
| 6 | A 20 kg box on a floor with μ_s = 0.5 and μ_k = 0.3. What force starts it moving? What force keeps it moving at constant speed? | > 100 N; 60 N |
| 7 | A 4 kg block on a 37° incline with μ_k = 0.25 slides down. Find a. (sin37° = 0.6, cos37° = 0.8) | 6 − 2 = 4 m/s² |
| 8 | Name the 3rd-law partner of: (a) Earth pulls on you; (b) your foot pushes the ground; (c) the bat hits the ball. | (a) You pull on Earth; (b) ground pushes your foot; (c) ball hits bat |
| 9 | *Challenge:* A 5 kg block is pulled by a 30 N horizontal force across μ_k = 0.2. A second 3 kg block with the same μ_k is tied behind it by a rope. Find a and the rope tension. | a = (30 − 0.2·80)/8 = 1.75 m/s²; T = 3(1.75 + 2) = 11.25 N |

## Lab and activity ideas

- **Forces and Motion: Basics / Friction (PhET):** apply forces, predict motion, then test.
- **Atwood machine:** measure a for different mass pairs, compare with a = (m₂ − m₁)g/(m₁ + m₂).
- **Friction coefficient lab:** find the angle at which a block just starts to slide; μ_s = tanθ.
- **Egg-drop or crumple-zone design (HS-PS2-3):** build a device, measure impact, and optimize the force-time trade-off with a force sensor or slow-motion video.
- **Elevator scale:** use a bathroom scale and a phone accelerometer in a lift.

## Real-world connections

- **Automotive safety:** seat belts (1st law), crumple zones and airbags (2nd law).
- **Spaceflight:** rocket thrust is a 3rd-law pair with exhaust.
- **Construction:** cranes, elevators and cable tension limits.
- **Sports:** footwear traction (friction), cycling aerodynamics (drag).
- **Skydiving:** parachute drag and terminal velocity.
