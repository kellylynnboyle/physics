# Unit 3: Newton's Laws

**Suggested pacing:** about 4 weeks · **Standards:** HS-PS2-1 (primary). Also supports HS-PS2-4 (gravitational and electrostatic forces) when extended. See [docs/standards-alignment.md](../docs/standards-alignment.md).

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- [ ] **N1.** State Newton's three laws and identify each in everyday situations.
- [ ] **N2.** Draw accurate free-body diagrams for objects at rest, at constant velocity and accelerating.
- [ ] **N3.** Find the net force and use ΣF = ma to calculate acceleration, mass or force.
- [ ] **N4.** Distinguish mass from weight and explain apparent weight in an accelerating elevator.
- [ ] **N5.** Identify action–reaction pairs and explain why they never cancel.
- [ ] **N6.** Model friction (static and kinetic) and forces on an inclined plane.
- [ ] **N7.** Analyze data to support the claim that a = F_net/m (HS-PS2-1).

## Key concepts

| Concept | What students must be able to say or do |
|---|---|
| First law (inertia) | No net force ⇒ constant velocity (including at rest). Force changes motion; it isn't needed to keep it. |
| Second law | ΣF = ma. Direction of a equals direction of net force. |
| Third law | Forces come in pairs: same type, equal size, opposite direction, **on different objects**. |
| Free-body diagram | Draw only forces *on* the object: weight (mg), normal (N), friction (f), tension (T), applied (F). |
| Mass vs. weight | Mass is invariant (kg). Weight W = mg (N), depends on location. |
| Normal force | Perpendicular contact force; not always equal to mg (elevators, inclines, extra pushes). |
| Friction | f_k = μ_k N (sliding); f_s ≤ μ_s N (not sliding). Opposes relative motion or its tendency. |
| Inclines | Weight component along slope: mg sinθ. Perpendicular: mg cosθ (balanced by N). |
| Systems | Connected objects share acceleration; find the system a first, then forces on parts. |

## Cornell notes

| Cue / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is Newton's first law? | An object keeps its velocity (rest or constant) unless a net force acts. Inertia = resistance to changes in motion. |
| What does a net force of zero mean? | Constant velocity or rest. Forces are balanced. It does **not** mean no forces. |
| What is Newton's second law? | ΣF = ma. Units: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². |
| How do I draw a free-body diagram? | Dot for object; arrows for each force on it from the dot; label. No velocity arrows; no forces the object exerts. |
| What is Newton's third law? | If A pushes on B, B pushes on A equally and oppositely. Pairs are on different objects, so they don't cancel. |
| Is the normal force the partner of weight? | No. Weight (Earth on book) pairs with book on Earth. Normal (table on book) pairs with book on table. |
| Mass vs. weight? | Mass: kg, doesn't change. Weight: N, W = mg. |
| How does friction work? | Opposes sliding. f_k = μ_k N. If static, friction matches the applied force up to f_s,max. |
| What happens on an incline? | Parallel: mg sinθ. Perpendicular: N = mg cosθ. Frictionless a = g sinθ. |
| What does a scale read in an accelerating elevator? | Up: N = m(g + a). Down: N = m(g − a). Free fall: N = 0 (feels weightless). |

**Summary (student writes 3–4 sentences):** Explain how Newton's three laws work together to predict the motion of an object, using a pushed shopping cart as an example.

## Most-missed questions

The [interactive quiz](../quiz/index.html) covers:

1. Constant velocity means zero net force.
2. a = F/m (divide, don't multiply).
3. Third-law forces are equal even in a truck–car collision.
4. Mass vs. weight on the Moon.
5. Apparent weight in an accelerating elevator.
6. Normal force is not the third-law partner of weight.
7. Constant velocity: friction equals the push.
8. Use net force, not one force, in ΣF = ma.
9. Doubling mass halves acceleration.
10. Acceleration on an incline: g sinθ.

## Practice problems

<details>
<summary>Problems (answers below)</summary>

1. A 1200 kg car's engine produces 3000 N of forward force against 600 N of friction. Find the acceleration.
2. A 70 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator. What does the scale read when the elevator accelerates (a) up at 2 m/s² and (b) down at 2 m/s²?
3. A 5 kg block is pulled across a floor by a 30 N horizontal force. If μ_k = 0.30, find the acceleration.
4. A 10 kg block slides down a frictionless 30° incline. Find its acceleration and the normal force.
5. A 10 N horizontal force pushes a 2 kg block that touches a 3 kg block on a frictionless table. Find the acceleration and the force the 2 kg block exerts on the 3 kg block.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. a = (3000 − 600)/1200 = **2.0 m/s²**.
2. (a) N = 70(9.8 + 2) = **826 N**. (b) N = 70(9.8 − 2) = **546 N**. (Rest reading: 686 N.)
3. N = 5(9.8) = 49 N; f = 0.30 × 49 = 14.7 N; a = (30 − 14.7)/5 = **3.06 m/s²**.
4. a = 9.8 sin30° = **4.9 m/s²**; N = 98 cos30° ≈ **84.9 N**.
5. a = 10/(2 + 3) = **2 m/s²**; force on 3 kg block = 3 × 2 = **6 N**.

</details>

## Lab and hands-on ideas

- **Fan cart / Atwood-style a = F/m lab (HS-PS2-1):** vary hanging mass (force) with total mass held constant; then vary total mass with force held constant. Graph a vs. F and a vs. 1/m. This is a direct data-analysis task for the standard.
- **Friction coefficient:** tilt a board until a block just slips (μ_s = tanθ), then measure kinetic friction with a spring scale.
- **Force sensors:** two sensors hooked together to show third-law pairs in real time.
- **Bathroom scale elevator:** record scale readings in a school elevator and compare to N = m(g ± a).
- **PhET:** *Forces and Motion: Basics*, *Friction*, *Ramp: Forces and Motion*.

## Real-world applications

- **Seat belts and headrests:** inertia keeps you moving when the car stops; belts supply the net force.
- **Rockets and swimming:** third-law propulsion (push back on exhaust or water; it pushes you forward).
- **Elevator and amusement-park ride design:** apparent weight limits.
- **Anti-lock brakes:** keeping tires in static (not kinetic) friction for better stopping force.
- **Ramps and accessibility:** gentler incline angles lower the force needed to move loads.
