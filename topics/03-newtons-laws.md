# Unit 3 – Newton's Laws

NGSS: HS-PS2-1, HS-PS2-3 · Objectives N1–N5 (see [standards-and-assessment.md](../standards-and-assessment.md))

## Key concepts
- Force as a vector; net force
- First law (inertia), second law (F_net = ma), third law (action–reaction)
- Free-body diagrams; weight, normal, friction, tension
- Mass vs weight; inclines

## Cornell notes

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| What does Newton's first law say? | An object keeps its velocity (rest or constant velocity) unless a **net external force** acts. Constant velocity ⇒ net force = 0 (forces balanced), not "no forces". **Inertia**: resistance to change in motion; measured by mass. |
| What does Newton's second law say, and how do I use it? | a = F_net/m (or F_net = ma). Units: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². Ex: 10 kg, net force 30 N: a = 3 m/s². a ∝ F_net, a ∝ 1/m. |
| What is Newton's third law, and how do I find a force pair? | Forces come in pairs: A on B equals B on A in magnitude, opposite in direction, same type, **on different objects**, so they never cancel. Ex: book on table: weight's pair is the book's gravitational pull on Earth, **not** the normal force. A truck hitting a car: equal forces; the car's smaller mass gives it a larger acceleration. |
| What goes in a free-body diagram? | One object as a dot; arrows for forces **on that object only**, sized to magnitude: F_g (down), F_N (perpendicular to surface), friction (parallel to surface, opposing motion/attempted motion), tension (along rope), applied force. Then sum components. |
| What is the difference between mass and weight? | Mass (kg) is amount of matter, same everywhere. Weight F_g = mg (N) depends on gravity. 5 kg: 49 N on Earth, about 8 N on the Moon, still 5 kg. |
| How do I model friction and the normal force? | f_k = μ_k F_N (sliding), f_s ≤ μ_s F_N (static). On flat ground F_N = mg; on an incline F_N = mg cosθ. Ex: 20 kg pushed with 100 N, μ_k = 0.3: f = 58.8 N, net = 41.2 N, a = 2.06 m/s². |
| How does an incline work? | Along the slope: mg sinθ. Frictionless: a = g sinθ (30° → 4.9 m/s²). Perpendicular: F_N = mg cosθ. |
| Why does apparent weight change in an elevator? | Normal force is what a scale reads: F_N = m(g + a) accelerating up. 60 kg, a = 2 m/s² up: 60(11.8) = 708 N. Accelerating down it is smaller; free fall gives 0 (weightless). |

**Memory tricks:** "ΣF = ma" · "Balanced forces ⇒ no change in velocity" · "Pairs act on different objects" · "SOH-CAH-TOA for components."

**Summary:** Forces cause changes in velocity. Draw an FBD, resolve into components, apply ΣF = ma along each axis. Third-law pairs are always on two different objects.

## Practice problems (g = 9.8 m/s²)
1. A 1200 kg car accelerates at 2.5 m/s². Net force? **3000 N**
2. A 10 kg block on a frictionless 30° incline. Acceleration? **4.9 m/s² down the slope**
3. Pushing a 20 kg crate with 100 N, μ_k = 0.3. Acceleration? **2.06 m/s²**
4. Draw FBDs for: a book on a table; a skydiver at terminal velocity; a car braking.
5. Explain, using the third law, how a rocket accelerates in space.

## Real-world applications
Seat belts and crumple zones (HS-PS2-3) · rocket launches · braking distance on wet vs dry roads · elevators and amusement-park rides · why lighter cars accelerate faster.
