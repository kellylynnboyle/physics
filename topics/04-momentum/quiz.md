# Quiz — Momentum & Collisions: Most Commonly Missed Questions

Take this quiz *before* checking any answers.

---

**1.** A 2 kg ball moving at +5 m/s bounces off a wall and moves away at −5 m/s. What is the change in momentum, Δp?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Δp = m(v_f − v_i) = 2(−5 − 5) = −20 kg·m/s. The most common error is computing Δp = m(v_f) − m(v_i) using speeds only (ignoring the sign flip on direction), which would incorrectly give zero change. Because the ball reverses direction, the change in momentum is large, not small — this is exactly why bouncing collisions involve bigger impulses than collisions where an object just stops.
</details>

---

**2.** In any closed-system collision (no external forces), is kinetic energy always conserved?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

No — only momentum is *always* conserved in a closed system. Kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions. This is the single most common momentum-unit misconception: students generalize "conservation" from momentum to energy without checking the collision type.
</details>

---

**3.** Two ice skaters, initially at rest, push off from each other. Skater A (50 kg) moves away at 4 m/s. If skater B has a mass of 100 kg, what is B's speed?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Total momentum starts at zero (both at rest), so it must stay zero: 0 = m_A·v_A + m_B·v_B → 0 = (50)(4) + (100)(v_B) → v_B = −2 m/s (i.e., 2 m/s in the opposite direction). A common mistake is forgetting that the two skaters must move in *opposite* directions to keep total momentum at zero, and instead just dividing masses without considering direction.
</details>

---

**4.** Why do airbags reduce injury in a car crash, in terms of impulse?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

The change in momentum (Δp) of a passenger during a crash is fixed by the crash itself (mass and change in velocity). An airbag increases the time (Δt) over which that momentum change happens, and since FΔt = Δp is constant, a larger Δt means a smaller average force F on the passenger. A common wrong answer is "airbags absorb the force" without connecting it to the time-extension mechanism.
</details>

---

**5.** A 3 kg cart moving at 4 m/s collides and sticks to a stationary 1 kg cart. Is momentum conserved? Is kinetic energy conserved?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Momentum: yes, always (in a closed system) — total momentum before = total after. Kinetic energy: no — this is a perfectly inelastic collision (they stick together), and perfectly inelastic collisions always lose the maximum possible kinetic energy for a given momentum. Compute both: p_before = 3(4) = 12 kg·m/s; v_final = 12/4 = 3 m/s; KE_before = ½(3)(4²) = 24 J; KE_after = ½(4)(3²) = 18 J. KE dropped from 24 J to 18 J — confirming it's inelastic, even though momentum matched exactly.
</details>

---

**6.** A heavy truck and a light motorcycle collide head-on. Which object experiences the larger change in velocity?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

The motorcycle (the less massive object). Both experience the same impulse (equal and opposite forces over the same time, by Newton's 3rd Law), so Δp is equal in magnitude for both — but since Δp = mΔv, the smaller mass requires a much larger Δv to have the same momentum change. Students often think the more massive object "does more" to itself, when actually the effect (in terms of velocity change) is worse for the less massive object.
</details>

---

**7.** Why does follow-through (continuing your swing after contact) increase the distance a golf ball or baseball travels?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Follow-through increases the time of contact between the club/bat and the ball, increasing the impulse (J = FΔt) delivered for roughly the same applied force, which increases the ball's change in momentum (and therefore its exit velocity). A common wrong answer focuses only on "hitting harder" (force) rather than recognizing that time of contact is an equally important variable.
</details>
