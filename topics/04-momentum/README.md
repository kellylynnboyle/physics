# Momentum and Collisions

Momentum is a way of measuring "quantity of motion" that combines how much mass an object has with how fast it's moving. This topic builds the tools to analyze collisions and impacts — from car crashes to billiard balls — using the impulse-momentum theorem and the law of conservation of momentum. Unlike energy, momentum is a vector, so direction matters at every step of the math.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Momentum (p = mv)** — Momentum is a vector quantity equal to an object's mass times its velocity, measured in kg·m/s, and it points in the same direction as the velocity.
- **Impulse (J = FΔt)** — Impulse is the product of the net force applied to an object and the time interval over which it acts, measured in N·s.
- **Impulse-Momentum Theorem (J = Δp)** — The impulse delivered to an object equals its change in momentum, linking force-over-time directly to a change in motion.
- **Force-time graphs** — The area under a force-vs-time graph equals the impulse delivered, even when the force is not constant.
- **Conservation of momentum** — In an isolated system (no net external force), the total momentum before an interaction equals the total momentum after, even though individual objects' momenta can change.
- **Elastic collisions** — In an elastic collision both total momentum and total kinetic energy are conserved, and the objects bounce apart without permanent deformation.
- **Inelastic collisions** — In an inelastic collision total momentum is conserved but total kinetic energy is not, because some energy converts to heat, sound, or deformation.
- **Perfectly inelastic collisions** — When two objects collide and stick together, moving with one common final velocity, the collision is perfectly inelastic and loses the maximum possible kinetic energy while still conserving momentum.
- **Momentum is always conserved, KE is not** — Momentum conservation applies to every isolated collision type; kinetic energy conservation is the special case that only holds for elastic collisions.
- **Signs and direction in 1D** — In one-dimensional problems, velocities and momenta moving in one direction are positive and the other negative, so an object can "lose" momentum by reversing direction (bouncing back).
- **2D momentum conservation** — In two-dimensional collisions, momentum is conserved separately along the x-axis and the y-axis, so each component must be analyzed independently.
- **Newton's third law and collisions** — During a collision, the two objects exert equal and opposite forces on each other for the same time interval, which is why the total momentum of the system doesn't change.

## Learning Objectives

Students will be able to...

1. Calculate the momentum of an object given its mass and velocity, including direction.
2. Calculate the impulse delivered by a constant force over a time interval, and find impulse from a force-vs-time graph.
3. Apply the impulse-momentum theorem to find an unknown force, time, or change in velocity.
4. State and apply the law of conservation of momentum to isolated systems.
5. Distinguish between elastic, inelastic, and perfectly inelastic collisions using energy and momentum criteria.
6. Set up and solve one-dimensional conservation of momentum equations for two-object collisions, including correct use of sign conventions.
7. Analyze a simple two-dimensional collision by conserving momentum independently along perpendicular axes.
8. Explain real-world impulse applications (airbags, crumple zones, follow-through in sports) in terms of extending collision time to reduce force.

## Assessment Criteria

| Level | Descriptor |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Can state the formulas p = mv and J = FΔt but struggles to apply them; confuses momentum with force or energy; cannot set up a conservation of momentum equation without heavy guidance. |
| **Developing** | Can calculate momentum and impulse for single objects; attempts conservation of momentum problems but makes sign errors or forgets that momentum is a vector; inconsistently distinguishes elastic from inelastic collisions. |
| **Proficient** | Correctly solves 1D conservation of momentum problems for both elastic and perfectly inelastic collisions with proper sign conventions; applies the impulse-momentum theorem to multi-step problems; explains why momentum is conserved but KE may not be. |
| **Mastery** | Solves multi-step and simple 2D collision problems independently; correctly analyzes force-time graphs for impulse; connects impulse concepts to real-world safety design with quantitative reasoning; can justify every step using underlying conservation principles. |

**Quantitative proficiency bar:** 80%+ on the topic quiz, with demonstrated ability to correctly set up and solve conservation of momentum equations for 1D collisions (elastic and perfectly inelastic), including correct sign conventions and units.

## Practice Problems

1. A 0.50 kg ball moves at 6.0 m/s to the right. What is its momentum?
2. A shopping cart with a mass of 15 kg is pushed from rest to a velocity of 2.0 m/s. What impulse was delivered to the cart?
3. A 60 kg skater at rest pushes off a wall, and a constant force of 120 N acts on her for 0.50 s. What is her final speed?
4. A 1000 kg car moving at 15 m/s to the east collides head-on with a 1500 kg car moving at 10 m/s to the west, and the two cars stick together. What is their common velocity immediately after the collision (magnitude and direction)?
5. A 0.20 kg billiard ball moving at 3.0 m/s to the right strikes a stationary, identical 0.20 kg billiard ball. If the collision is elastic and the first ball stops completely, what is the velocity of the second ball afterward?
6. A 1200 kg car traveling at 20 m/s crashes into a stationary 1200 kg car, and the two vehicles lock together. (a) Find their common velocity after the crash. (b) Find the kinetic energy lost in the collision.
7. A tennis ball of mass 0.058 kg is served and leaves the racket at 40 m/s after being in contact with the racket for 0.020 s, starting from rest. What average force did the racket exert on the ball?
8. During a car crash test, a 900 kg crash-test dummy's velocity changes from 18 m/s to 0 m/s. In Case A, an airbag extends the stopping time to 0.15 s; in Case B, no airbag is used and the stopping time is 0.015 s. Explain, using the impulse-momentum theorem, why the force experienced in Case A is smaller even though the impulse (change in momentum) is the same in both cases.

### Answers

1. 3.0 kg·m/s to the right
2. 30 kg·m/s (in the direction of the push)
3. 1.0 m/s
4. 0 m/s (the cars come to rest — momenta cancel: +15,000 kg·m/s and −15,000 kg·m/s)
5. 3.0 m/s to the right
6. (a) 10 m/s in the original direction of motion; (b) 120,000 J lost (KE before = 240,000 J, KE after = 120,000 J)
7. 116 N (approximately)
8. Same impulse (Δp) in both cases, but F = Δp/Δt, so the longer time (0.15 s) in Case A produces a much smaller average force than the shorter time (0.015 s) in Case B — the airbag doesn't change the impulse, it spreads it over more time to reduce peak force.

## Real-World Applications

- **Crumple zones in cars** — Vehicle frames are engineered to deform and increase the collision's stopping time, reducing the force transmitted to passengers for the same impulse.
- **Airbags and seatbelts** — Airbags and seatbelts extend the time over which a passenger's momentum changes during a crash, lowering the peak force experienced by the body.
- **Rocket propulsion** — Rockets expel mass (exhaust gas) backward at high speed, and conservation of momentum means the rocket itself gains momentum in the opposite direction.
- **Sports collisions** — Billiards, bowling, and football tackles all rely on momentum transfer between colliding objects or players, with follow-through techniques increasing contact time to control force.
- **Recoil in firearms** — When a gun fires a bullet forward, conservation of momentum requires the gun itself to recoil backward with equal and opposite momentum.
