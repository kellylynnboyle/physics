# Topic 4: Momentum & Collisions

Momentum connects Newton's Laws to a powerful conservation law — one that holds true even during the messy, hard-to-model instant of a collision.

## Key concepts to master

- **Momentum** — p = mv, a vector, measured in kg·m/s
- **Impulse** — J = FΔt, the change in momentum caused by a force acting over a time interval
- **Impulse-momentum theorem** — J = Δp, i.e., FΔt = mΔv
- **Conservation of momentum** — in a closed system (no external forces), total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after: Σp_before = Σp_after
- **Elastic collisions** — both momentum AND kinetic energy are conserved (objects bounce apart)
- **Inelastic collisions** — momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is NOT (some KE converts to heat/sound/deformation)
- **Perfectly inelastic collisions** — the special inelastic case where objects stick together and move with a common final velocity
- **Sign conventions** — direction matters; a consistent positive direction must be chosen for all objects in a collision problem

## Learning objectives

By the end of this topic, students can:
1. Calculate momentum and impulse for an object
2. Apply the impulse-momentum theorem to relate force, time, and change in momentum
3. Apply conservation of momentum to solve 1D elastic and perfectly inelastic collision problems
4. Distinguish elastic from inelastic collisions by comparing total kinetic energy before and after
5. Explain, using FΔt = Δp, why extending contact time reduces the force experienced in a collision

## Practice problems

1. A 0.5 kg ball moving at 6 m/s is struck by a bat and leaves at 8 m/s in the opposite direction. If the bat was in contact for 0.02 s, what average force did the bat exert?
2. A 1000 kg car moving at 15 m/s collides with a stationary 1500 kg car, and they stick together. Find their common velocity after the collision.
3. Using the result from problem 2, calculate the kinetic energy before and after the collision. Was kinetic energy conserved? What does this tell you about the collision type?
4. A 2 kg cart moving at 3 m/s collides elastically with a stationary 2 kg cart. Given that equal masses in an elastic collision simply exchange velocities, what is the final velocity of each cart?
5. Explain, in terms of impulse, why boxers "roll with the punch" and why cars have crumple zones.

## Real-world applications

- **Car safety design** — crumple zones and airbags increase collision time to reduce peak force (FΔt = Δp)
- **Sports** — following through on a swing (baseball, golf, tennis) increases contact time and therefore imparted impulse
- **Rocket propulsion** — momentum conservation between expelled fuel and the rocket
- **Newton's cradle** — a classic elastic-collision demonstration
- **Ballistic pendulum** — a classic experimental method for measuring a bullet's speed using perfectly inelastic collision + energy conservation
