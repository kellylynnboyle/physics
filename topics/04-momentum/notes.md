# Unit 4: Momentum

Momentum extends Newton's Laws to collisions and interactions between
objects. This unit provides direct evidence for NGSS **HS-PS2-2**
(conservation of momentum) and **HS-PS2-3** (designing a device that
minimizes force during a collision).

## Key Concepts

- **Momentum (p)** — p = mv, a vector with the same direction as velocity,
  measured in kg·m/s.
- **Impulse (J)** — J = FΔt = Δp. Impulse is the change in momentum caused
  by a force acting over a time interval. This is the "impulse-momentum
  theorem."
- **Conservation of momentum** — in a closed system (no net external
  force), total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum
  after: p_total,before = p_total,after. This holds for *every* collision
  type, even when kinetic energy is lost.
- **Elastic collisions** — both momentum AND kinetic energy are conserved.
  Objects bounce apart without permanent deformation or heat loss (an
  idealization; billiard balls are close).
- **Inelastic collisions** — momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is
  NOT (some converts to heat, sound, deformation).
- **Perfectly inelastic collisions** — objects stick together and move
  with a common final velocity after collision; momentum is conserved,
  and this is the case with the largest kinetic energy loss.
- **Extending time reduces force** — since J = FΔt is fixed by the momentum
  change needed, increasing the time of impact (airbags, crumple zones,
  padded surfaces) decreases the average force experienced. This is the
  engineering principle behind HS-PS2-3.

## Common Misconceptions (most often missed)

1. **"Kinetic energy is always conserved in a collision."** False — only
   momentum is conserved in *every* collision. Kinetic energy is only
   conserved in elastic collisions.
2. **"A bigger/heavier object always ends up moving after a collision;
   momentum belongs to the bigger object."** Momentum is a property of
   each object's own mass and velocity — a small, fast object can have
   more momentum than a large, slow one. Compare, don't assume by size.
3. **Forgetting signs/direction.** Momentum is a vector. In a head-on
   collision, objects moving in opposite directions must have opposite
   signs before adding momenta — a very common arithmetic error that
   silently produces a wrong "total."
4. **"Momentum and force are the same thing."** Momentum is mv; force
   is what changes momentum over time (F = Δp/Δt). Confusing the two
   leads to unit and reasoning errors.
5. **"If two objects stick together, momentum is lost."** Momentum is
   still conserved in perfectly inelastic collisions — it's *kinetic
   energy* that's lost, not momentum.
6. **"A longer collision time means a bigger impulse."** Impulse (Δp) is
   fixed by the actual change in velocity/mass — extending Δt reduces the
   *force*, it does not change the impulse itself.

## Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

- Calculate momentum and impulse for single objects and systems.
- Apply conservation of momentum to solve 1D elastic, inelastic, and
  perfectly inelastic collision problems (HS-PS2-2).
- Distinguish which quantities (momentum, kinetic energy) are conserved
  in each collision type.
- Use the impulse-momentum theorem to explain why extending collision
  time reduces force.
- Design, test, and refine a simple device (e.g., egg-drop container,
  crumple-zone model) that minimizes force during a collision, and
  explain the design using impulse-momentum reasoning (HS-PS2-3).

## Practice Problems

1. A 0.15 kg baseball traveling at 40 m/s is hit and leaves the bat at
   50 m/s in the opposite direction. Find the impulse delivered by the bat.
2. A 1000 kg car traveling at 15 m/s collides with a stationary 1500 kg
   car, and they stick together. Find their common velocity after the
   collision. Is kinetic energy conserved? Show your work.
3. Two ice skaters (50 kg and 70 kg), initially at rest, push off from
   each other. If the 50 kg skater moves at 3.0 m/s, find the 70 kg
   skater's velocity.
4. Explain, using J = FΔt, why a car's crumple zone reduces injury even
   though the car's change in momentum during the crash is the same with
   or without it.
5. A 0.2 kg ball moving at 5 m/s bounces elastically off a wall and returns
   at 5 m/s. What is the impulse delivered by the wall?

## Real-World Applications

- **Car safety engineering** — crumple zones, airbags, and seatbelts all
  work by extending collision time to reduce force (HS-PS2-3 design
  focus).
- **Sports equipment** — padded helmets, boxing gloves, and landing mats
  reduce force by extending impact time.
- **Rocket propulsion** — momentum conservation explains how expelling
  mass (exhaust) propels a rocket forward with no external push.
- **Ballistic pendulum** — a classic lab/demo application used to measure
  a bullet's velocity using momentum conservation.
