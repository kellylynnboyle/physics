# Unit 1 — Kinematics

> The study of motion: describing *how* things move, without yet asking *why*.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- [ ] Distinguish between scalar and vector quantities (distance vs. displacement, speed vs. velocity).
- [ ] Interpret and construct position-time, velocity-time, and acceleration-time graphs.
- [ ] Calculate average and instantaneous velocity and acceleration from data or graphs.
- [ ] Apply the four kinematic equations to solve 1-D constant-acceleration problems.
- [ ] Analyze free-fall motion using g = 9.8 m/s².
- [ ] Solve multi-stage motion problems (e.g., an object that accelerates, then moves at constant velocity, then decelerates).

## Cornell Notes

### Cues / Questions | Notes

**What's the difference between distance and displacement?**
Distance = total path length traveled (scalar, always positive).
Displacement = straight-line change in position, from start to end (vector, can be negative). A runner who does one full lap has distance = track length, but displacement = 0.

**Speed vs. velocity?**
Speed is scalar (magnitude only); velocity is a vector (magnitude + direction). Average speed = total distance / total time. Average velocity = displacement / time.

**What is acceleration?**
Rate of change of velocity: a = Δv / Δt. Units: m/s². Acceleration is a vector — it can speed an object up, slow it down, or change its direction. Negative acceleration ("deceleration") only means slowing down if velocity is positive; if velocity is negative, negative acceleration means speeding up.

**The four kinematic equations (constant acceleration only):**
1. v = v₀ + at
2. Δx = v₀t + ½at²
3. v² = v₀² + 2aΔx
4. Δx = ½(v₀ + v)t

Each equation is missing exactly one of the five variables (v₀, v, a, t, Δx). Pick the equation missing the variable you don't have/need.

**How do I read motion graphs?**
- Position-time graph: **slope = velocity**. Curved line = accelerating.
- Velocity-time graph: **slope = acceleration**; **area under curve = displacement**.
- Acceleration-time graph: **area under curve = change in velocity**.

**Free fall**
All objects near Earth's surface accelerate downward at g ≈ 9.8 m/s² (ignoring air resistance), regardless of mass. At the top of a vertical throw, velocity = 0 but acceleration is still −9.8 m/s².

**Sign convention matters most**
Choose "up/right = positive" (or vice versa) at the start of a problem and stay consistent. Most kinematics errors come from mixing sign conventions mid-problem, not from the math itself.

### Summary
Kinematics describes motion using position, velocity, and acceleration as functions of time. For constant acceleration, four equations connect five variables — pick the equation that omits the unknown you don't need. Graphs encode the same information geometrically: slope and area are the two tools that unlock every graph question.

## Most Often Missed Concepts (feeds the interactive quiz)

1. **Confusing distance/displacement or speed/velocity** — especially in round-trip problems where displacement is 0 but distance isn't.
2. **Misreading v-t graphs** — students often think the *slope* of a v-t graph gives displacement (it gives acceleration); *area* gives displacement.
3. **Sign errors in free fall** — treating "up" as positive but then plugging g in as +9.8 instead of −9.8, or vice versa.
4. **Picking the wrong kinematic equation** — not identifying which variable is missing before choosing an equation.
5. **Constant velocity vs. constant acceleration** — assuming acceleration when velocity is actually constant (a horizontal line on a v-t graph means a = 0).
6. **Instantaneous vs. average velocity** — treating average velocity over an interval as if it equals the velocity at every point in that interval.
7. **Object at the peak of a throw has zero acceleration** — a very common misconception. Velocity is zero at the peak; acceleration (g) is never zero during free fall.

## Practice Problems

**Basic**
1. A cyclist accelerates from rest at 2.0 m/s² for 6.0 s. Find her final velocity and distance traveled.
2. A ball is dropped from a 45 m tall building. How long does it take to hit the ground? What is its velocity on impact?

**Intermediate**
3. A car traveling at 25 m/s brakes at −5.0 m/s². How far does it travel before stopping? Is this farther or shorter than its stopping distance at 35 m/s (same deceleration)?
4. Sketch the position-time, velocity-time, and acceleration-time graphs for a ball thrown straight up and caught at the same height.

**Challenge**
5. A train leaves a station accelerating at 1.2 m/s² for 20 s, then travels at constant velocity for 3.0 minutes, then decelerates uniformly to a stop over 15 s. Find the total distance traveled and the average velocity for the whole trip.
6. Two cars start 300 m apart and drive toward each other, one at constant 15 m/s, the other starting from rest and accelerating at 2.0 m/s². When and where do they meet?

## Real-World Applications

- **Airbag deployment timing** — engineers calculate deceleration during a crash to trigger airbags within milliseconds.
- **Sports analytics** — sprinters' velocity-time curves reveal reaction time, acceleration phase, and top speed.
- **Traffic safety** — stopping-distance calculations (reaction distance + braking distance) set speed limits and following-distance guidelines.
- **Elevators and roller coasters** — designed around comfortable acceleration limits for passengers.
- **Space launches** — rocket engineers track velocity and altitude vs. time to plan staging and orbital insertion.
