# Unit 5 — Work & Energy

> A different lens on motion: instead of forces over time (momentum), track forces over distance — and the quantity that's conserved is total mechanical energy.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- [ ] Calculate work done by a constant force: W = Fd cos θ, and recognize when a force does zero work.
- [ ] Define and calculate kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²) and gravitational potential energy (PE = mgh).
- [ ] Apply the Work-Energy Theorem (W_net = ΔKE).
- [ ] Apply Conservation of Mechanical Energy to systems without friction or other non-conservative forces.
- [ ] Account for energy "lost" to friction/air resistance using the extended work-energy equation.
- [ ] Calculate power (P = W/t = Fv) and apply it to real-world rated devices.
- [ ] Analyze energy transformations in systems such as pendulums, roller coasters, and springs (Hooke's Law, elastic PE = ½kx²).

## Assessment Criteria

| Level | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Mastery (A) | Correctly identifies which forces do work, sets up full energy-conservation equations including non-conservative losses, and solves multi-stage energy-transformation problems (e.g., roller coasters with friction). |

## Cornell Notes

### Cues / Questions | Notes

**What counts as "work" in physics?**
W = Fd cos θ, where θ is the angle between the force and the direction of displacement. Work is a scalar, measured in joules (J). If a force is perpendicular to motion (θ = 90°), it does **zero work** — e.g., the normal force on a walking person, or gravity on a satellite in circular orbit. If there's no displacement, no work is done, no matter how large the force (e.g., pushing on a wall that doesn't move).

**Kinetic energy**
KE = ½mv². Energy of motion; always positive (or zero); scalar; depends on speed squared, so doubling speed quadruples KE.

**Gravitational potential energy**
PE_grav = mgh, measured relative to a chosen reference height (h = 0 is your choice — only *changes* in height matter for calculations).

**Elastic (spring) potential energy**
PE_spring = ½kx², where k is the spring constant and x is displacement from equilibrium (Hooke's Law: F_spring = −kx).

**Work-Energy Theorem**
W_net = ΔKE. The net work done on an object by ALL forces equals its change in kinetic energy. This is really just Newton's 2nd law rewritten in terms of energy — useful when you don't know acceleration or time but do know force and distance.

**Conservation of Mechanical Energy**
In a system with only conservative forces (gravity, springs — no friction/air resistance): KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f. Mechanical energy (KE + PE) is constant; energy converts between forms but the total doesn't change.

**When friction is present**
Energy isn't destroyed, but *mechanical* energy isn't conserved either — some converts to heat. Extended equation: KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + W_friction (where W_friction = f·d is the energy "lost" to friction, actually transferred to thermal energy).

**Power**
P = W/t (average power) or P = Fv (instantaneous power for constant force in the direction of motion). Units: watts (1 W = 1 J/s). A more powerful engine does the same work faster, not necessarily "more" work.

### Summary
Work is the transfer of energy via a force acting through a distance; only the force component parallel to displacement counts. Energy comes in interchangeable forms (kinetic, gravitational PE, elastic PE, thermal), and total mechanical energy is conserved whenever only conservative forces act — this is often a faster problem-solving route than kinematics, especially when time isn't given. Power measures how quickly energy is transferred.

## Most Often Missed Concepts (feeds the interactive quiz)

1. **Forgetting the cos θ term** in W = Fd cos θ, especially for forces applied at an angle to motion (e.g., pulling a sled with a rope at an angle).
2. **Believing the normal force or a perpendicular force does work** — any force perpendicular to displacement does zero work (a very common trap with circular motion or walking).
3. **Confusing work and energy** — treating them as always numerically identical rather than understanding work as the *transfer mechanism* between energy forms.
4. **Ignoring friction and applying pure conservation of mechanical energy** when the problem explicitly involves a rough surface — students often use KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f even when friction is stated, forgetting the extra term.
5. **Squaring errors in KE** — forgetting the exponent on v (calculating KE as proportional to v instead of v², which massively under/overestimates energy at higher speeds).
6. **Mixing up mgh reference points** — inconsistent choice of "h = 0" between the initial and final states in the same problem.
7. **Power vs. work confusion** — thinking a machine that does the same amount of work in less time does "more work," rather than recognizing it has more power.

## Practice Problems

**Basic**
1. A 20 kg box is pushed 5.0 m across a floor by a 60 N horizontal force. How much work is done on the box?
2. A 1500 kg roller coaster car is at the top of a 40 m hill, starting from rest. Ignoring friction, find its speed at the bottom.

**Intermediate**
3. A rope pulls a 15 kg sled across snow at a 25° angle above horizontal with 50 N of tension, over a distance of 10 m. How much work does the rope do? If friction does −80 J of work over the same distance, find the sled's final kinetic energy (assuming it starts at rest).
4. A spring with k = 200 N/m is compressed 0.15 m and used to launch a 0.50 kg ball. Find the ball's launch speed (assume all spring PE converts to KE, no friction).

**Challenge**
5. A 60 kg skier starts from rest at the top of a 30 m tall slope. She reaches the bottom at 20 m/s. How much energy was lost to friction and air resistance during the descent?
6. A 800 kg roller coaster car starts at rest at the top of an 80 m hill. It reaches a second hill of height 50 m. If 15% of the initial potential energy is lost to friction by that point, find the car's speed at the top of the second hill.
7. An electric motor lifts a 200 kg elevator car 12 m in 8.0 s at constant velocity. Find the average power output of the motor (ignore losses).

## Real-World Applications

- **Roller coaster design** — engineers use energy conservation (plus friction losses) to ensure cars have enough KE to complete each hill and loop.
- **Hydroelectric dams** — convert gravitational PE of stored water into KE, then electrical energy, via turbines.
- **Vehicle fuel efficiency / regenerative braking** — hybrid and electric cars convert KE back into stored energy during braking instead of losing it all to friction as heat.
- **Home energy bills** — power (kW) × time (hours) = energy used (kWh), the exact quantity utilities bill for.
- **Athletic performance** — pole vaulting and gymnastics are direct KE ↔ PE (and elastic PE) conversion problems.
