# Kinematics

Kinematics is the study of how objects move — describing position, velocity, and acceleration without worrying about the forces that cause the motion. This unit builds the mathematical and graphical toolkit (motion graphs, the kinematic equations, and free fall) that underlies every later topic in mechanics, from forces to projectile motion to energy. Mastering kinematics also builds the core skill of translating between words, graphs, and equations — a skill every science and engineering field depends on.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Scalar vs. vector** — A scalar has only magnitude (like speed or distance), while a vector has both magnitude and direction (like velocity or displacement).
- **Displacement vs. distance** — Displacement is the straight-line change in position (can be negative or zero), while distance is the total path length traveled (always positive).
- **Average vs. instantaneous velocity** — Average velocity is total displacement divided by total time over an interval, while instantaneous velocity is the velocity at one specific moment (the slope of the position-time graph at that point).
- **Speed vs. velocity** — Speed is the scalar magnitude of how fast something moves, while velocity also specifies the direction of motion.
- **Acceleration** — Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes over time, and it can speed an object up, slow it down, or change its direction.
- **Position-time graphs** — The slope of a position-time graph tells you the velocity at that instant, and a curved line means the object is accelerating.
- **Velocity-time graphs (slope and area)** — The slope of a velocity-time graph equals acceleration, and the area under the curve equals displacement.
- **Sign conventions** — A negative velocity means motion in the negative direction, not necessarily "slowing down," and a negative acceleration means velocity is decreasing (becoming more negative), not necessarily "speeding up" or "slowing down."
- **The four kinematic equations** — For motion with constant acceleration, four equations relate displacement, initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, and time, letting you solve for any unknown if you know three others.
- **Free fall and g** — Near Earth's surface, every object in free fall accelerates downward at g = 9.8 m/s², regardless of its mass, as long as air resistance is ignored.
- **Acceleration at the top of a vertical throw** — An object thrown straight up has zero velocity at its highest point but still has a constant downward acceleration of 9.8 m/s² throughout the entire flight, including at the peak.
- **Relative motion** — An object's velocity depends on the reference frame it's measured from, so velocities of two objects can be combined or compared by choosing a consistent frame.

## Learning Objectives

Students will be able to...

1. Distinguish between scalar and vector quantities and correctly classify distance, displacement, speed, velocity, and acceleration.
2. Calculate distance and displacement from a description of motion or a position-time graph.
3. Calculate average velocity and average speed given position and time data, and distinguish them from instantaneous velocity.
4. Interpret position-time graphs to determine velocity (including identifying constant velocity, rest, and changing velocity from graph shape).
5. Interpret velocity-time graphs to determine acceleration (from slope) and displacement (from area under the curve).
6. Apply the four kinematic equations to solve one-dimensional constant-acceleration problems, including correctly handling zero initial velocity and choosing sign conventions.
7. Solve free-fall problems using g = 9.8 m/s², including objects dropped, thrown downward, and thrown upward, correctly identifying velocity and acceleration at the peak of the motion.
8. Analyze and construct matching position-time and velocity-time graphs for a given motion scenario, and critique common misconceptions (e.g., negative velocity vs. negative acceleration).

## Assessment Criteria

| Level | Descriptor |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Can define basic terms (position, velocity, acceleration) but confuses scalar/vector quantities and cannot read a motion graph accurately. Struggles to set up a kinematic equation even when given variables. |
| **Developing** | Correctly distinguishes distance vs. displacement and speed vs. velocity in simple cases. Can read slope OR area from a graph but not both reliably. Solves kinematic equations only when initial velocity is nonzero and the setup is given. |
| **Proficient** | Reliably reads both slope and area from velocity-time graphs and slope from position-time graphs. Selects and applies the correct kinematic equation independently, including special cases (v₀ = 0, free fall). Uses correct sign conventions and explains the difference between negative velocity and negative acceleration. |
| **Mastery** | Fluently moves between verbal descriptions, graphs, and equations for any 1D motion scenario. Explains why acceleration is nonzero at the top of a vertical throw. Solves multi-step problems combining several kinematic concepts and justifies reasoning with correct units and sign conventions throughout. |

**Quantitative bar for Proficient:** 80%+ on the topic quiz, and the ability to solve 1D kinematics problems (including free fall and graph-reading problems) correctly in 2 attempts or fewer.

## Practice Problems

1. A student walks 4 m east, then 3 m west. What is the student's total distance traveled and total displacement?
2. A car travels at a constant 20 m/s for 5 seconds. How far does it travel?
3. A position-time graph shows a straight line rising from (0 s, 0 m) to (10 s, 50 m). What is the object's velocity?
4. A velocity-time graph shows velocity constant at 6 m/s from t = 0 s to t = 4 s, then dropping in a straight line to 0 m/s at t = 6 s. What is the displacement over the full 6 seconds, and what is the acceleration during the last 2 seconds?
5. A cyclist accelerates from rest at 2 m/s² for 6 seconds. What is her final velocity and how far did she travel?
6. A ball is dropped from rest from a height and falls for 3 seconds before hitting the ground. What is its velocity just before impact, and how far did it fall?
7. A ball is thrown straight upward at 15 m/s. What is its velocity and acceleration at the very top of its flight? How long does it take to reach the top?
8. A car moving at 25 m/s brakes with a constant deceleration of 5 m/s². How far does it travel before stopping, and how long does it take to stop?

### Answers

1. Distance = 7 m; Displacement = 1 m east
2. 100 m
3. 5 m/s
4. Displacement = 30 m; Acceleration = -3 m/s²
5. Final velocity = 12 m/s; Distance = 36 m
6. Velocity ≈ 29.4 m/s (downward); Distance ≈ 44.1 m
7. Velocity = 0 m/s; Acceleration = 9.8 m/s² downward (unchanged); Time to top ≈ 1.53 s
8. Distance = 62.5 m; Time = 5 s

## Real-World Applications

- **Car braking distance** — Traffic engineers and driver's ed courses use kinematic equations to calculate stopping distances at different speeds, which is why speed limits and following-distance rules exist.
- **Sprinters' acceleration curves** — Coaches analyze 100 m dash velocity-time graphs to see how quickly an athlete reaches top speed versus how well they maintain it.
- **Elevators** — Elevator motion is designed with smooth acceleration and deceleration phases so passengers don't feel a jarring jolt, which engineers plan using velocity-time profiles.
- **Sports (basketball, baseball, diving)** — The height and hang time of a jump shot, pop fly, or dive are all governed by the same free-fall equations used for a dropped ball.
- **Airbag and crumple zone design** — Automotive safety engineers use deceleration (negative acceleration) calculations to design crumple zones that reduce the acceleration experienced by passengers during a collision.
