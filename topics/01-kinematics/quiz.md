# Quiz — Kinematics: Most Commonly Missed Questions

Take this quiz *before* checking any answers. Click each "Answer & explanation" to reveal — these ten questions target the specific mistakes students make most often on kinematics tests.

---

**1.** A ball is thrown straight up. At the very top of its path, what is true about its velocity and acceleration?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Velocity = 0, but acceleration = −9.8 m/s² (still full gravity, unchanged). This is the #1 missed kinematics concept: students assume acceleration must be zero whenever velocity is zero. Acceleration only depends on the net force acting (gravity, here), not on the current velocity.
</details>

---

**2.** A car has a velocity of −20 m/s and an acceleration of −3 m/s². Is the car speeding up or slowing down?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Speeding up. Velocity and acceleration have the same sign (both negative), so the car is accelerating in the same direction it's already moving — it speeds up in the negative direction. "Negative acceleration" ≠ "slowing down"; only when velocity and acceleration have *opposite* signs is the object decelerating.
</details>

---

**3.** An object moves 5 m to the right, then 3 m to the left. What is its distance traveled and its displacement?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Distance = 8 m (5 + 3). Displacement = 2 m to the right (5 − 3). Students frequently report both as the same number because they don't separate the scalar path-length question from the vector net-change question.
</details>

---

**4.** On a velocity-time graph, how do you find the total displacement over an interval where velocity changes sign (crosses zero)?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Find the area above the axis and the area below the axis *separately*, then add them as signed values (area below the axis counts as negative displacement). A common error is adding the two areas as if both were positive, which gives total distance, not displacement.
</details>

---

**5.** Which kinematic equation should you use if you know v₀, a, and t, and need to find Δx — but you don't know (and don't need) the final velocity v?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Δx = v₀t + ½at². The most common error here is defaulting to Δx = ½(v₀+v)t out of habit, which requires v — a variable you weren't given. Always list your knowns/unknowns first and pick the equation that omits the variable you don't have.
</details>

---

**6.** A ball is dropped (not thrown) from a height. What is its initial velocity?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

v₀ = 0. "Dropped" means released from rest. Students sometimes confuse this with "thrown downward," which would have a nonzero initial velocity. Read problems carefully for this distinction.
</details>

---

**7.** Two balls are dropped from the same height at the same time — one has twice the mass of the other. Ignoring air resistance, which hits the ground first?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

They hit at the same time. In free fall (no air resistance), acceleration due to gravity is independent of mass — a = −g for everything. This is one of the most persistent physics misconceptions (dating back to Aristotle); Galileo's insight was that it's false.
</details>

---

**8.** A position-time graph is a horizontal line. What does that tell you about the object's velocity and acceleration?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Velocity = 0 (slope of x-t graph is zero) and acceleration = 0 as well. The object is simply at rest. A common mix-up is confusing a flat x-t graph (at rest) with a flat v-t graph (constant nonzero velocity) — they look the same but mean very different things depending on which graph you're reading.
</details>

---

**9.** A rock is thrown straight up with initial speed v₀ from ground level. In terms of v₀ and g, what is the total time until it lands back at ground level?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

t = 2v₀/g. By symmetry, time up = time down = v₀/g each. A frequent error is solving only for the time to reach the top (v₀/g) and forgetting to double it for the full round trip, or plugging into Δx = v₀t + ½at² with Δx = 0 and mishandling the signs.
</details>

---

**10.** Why can't you use the standard kinematic equations (v = v₀+at, etc.) for an object whose acceleration is changing over time (e.g., a car with a jerky, non-constant acceleration)?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

All four kinematic equations are derived assuming acceleration is constant. If acceleration varies with time, these equations give wrong answers — you'd need calculus (integrating a(t)) or a v-t graph's changing slope instead. Always check "is acceleration constant here?" before reaching for these formulas.
</details>
