# Unit 1: Kinematics

Kinematics is the description of motion — position, velocity, and
acceleration — without worrying about what causes it (that's Newton's
Laws, Unit 3). Everything in Units 2–5 depends on getting kinematics solid
first.

## Key Concepts

- **Position (x)** — location relative to a chosen origin and direction; a
  vector.
- **Displacement (Δx)** — change in position; depends only on start and end
  points, not the path taken. Different from **distance**, which is the
  total path length traveled.
- **Velocity (v)** — rate of change of displacement; a vector (has
  direction). *Average velocity* = Δx/Δt. *Instantaneous velocity* = slope
  of the tangent line on a position-time graph.
- **Speed** — magnitude of velocity only; always positive/zero.
- **Acceleration (a)** — rate of change of velocity; a = Δv/Δt. Acceleration
  is not the same as speed — an object can accelerate while slowing down,
  speeding up, or only changing direction.
- **Sign conventions** — velocity and acceleration are vectors. A negative
  sign means "opposite the chosen positive direction," not "slowing down."
- **The kinematic equations** (constant acceleration only):
  - v = v₀ + at
  - Δx = v₀t + ½at²
  - v² = v₀² + 2aΔx
  - Δx = ½(v₀ + v)t
- **Graphical relationships**:
  - Position-time graph: slope = velocity
  - Velocity-time graph: slope = acceleration; area under curve =
    displacement
  - Acceleration-time graph: area under curve = change in velocity
- **Free fall** — near Earth's surface, a = −9.8 m/s² (downward), the same
  for all objects regardless of mass, if air resistance is ignored.

## Common Misconceptions (most often missed)

1. **"Negative acceleration always means slowing down."** False — an object
   moving in the negative direction that speeds up also has negative
   acceleration. Slowing down happens when velocity and acceleration have
   *opposite* signs, regardless of which sign is positive.
2. **"At the top of a thrown object's path, acceleration is zero."** False
   — acceleration due to gravity is −9.8 m/s² the entire time it's in the
   air, including at the peak, where only velocity (not acceleration) is
   momentarily zero.
3. **Confusing a d-t graph with a v-t graph.** A curved, upward-bending
   line on a position-time graph means increasing velocity (speeding up),
   *not* increasing acceleration by itself — students often misread graph
   type.
4. **"Distance and displacement are always the same."** Only true for
   one-directional straight-line motion with no backtracking.
5. **Heavier objects fall faster.** In the absence of air resistance, all
   objects in free fall accelerate identically.

## Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

- Distinguish distance from displacement and speed from velocity, and
  correctly apply sign conventions to 1D motion.
- Read and interpret position-time, velocity-time, and acceleration-time
  graphs, including converting between them (slope and area).
- Select and apply the correct kinematic equation to solve for an unknown
  variable in constant-acceleration problems.
- Solve free-fall problems, including objects thrown upward.
- Explain acceleration at the top of a projectile's vertical path.

## Practice Problems

1. A car accelerates from rest at 3.0 m/s² for 6.0 s. Find its final
   velocity and the distance traveled.
2. A ball is thrown straight up at 20 m/s. How long until it returns to
   the thrower's hand? What is its velocity at the top?
3. Sketch the v-t graph for an object that speeds up, moves at constant
   velocity, then slows to a stop, all in the positive direction. Sketch
   the corresponding a-t graph.
4. A runner covers 400 m in one direction, then jogs back 150 m. What is
   the total distance and the displacement?
5. A train decelerates from 30 m/s to rest over 200 m. Find its
   acceleration (should be negative).

## Real-World Applications

- **Vehicle stopping distance** — reaction time (constant velocity phase)
  plus braking (constant deceleration phase); used in driver's ed and
  traffic safety design.
- **Sports analytics** — sprinter velocity/acceleration curves from
  100 m dash data.
- **Airbag and seatbelt design** — relies on understanding rapid
  deceleration.
- **Elevators and roller coasters** — engineers use kinematic equations to
  design comfortable, safe acceleration profiles.
