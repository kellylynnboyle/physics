# Unit 5: Work & Energy

**Standards:** HS-PS3-1 (create a computational model to calculate energy changes when the other quantities are known); HS-PS3-2 (develop and use models to illustrate that energy at the macroscopic scale can be accounted for as motion, position, or fields); HS-PS3-3 (design a device that converts one form of energy into another); HS-PS3-4 (investigate thermal energy transfer, introduced here and extended in Semester 2).
**Science & Engineering Practices:** Developing and using models; using computational thinking; designing solutions.
**Suggested pacing:** 3 weeks

## Learning objectives
Students will be able to…
- [ ] **E1.** Calculate work done by a constant force, including at an angle (W = Fd cosθ), and identify when work is zero.
- [ ] **E2.** Calculate kinetic energy (½mv²), gravitational potential energy (mgh), and elastic potential energy (½kx²).
- [ ] **E3.** Apply the work-energy theorem (W_net = ΔKE).
- [ ] **E4.** Apply conservation of mechanical energy, and account for energy converted to thermal energy by friction.
- [ ] **E5.** Calculate power (P = W/t) and efficiency, and compare energy-conversion devices.
- [ ] **E6.** Create and use a computational model (spreadsheet or simple code) or an energy bar chart to track energy transfers for a system.
- [ ] **E7.** Design and test a device that converts energy from one form to another and evaluate its efficiency.

## Key concepts and equations
| Concept | Equation / idea |
|---|---|
| Work | W = Fd cosθ (joules). Only the force component along displacement does work. |
| Kinetic energy | KE = ½mv² |
| Gravitational PE | PE = mgh (height measured from a chosen zero level) |
| Elastic PE | PE = ½kx² |
| Work-energy theorem | W_net = ΔKE |
| Conservation | KE₁ + PE₁ + W_nc = KE₂ + PE₂ (W_nc = work by non-conservative forces such as friction, negative) |
| Power | P = W/t = Fv (watts) |
| Efficiency | η = useful energy out / energy in |

## Most-missed ideas
1. Physics work needs displacement along the force; carrying a box horizontally does zero work on it.
2. KE depends on v² (double speed → quadruple KE).
3. Mass cancels in v = √(2gh) free-fall problems.
4. Energy isn't "used up"; it is transformed (friction → thermal energy).
5. Use the height **difference** in energy conservation, not total height.
6. Confusing work (J) with power (W).

## Lab ideas
- **Roller-coaster track lab:** Predict speed at a point with energy conservation; measure with photogates; compute % energy lost.
- **Spring launcher:** Measure k from a force-stretch graph, predict launch speed from ½kx² = ½mv², and test it.
- **Stair-climb power:** Compute power from mass, height, and time.
- **Energy-converter design (HS-PS3-3):** Build a solar-car, hand-crank generator, or Rube-Goldberg machine and calculate its efficiency.

## Real-world applications
- Roller coasters and skate parks: PE ↔ KE.
- Hybrid and electric cars: regenerative braking.
- Hydroelectric dams and wind turbines: converting PE/KE to electricity.
- Fitness: power output while cycling or climbing.
- Safe following distances: braking work and KE.

## Practice problems
1. A 20 kg crate is pushed 8 m by a 50 N horizontal force against 30 N of friction. Find the net work and the final speed (from rest).
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>W_net = (50 − 30)(8) = 160 J; v = √(2·160/20) = 4 m/s.</details>
2. A 0.2 kg ball is thrown straight up at 15 m/s. Find the maximum height and its PE at the top.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>h = 15²/(2·9.8) ≈ 11.5 m; PE = mgh ≈ 22.5 J (equals the initial KE).</details>
3. A spring (k = 200 N/m) is compressed 0.10 m and launches a 0.05 kg ball. Find the spring's energy and the ball's speed.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>PE = ½(200)(0.01) = 1.0 J; v = √(2·1.0/0.05) ≈ 6.3 m/s.</details>
4. A 70 kg climber climbs stairs 3 m high in 5 s. Find the power.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>P = 70(9.8)(3)/5 ≈ 412 W.</details>
5. A 1 m pendulum bob is raised 0.20 m above the lowest point and released. Find its speed at the bottom.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>v = √(2·9.8·0.20) ≈ 2.0 m/s.</details>
6. A 1000 kg car going 20 m/s brakes to a stop. How much thermal energy is produced?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>ΔKE = ½(1000)(20²) = 200,000 J = 200 kJ of thermal energy (plus a little sound).</details>

## Assessment criteria
| Level | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Beginning (1)** | Names energy forms; applies W = Fd without checking direction; no energy accounting. |
| **Developing (2)** | Calculates KE, PE, and work separately; sets up conservation with prompting; errors with angles or reference heights. |
| **Proficient (3)** | Uses energy bar charts and conservation with friction correctly; solves problems with units; interprets energy graphs. Quiz score ≥ 80%. |
| **Advanced (4)** | Builds a computational model, quantifies efficiency and energy loss for a real device, and proposes data-based design improvements. |
