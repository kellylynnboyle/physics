# Work and Energy

Work and energy give us a powerful alternative to Newton's laws for analyzing motion — instead of tracking forces moment by moment, we track how energy is transferred and transformed. This topic builds from the definition of work, through kinetic and potential energy, to the conservation of mechanical energy, and ends with power as the rate of doing work. These tools let you solve problems (like a skier's speed at the bottom of a hill) that would be very difficult using forces and kinematics alone.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Work (W = Fd cos θ)** — Work is done on an object when a force causes a displacement, and only the component of force parallel to the displacement counts.
- **Zero work conditions** — Work is zero when there is no displacement, or when the force is perpendicular to the displacement (e.g., carrying a box at constant height does zero work against gravity).
- **Sign of work** — Work is positive when the force component and displacement point the same way, and negative when they oppose (e.g., friction, or gravity while an object rises).
- **Kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²)** — The energy of motion, which depends on mass linearly but on speed squared, so doubling speed quadruples KE.
- **Gravitational potential energy (PE = mgh)** — Stored energy due to an object's height above a chosen reference level in a gravitational field.
- **Elastic potential energy (PE = ½kx²)** — Stored energy in a stretched or compressed spring, where k is the spring constant and x is the displacement from equilibrium.
- **Work-energy theorem (W_net = ΔKE)** — The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, linking forces directly to changes in speed.
- **Conservation of mechanical energy** — In the absence of friction or other non-conservative forces, the sum of KE and PE stays constant as an object moves.
- **Non-conservative work (friction/heat)** — Friction and air resistance remove mechanical energy from a system, converting it into thermal energy, so KE + PE decreases over time.
- **Power (P = W/t)** — Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred, not the total amount of energy itself.
- **Units** — Energy and work are measured in joules (J = N·m = kg·m²/s²); power is measured in watts (W = J/s).
- **Energy bar charts / pie charts** — A useful way to visualize how total mechanical energy is distributed between KE, PE, and losses to friction at different points in a system.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, students will be able to:

1. Calculate the work done by a constant force using W = Fd cos θ, including cases with an angled force.
2. Identify situations where work done is zero, positive, or negative based on the force-displacement relationship.
3. Calculate kinetic energy given an object's mass and velocity, and explain why KE depends on v².
4. Calculate gravitational potential energy and elastic potential energy for a given system.
5. Apply the work-energy theorem to find an object's final speed or the net force acting on it.
6. Apply conservation of mechanical energy to solve multi-stage problems (e.g., roller coasters, pendulums, ramps) when friction is negligible.
7. Account for energy lost to friction or air resistance when mechanical energy is not conserved, using energy bar/pie charts to track the losses.
8. Calculate power output or input in mechanical and everyday contexts, and distinguish power from total work/energy.

## Assessment Criteria

| Level | Descriptor |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Can state the formulas for work, KE, PE, and power but struggles to apply them; confuses work with force or energy with power; cannot identify when work is zero. |
| **Developing** | Correctly calculates W, KE, PE, and P in single-step problems; sometimes forgets cos θ in the work formula or mixes up mgh vs. ½kx²; needs guidance on multi-step conservation problems. |
| **Proficient** | Reliably applies the work-energy theorem and conservation of mechanical energy to two-stage problems (e.g., height to speed); correctly identifies when to use gravitational vs. elastic PE; can account qualitatively for friction losses. |
| **Mastery** | Solves multi-stage, multi-force problems fluently, including systems with friction and energy converted to heat; explains reasoning with correct units and sign conventions throughout; can move flexibly between energy bar charts, equations, and real-world predictions. |

**Quantitative proficiency bar:** 80%+ on the topic quiz, with demonstrated ability to apply conservation of energy to solve multi-stage problems including friction (i.e., correctly setting up KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + E_lost).

## Practice Problems

1. A student pushes a box with a force of 50 N over a distance of 4 m in the direction of the push. How much work is done on the box?
2. A 60 kg runner is moving at 5 m/s. What is the runner's kinetic energy?
3. A 2 kg book sits on a shelf 1.5 m above the floor. What is its gravitational potential energy relative to the floor?
4. A spring with spring constant k = 200 N/m is compressed 0.10 m. How much elastic potential energy is stored?
5. A person pulls a 20 kg sled with a rope at a 30° angle above the horizontal, applying 80 N of force, over a distance of 10 m. How much work does the person do on the sled?
6. A frictionless roller coaster car (mass 500 kg) starts from rest at the top of a 20 m hill. What is its speed at the bottom of the hill?
7. A 15 kg crate slides 6 m across a floor where the force of friction is 25 N, while an applied force of 60 N (parallel to motion) pushes it forward. Using the work-energy theorem, find the crate's final kinetic energy if it started at rest.
8. A weightlifter raises a 100 kg barbell 2 m in 1.5 s. What is the average power output during the lift?

### Answers

1. 200 J
2. 750 J
3. 29.4 J
4. 1.0 J
5. 692.8 J
6. ≈19.8 m/s
7. 210 J
8. ≈1307 W

## Real-World Applications

- **Roller coasters** — Cars convert gravitational PE at the top of hills into KE on the way down, illustrating conservation of mechanical energy (with some loss to friction and air resistance).
- **Hydroelectric dams** — Water stored at height has gravitational PE that is converted to KE as it falls, then to electrical energy as it turns turbines.
- **Hybrid/electric car regenerative braking** — Kinetic energy that would normally be lost as heat during braking is instead converted back into stored electrical energy in the battery.
- **Pendulum clocks** — The pendulum continuously exchanges gravitational PE and KE as it swings, with small energy losses to air resistance and friction at the pivot.
- **Weightlifting and power output** — Athletes performing the same lift (same work) in less time produce more power, illustrating the distinction between work/energy and power.
