# Unit 5: Work & Energy

This unit closes out mechanics by giving students a second, often easier,
way to solve motion problems: energy methods instead of forces. It
provides direct evidence for NGSS **HS-PS3-1**, **HS-PS3-2**, and
**HS-PS3-3**.

## Key Concepts

- **Work (W)** — W = Fd·cosθ, the energy transferred to/from an object by
  a force acting over a displacement. Measured in joules (J). Only the
  force component *parallel* to displacement does work; a force
  perpendicular to motion does zero work.
- **Kinetic energy (KE)** — KE = ½mv², the energy of motion.
- **Gravitational potential energy (PE_grav)** — PE = mgh, energy stored
  due to position in a gravitational field, relative to a chosen
  reference height.
- **Elastic potential energy (PE_spring)** — PE = ½kx², energy stored in a
  stretched/compressed spring.
- **Work-energy theorem** — the net work done on an object equals its
  change in kinetic energy: W_net = ΔKE.
- **Conservation of mechanical energy** — in the absence of
  non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance), total mechanical
  energy (KE + PE) is constant: KE₁ + PE₁ = KE₂ + PE₂.
- **When energy "seems lost"** — it isn't destroyed; it converts to
  another form, usually heat/thermal energy via friction or air
  resistance (this is HS-PS3-2: energy accounted for at the
  particle/thermal level).
- **Power (P)** — P = W/t = Fv, the *rate* of doing work or transferring
  energy, measured in watts (J/s). Two machines can do the same work but
  have very different power if the time differs.
- **Energy transformation devices** — designing/evaluating a device that
  converts one energy form into another (e.g., a roller coaster, a
  pendulum, a generator) is the core task of HS-PS3-3.

## Common Misconceptions (most often missed)

1. **"Holding a heavy object still, or carrying it at constant height and
   velocity, does work on it."** False — work requires displacement in
   the direction of the force. Holding something static, or carrying it
   horizontally while gravity acts vertically, does zero work on the
   object by that force.
2. **"Energy is lost/destroyed by friction."** False — energy is
   conserved overall; friction converts mechanical energy into thermal
   energy (heat), it doesn't destroy it. "Lost" should mean "no longer
   mechanical," not "gone."
3. **"More work always means more power, and vice versa."** False — power
   depends on *time*. The same work done faster requires more power; the
   same power sustained longer produces more work. These are often
   conflated on tests.
4. **"Potential energy is a fixed, absolute quantity."** False — PE_grav
   depends on the chosen reference height (h = 0 point); only *changes* in
   PE are physically meaningful, not the absolute number.
5. **"An object with more speed always has more kinetic energy than a
   slower one."** Usually true only when comparing equal masses — KE
   depends on mass too (½mv²), and doubling velocity quadruples KE (v is
   squared), a very commonly missed proportional-reasoning point.
6. **Mixing up work-energy theorem with conservation of mechanical
   energy.** Work-energy theorem (W_net = ΔKE) applies even with friction
   present; conservation of mechanical energy (KE+PE constant) only
   applies when there is no friction/air resistance.

## Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

- Calculate work done by a force, including cases where the force is at
  an angle to displacement or does zero work.
- Calculate kinetic energy, gravitational PE, and elastic PE for given
  conditions.
- Apply the work-energy theorem to find velocity, force, or distance in
  problems involving friction.
- Apply conservation of mechanical energy to solve problems (pendulums,
  roller coasters, free fall) where friction is negligible.
- Explain, using particle-level reasoning, where mechanical energy goes
  when it appears to disappear (HS-PS3-2).
- Design, build, and refine a device that converts one energy form into
  another within given constraints (HS-PS3-3), and justify the design
  using energy accounting (HS-PS3-1).
- Calculate power and distinguish it from work/energy.

## Assessment Criteria

| Level | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Mastery | Solves multi-step energy-conservation problems including friction, correctly applies work-energy theorem vs. conservation of mechanical energy, and can account for energy transformations at a conceptual level. |
| Proficient | Solves basic KE/PE/work problems correctly; needs support distinguishing when mechanical energy is or isn't conserved. |
| Developing | Computes individual quantities (W, KE, PE) but cannot connect them in multi-step problems. |

## Practice Problems

1. A 20 kg box is pushed 5.0 m across a floor by a 60 N horizontal force.
   How much work is done on the box?
2. A 1500 kg roller coaster car starts at rest at the top of a 40 m hill
   (frictionless track). Find its speed at the bottom.
3. The same roller coaster car (from #2) actually arrives at the bottom
   with a speed of 25 m/s instead of the frictionless prediction. How much
   energy was converted to heat by friction?
4. A 0.50 kg ball is dropped from 10 m. Use the work-energy theorem to
   find its speed just before hitting the ground (ignore air resistance),
   and check it against conservation of mechanical energy.
5. Two motors each lift a 100 kg load 5.0 m. Motor A takes 10 s; Motor B
   takes 4 s. Which motor is more powerful, and by how much? Do they do
   the same amount of work?

## Real-World Applications

- **Roller coasters** — nearly pure conservation-of-mechanical-energy
  systems (plus friction/air resistance losses engineers must account
  for).
- **Hydroelectric dams** — gravitational PE of stored water converts to
  KE, then to electrical energy (HS-PS3-3 device design).
- **Regenerative braking (electric/hybrid vehicles)** — converts a
  vehicle's KE back into stored electrical energy instead of losing it
  all to heat via friction brakes.
- **Home energy efficiency** — power ratings (watts) on appliances tell
  you the *rate* of energy use, directly connecting to the power concept.
