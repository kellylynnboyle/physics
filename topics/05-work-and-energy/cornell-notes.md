# Cornell Notes: Work and Energy

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is the formula for work? | W = Fd cos θ, where F is force (N), d is displacement (m), and θ is the angle between the force and displacement vectors. Work is measured in joules (J). |
| When is work equal to zero? | Work is zero when there is no displacement (d = 0) OR when the force is perpendicular to the displacement (θ = 90°, cos 90° = 0). Example: carrying a box at constant height — the normal/lifting force is vertical, motion is horizontal. |
| What does negative work mean? | Work is negative when the force has a component opposite to the displacement (θ between 90° and 180°). Example: friction acting opposite to motion, or gravity while an object moves upward. |
| What is kinetic energy? | KE = ½mv², where m is mass (kg) and v is speed (m/s). KE depends on velocity **squared** — doubling speed quadruples KE, not doubles it. |
| What is gravitational potential energy? | PE_grav = mgh, where m is mass, g is 9.8 m/s², and h is height above a chosen reference point. PE depends on the reference level chosen. |
| What is elastic potential energy? | PE_elastic = ½kx², where k is the spring constant (N/m) and x is displacement from the spring's equilibrium (natural) position. Use this only for springs/elastic materials, not for height above ground. |
| What is the work-energy theorem? | W_net = ΔKE = KE_f − KE_i. The **net** work done by all forces on an object equals its change in kinetic energy. |
| What is conservation of mechanical energy? | If no friction or other non-conservative forces act, KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f. Total mechanical energy (KE + PE) stays constant. |
| What happens to energy when friction is present? | KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + E_lost, where E_lost is energy converted to heat/sound by friction or air resistance. Mechanical energy is NOT conserved, but total energy still is. |
| What is power? | P = W/t = energy transferred ÷ time taken. Power is a **rate**, measured in watts (W = J/s), and is different from the total amount of work or energy. |
| How do I know which PE formula to use? | Use PE = mgh for objects raised/lowered in a gravitational field (height above a surface). Use PE = ½kx² for compressed/stretched springs or elastic objects (displacement from natural length). |
| What are the units of work, energy, and power? | Work and all forms of energy: joules (J) = N·m = kg·m²/s². Power: watts (W) = J/s. Never mix these up on a quiz — a joule is an amount, a watt is a rate. |
| Does an object at the very top of its path (max height) have kinetic energy? | Only if it still has horizontal (or any) velocity component at that instant. In pure vertical projectile motion, velocity = 0 at the peak, so KE = 0 momentarily. In projectile motion with horizontal velocity, KE is NOT zero at the peak because horizontal speed remains. |
| How do I set up a conservation of energy problem? | 1) Choose a reference height (h = 0). 2) Identify KE and PE at the initial and final points. 3) Write KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f (+ E_lost if friction). 4) Solve for the unknown (usually v or h). |

## Summary

Work is done on an object only when a force causes displacement in the direction of that force, so carrying something at constant height or pushing against a perpendicular force does zero work. Kinetic energy depends on the square of velocity, which is why speed changes matter so much more than mass changes when comparing KE. I need to remember that gravitational PE (mgh) and elastic PE (½kx²) are used in different situations — one for height, one for springs — and not mix them up. The work-energy theorem connects net work directly to the change in KE, while conservation of mechanical energy lets me solve for speeds and heights without ever calculating individual forces, as long as friction is absent (or I explicitly subtract energy lost to friction if it isn't). Power is a rate, not an amount, so two people doing the same amount of work in different times have different power outputs even though they did the same work. Overall, energy methods are often faster than force methods for multi-stage problems like roller coasters, pendulums, and ramps.
