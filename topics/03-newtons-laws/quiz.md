# Quiz — Newton's Laws: Most Commonly Missed Questions

Take this quiz *before* checking any answers.

---

**1.** A book sits at rest on a table. Is there a force acting on it?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Yes — two forces: gravity (down) and the normal force from the table (up). They are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so the *net* force is zero, which is why the book stays at rest (Newton's 1st Law). "At rest" does not mean "no forces," it means "balanced forces."
</details>

---

**2.** A book on a table experiences gravity (mg, down) and a normal force (N, up). Is this a Newton's-third-law pair?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

No. This is the single most common Newton's-laws mix-up. A third-law pair must act on *two different objects* and involve the *same type* of interaction. Gravity and the normal force act on the *same* object (the book) and are different types of force — this is a balanced-force pair, not a third-law pair. The actual third-law pair to gravity-on-book is gravity-on-Earth-from-book; the pair to normal-force-on-book-from-table is normal-force-on-table-from-book.
</details>

---

**3.** A truck and a small car collide head-on. Which one experiences the greater force?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

They experience exactly the same magnitude of force (Newton's 3rd Law — forces always come in equal-and-opposite pairs regardless of mass). What differs is the acceleration: the smaller car has less mass, so for the same force it experiences a much larger acceleration (ΣF = ma), which is why it's damaged more and its occupants feel a bigger effect — not because the force on it was bigger.
</details>

---

**4.** You double the net force on an object and also double its mass. What happens to its acceleration?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Acceleration stays the same. a = F/m — doubling both F and m leaves the ratio unchanged. Students who don't think proportionally about ΣF = ma often guess the acceleration doubles (focusing only on force) or that it's unclear.
</details>

---

**5.** A box sits on a 20° frictionless incline. What is the normal force compared to the box's weight (mg)?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

N = mg·cosθ, which is *less* than mg (since cos of an angle between 0° and 90° is less than 1). A common error is setting N = mg regardless of incline angle, which is only true on a flat, horizontal surface with no other vertical forces.
</details>

---

**6.** A rocket in deep space (no gravity, no air) fires its engines to expel gas backward. Why does the rocket move forward, if there's nothing for it to "push against"?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Newton's 3rd Law doesn't require pushing against an external object — the rocket pushes the exhaust gas backward, and the gas pushes the rocket forward, equally and oppositely. This is a common misconception ("rockets can't work in a vacuum because there's nothing to push against") — rockets actually work *better* in a vacuum, since there's no atmospheric drag.
</details>

---

**7.** Two blocks (2 kg and 4 kg) are connected by a string and pulled together across a frictionless floor by a 12 N force applied to the 4 kg block. What is the tension in the string connecting them?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

First find system acceleration: a = F/m_total = 12 N / 6 kg = 2 m/s². Then isolate the 2 kg block (only the tension acts on it horizontally): T = m·a = 2 kg × 2 m/s² = 4 N. A common mistake is using the full 12 N as the tension, or using the wrong block's mass when isolating for tension — always isolate the block *not* directly pulled by the external force, since only tension acts on it horizontally.
</details>

---

**8.** An object moves at a constant 10 m/s in a straight line. What is the net force acting on it?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Zero. Constant velocity (in both speed and direction) means zero acceleration, so by ΣF = ma, net force must be zero — even though the object is clearly moving. Students frequently assume that motion itself requires a net force; it's *changes* in motion that require a net force (1st Law).
</details>
