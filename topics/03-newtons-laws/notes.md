# Unit 3: Newton's Laws of Motion

Newton's Laws explain *why* objects move the way Unit 1 and 2 described.
This unit provides direct evidence for NGSS **HS-PS2-1**: analyzing data
to support the claim that F_net = ma describes the relationship among net
force, mass, and acceleration.

## Key Concepts

- **Force** — a push or pull; a vector, measured in newtons (N). Forces
  come in identifiable types: gravity/weight, normal force, friction,
  tension, applied force, air resistance.
- **Newton's First Law (inertia)** — an object at rest stays at rest, and
  an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted
  on by a net (unbalanced) external force. Inertia is the tendency to
  resist a change in motion; it depends only on mass.
- **Newton's Second Law** — F_net = ma. Net force is the vector sum of all
  forces acting on an object. Acceleration is directly proportional to
  net force and inversely proportional to mass.
- **Free-body diagrams (FBDs)** — a diagram showing all forces acting ON
  one object (never forces the object exerts on other things), each drawn
  as an arrow from the object's center, roughly proportional to
  magnitude.
- **Newton's Third Law** — for every action force, there is a reaction
  force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, acting on the
  *other* object. Action-reaction pairs never act on the same object and
  never cancel each other out.
- **Weight vs. mass** — mass (kg) is the amount of matter, constant
  everywhere. Weight (N) = mg, the force of gravity on that mass, and
  changes with local gravitational field strength.
- **Friction** — static friction (f_s ≤ μ_sN) resists the start of motion;
  kinetic friction (f_k = μ_kN) resists motion already in progress. Both
  depend on the normal force, not the contact area.
- **Equilibrium** — when F_net = 0, an object has zero acceleration (which
  includes being at rest OR moving at constant velocity).

## Common Misconceptions (most often missed)

1. **"A moving object needs a constant force to keep moving."** False
   (Aristotelian, not Newtonian) — an object in motion with no net force
   continues at constant velocity forever (Newton's First Law). Force is
   needed to *change* motion, not sustain it.
2. **"Action-reaction pairs cancel each other out."** False — they act on
   *different* objects, so they never cancel for either individual object.
   Forces only cancel when they act on the *same* object and sum to zero
   (that's equilibrium, a completely different idea).
3. **"Heavier objects require more force to keep moving at constant
   velocity."** Constant velocity means zero acceleration, which means
   zero *net* force — regardless of mass. Confusing this with the force
   needed to overcome friction (which does depend on weight) is the root
   of the error.
4. **"If an object is moving, there must be a net force in the direction
   of motion."** False — constant-velocity motion has zero net force in
   any direction. A net force in the direction of motion only exists if
   the object is speeding up.
5. **Putting the wrong forces on a free-body diagram** — including forces
   the object exerts on something else, or double-counting an
   action-reaction pair on one FBD.
6. **"A bigger/heavier object exerts a bigger force in a collision, so the
   reaction force from the smaller object is weaker."** False — by the
   Third Law, the forces are exactly equal in magnitude in every case
   (a car and a fly hitting each other exert equal-magnitude forces on
   each other; their *accelerations* differ hugely because their masses
   differ).

## Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

- State and apply Newton's First, Second, and Third Laws to explain
  observed motion.
- Draw correct free-body diagrams for objects on inclines, in
  equilibrium, and under applied forces with friction.
- Use F_net = ma to solve for unknown force, mass, or acceleration,
  including systems with multiple forces (friction, tension, gravity).
- Distinguish mass from weight and calculate weight given mass and
  gravitational field strength.
- Identify correct action-reaction pairs and explain why they don't
  produce equilibrium on a single object.
- Collect and analyze force/acceleration data (lab) to support the claim
  that F_net = ma (HS-PS2-1 evidence).

## Practice Problems

1. A 1200 kg car accelerates from rest to 25 m/s in 8.0 s. What is the net
   force acting on it?
2. A 5.0 kg box sits on a table. Draw its free-body diagram and state the
   magnitude of each force.
3. A 10 kg box is pulled across a floor with 40 N of applied force; kinetic
   friction is 15 N. Find the box's acceleration.
4. Explain, using Newton's Third Law, why a rifle recoils when it fires a
   bullet, and why the rifle's recoil velocity is much smaller than the
   bullet's velocity.
5. A 60 kg skater pushes off a 90 kg skater; both start at rest. Explain
   qualitatively (using F=ma and the Third Law) why the lighter skater
   ends up moving faster.

## Real-World Applications

- **Seatbelts and airbags** — directly demonstrate inertia (First Law):
  your body keeps moving forward in a crash unless a force acts on it.
- **Rocket propulsion** — Third Law: expelling exhaust gas backward
  pushes the rocket forward.
- **Vehicle design/traction control** — Second Law and friction combine to
  determine safe acceleration and braking.
- **Sports** — a sprinter's starting block push (Third Law), tackling in
  football (F=ma and momentum transfer).
