# Cornell Notes — Newton's Laws of Motion

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is Newton's First Law? | An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force. Also called the Law of Inertia. |
| What is inertia? | The tendency of an object to resist a change in its motion. Inertia depends only on mass — more mass = more inertia. |
| What is Newton's Second Law? Formula? | Net force equals mass times acceleration: **F_net = ma**. Rearranged: a = F_net / m. Units: Force in Newtons (N) = kg·m/s². |
| What is net force? | The vector sum of all forces acting on an object: F_net = ΣF. If forces are balanced, F_net = 0. |
| What is Newton's Third Law? | For every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force. **Key point:** the two forces act on two *different* objects, so they never cancel out for a single object. |
| What is a free body diagram (FBD)? | A diagram of a single object showing every force acting on it as an arrow, with length roughly proportional to force size and direction matching the force's direction. Used before applying F = ma. |
| What is normal force? | The support force a surface exerts perpendicular to itself on an object touching it. On a flat surface with no other vertical forces, N = mg — but N ≠ mg on an incline or when other vertical forces (like a pull or push) are present. |
| Static vs. kinetic friction — what's the difference? | Static friction (f_s) resists the *start* of sliding between surfaces at rest relative to each other; it adjusts up to a maximum: f_s(max) = μ_s·N. Kinetic friction (f_k) acts once surfaces are already sliding: f_k = μ_k·N. Usually μ_s > μ_k. |
| What is the coefficient of friction (μ)? | A unitless number describing the roughness between two surfaces. Larger μ = more friction. Depends on the two materials in contact, not on surface area. |
| What is tension? | The pulling force transmitted through a string, rope, or cable, directed along the string away from the object. |
| Mass vs. weight — what's the difference? | Mass (kg) = amount of matter, constant everywhere. Weight (N) = force of gravity on that mass: **W = mg**. Weight changes if gravity changes (e.g., on the Moon); mass does not. |
| What is equilibrium? | A state where net force = 0, so acceleration = 0. Object is either at rest (static equilibrium) or moving at constant velocity (dynamic equilibrium). |
| How do you handle an incline problem? | Resolve weight into components parallel and perpendicular to the incline surface: weight component along incline = mg·sin(θ); weight component into incline = mg·cos(θ). Normal force balances the perpendicular component: N = mg·cos(θ). |
| How do you handle connected objects (e.g., pulley systems)? | Treat each object separately with its own FBD and its own F = ma equation, using the same tension T and same magnitude of acceleration a (since they're connected); then solve the equations together (system approach can also combine total mass and total driving force). |

## Summary

Newton's three laws work together to explain motion: the First Law tells me objects resist changes in motion unless a net force acts on them, the Second Law (F = ma) lets me calculate exactly how much an object accelerates once I know the net force and mass, and the Third Law reminds me that forces always come in equal-and-opposite pairs acting on different objects. Before I can use F = ma, I have to draw a free body diagram and add up every force correctly, being careful with friction (static vs. kinetic) and normal force, which isn't always just mg. I need to remember that mass and weight are not the same thing, and that "balanced forces" means equilibrium, not "no forces at all." On inclines and with connected objects, the trickiest part is breaking forces into components with sin and cos and setting up one equation per object. Overall, this topic is really about being systematic: identify every force, draw it, sum it, then apply F = ma.
