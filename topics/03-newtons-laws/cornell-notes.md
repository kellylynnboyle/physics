| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What does Newton's First Law say? | An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net (unbalanced) force. "Net force = 0" describes both cases equally — rest is just the special case of constant velocity = 0. This property of resisting a change in motion is called **inertia**. |
| What does Newton's Second Law say? | Net force equals mass times acceleration: **F_net = ma**. It's a vector equation, so it applies separately along each axis — a vertical force does not cause horizontal acceleration, and vice versa. |
| What does Newton's Third Law say? | For every force one object exerts on a second object, the second exerts an equal-magnitude, opposite-direction force of the same type back on the first. The two forces act on **different objects** — never the same one — so they can never cancel each other out. |
| How do I build a free-body diagram (FBD)? | 1) Isolate ONE object. 2) Draw it as a dot or box. 3) Draw an arrow for every external force acting *on* it (weight, normal, tension, friction, applied) — nothing else. 4) Choose axes (tilt them along an incline if needed). 5) Resolve any diagonal forces into components before summing. |
| Is normal force always equal to weight? | No. On a flat horizontal surface with no other vertical forces, N = mg. On an incline, N = mg·cos(θ), which is less than mg. If another force has a vertical component (someone pushing down or pulling up), N must be found from the vertical force balance, not assumed equal to mg. |
| What's the difference between static and kinetic friction? | Static friction opposes the *start* of sliding and is variable — it adjusts up to a maximum of μ_s·N to match whatever's needed to keep the object still. Kinetic friction acts once the object is already sliding and has a roughly constant magnitude, μ_k·N, with μ_k typically less than μ_s. |
| Common trap: what's the Third Law pair of "gravity pulls the box down"? | NOT the normal force. The normal force acts on the box from the surface — same object, different force type — so it's a Second-Law/equilibrium relationship, not a Third-Law pair. The true Third-Law partner is "the box pulls the Earth up" (gravity, same type, acting on the Earth instead of the box). |
| Does mass equal weight? | No. Mass (kg) is the amount of matter and a measure of inertia; weight (N) = mg is the force of gravity on that mass. Mass is constant everywhere; weight changes with local gravity (a 10 kg mass weighs less on the Moon but is still 10 kg). |
| Why should I draw an FBD before writing F=ma? | Skipping it is the #1 cause of sign errors and missing forces (like forgetting a friction component, or a vertical component of an applied force at an angle). The FBD forces you to identify every force and its direction before you commit to an equation. |
| How do multi-object systems (Atwood machine, connected blocks) work? | Objects connected by an inextensible string/rope share the same magnitude of acceleration. Solve either with one equation for the whole system (net external force / total mass) or with separate FBDs per object, using the tension as the internal force linking them (equal tension throughout an ideal string, and the two ends' tension forces on the two objects are related by Newton's Third Law through the string). |

## Summary

Newton's three laws connect force, mass, and motion. The First Law says zero net force means constant velocity (including rest) — motion doesn't require a sustaining force, only a *change* in motion does. The Second Law, F_net = ma, is a vector equation applied per axis, and requires a correct free-body diagram to identify every force (weight, normal, tension, friction, applied, spring) before summing. The Third Law pairs are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, the same type of force, and always act on two *different* objects, so they never cancel within a single object's force balance — the most common student error is pairing gravity with the normal force instead of recognizing they act on the same object. Normal force is not automatically equal to weight; it must be solved from the vertical force balance, especially on an incline or with an added applied force. Friction is static (variable, up to a maximum) before sliding and kinetic (roughly constant) during sliding. Multi-object systems, like Atwood machines, extend F=ma by linking objects through a shared acceleration magnitude and internal tension forces.
