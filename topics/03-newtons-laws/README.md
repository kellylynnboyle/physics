# Unit 3: Newton's Laws

**Standards:** HS-PS2-1 (analyze data to support the claim that Newton's 2nd law describes the mathematical relationship among net force, mass, and acceleration); HS-PS2-4 (use mathematical representations of Newton's Law of Gravitation to describe and predict gravitational forces); HS-PS2-3 (design a device, with Unit 4).
**Science & Engineering Practices:** Analyzing data; using mathematics; planning and carrying out investigations.
**Suggested pacing:** 3 to 3.5 weeks

## Learning objectives
Students will be able to…
- [ ] **N1.** State Newton's three laws and give an everyday example of each.
- [ ] **N2.** Draw free-body diagrams that show all forces on one object with correct directions and labels.
- [ ] **N3.** Calculate net force and acceleration using F_net = ma in one and two dimensions.
- [ ] **N4.** Distinguish mass from weight, and weight from normal force (including in an accelerating elevator).
- [ ] **N5.** Identify Newton's 3rd-law force pairs (different objects, equal magnitude, opposite direction).
- [ ] **N6.** Model friction (static and kinetic), tension, and inclines in force problems.
- [ ] **N7.** Use Newton's law of universal gravitation, F = Gm₁m₂/r², to describe how gravity depends on mass and distance.
- [ ] **N8.** Plan an investigation that collects data on force, mass, and acceleration, and use a graph to support F_net = ma.

## Key concepts and equations
| Concept | Equation / idea |
|---|---|
| 1st law (inertia) | No net force → constant velocity (including zero) |
| 2nd law | ΣF = ma (vector equation, a points in the direction of F_net) |
| 3rd law | F_A on B = −F_B on A (different objects, same type of force) |
| Weight | W = mg (a force, in newtons) |
| Normal force | Perpendicular contact force, found from ΣF = ma, not always = mg |
| Friction | f_k = μ_k N; f_s ≤ μ_s N |
| Incline (frictionless) | a = g sinθ; component of weight along the slope = mg sinθ, perpendicular = mg cosθ |
| Gravitation | F = G m₁m₂/r², G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² |

## Most-missed ideas
1. A force is required to keep an object moving at constant velocity (no, only to change velocity).
2. Bigger objects exert bigger forces in a collision (they exert equal forces).
3. The normal force and weight are a 3rd-law pair (they aren't; they act on the same object).
4. Mixing up mass and weight, or kg and N.
5. Using the applied force instead of the net force in F = ma.
6. Forgetting that the normal force changes in elevators, on inclines, or when pulling at an angle.

## Lab ideas
- **Cart and hanging mass (2nd law):** Vary the hanging mass with total mass constant, graph a vs. F; then hold F constant and vary the mass, graph a vs. 1/m.
- **Friction lab:** Pull a block with a spring scale and find μ_s and μ_k for different surfaces.
- **Third-law force sensors:** Pull two force sensors against each other and show equal and opposite readings.
- **Elevator scale:** Use a phone accelerometer and a bathroom scale to explore apparent weight (if safe and permitted).

## Real-world applications
- Seat belts and crumple zones (inertia).
- Rocket and jet propulsion (3rd law).
- Roads and tires: friction, banking, ABS braking.
- Elevators and amusement-park rides: apparent weight.
- Satellites and orbits: gravity as the net force.

## Practice problems
1. A 1500 kg car has a net force of 3000 N. Find its acceleration, and its speed after 6 s from rest.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>a = 2 m/s²; v = 12 m/s.</details>
2. A 5 kg box on a frictionless floor is pulled by 20 N at 30° above horizontal. Find a and the normal force.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>a = 20 cos30°/5 ≈ 3.46 m/s²; N = 49 − 20 sin30° = 39 N.</details>
3. A 10 N push acts on a 2 kg block that touches a 3 kg block on a frictionless table. Find a and the force the 2 kg block exerts on the 3 kg block.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>a = 10/5 = 2 m/s²; force on 3 kg block = 3 × 2 = 6 N.</details>
4. A 4 kg block slides down a frictionless 30° incline. Find a.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>a = 9.8 sin30° = 4.9 m/s².</details>
5. An 80 kg skydiver experiences 500 N of air drag. Find the acceleration, and the drag at terminal velocity.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>W = 784 N; a = (784 − 500)/80 ≈ 3.55 m/s² down; at terminal velocity drag = 784 N.</details>
6. Two 1000 kg cars are 5 m apart. What is the gravitational force between them? (Why don't they move?)
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>F = 6.67×10⁻¹¹(10⁶)/25 ≈ 2.7×10⁻⁶ N, far too small compared with friction.</details>

## Assessment criteria
| Level | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Beginning (1)** | Recites the laws; free-body diagrams are missing forces or include "force of motion." |
| **Developing (2)** | Correct FBDs for simple cases; applies F = ma on one axis; confuses net and applied force at times. |
| **Proficient (3)** | Draws correct FBDs, writes ΣF = ma for each axis, solves with units, and identifies 3rd-law pairs. Quiz score ≥ 80%. |
| **Advanced (4)** | Solves multi-object and incline-with-friction problems, and uses lab graphs (slope = 1/m or m) to support the 2nd law with uncertainty. |
