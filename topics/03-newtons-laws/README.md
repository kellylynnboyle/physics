# Newton's Laws of Motion

Newton's three laws of motion explain how and why objects move the way they do, connecting the concepts of force, mass, and acceleration into a single predictive framework. Together they let us explain everyday phenomena — from why you lurch forward when a car stops short, to how rockets launch into space — using just a few equations and a systematic way of drawing forces. Mastering these laws means being able to identify every force acting on an object, represent them in a free body diagram, and use algebra to solve for unknown forces, masses, or accelerations.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Newton's First Law (Inertia)** — An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force.
- **Inertia** — The tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion, which depends only on its mass.
- **Newton's Second Law (F = ma)** — The net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration, giving a direct mathematical link between force, mass, and acceleration.
- **Net Force** — The vector sum of all forces acting on an object; it is the "unbalanced" force that actually causes acceleration.
- **Newton's Third Law (Action-Reaction)** — For every action force one object exerts on a second object, the second object exerts an equal and opposite force back on the first; these two forces act on different objects and never cancel each other out.
- **Free Body Diagrams (FBDs)** — A diagram showing an object as a point or box with arrows representing every force acting on it, used to organize forces before applying F = ma.
- **Normal Force** — The support force a surface exerts perpendicular to itself on an object in contact with it; it is not always equal to weight.
- **Friction (Static vs. Kinetic)** — Static friction resists the start of sliding between surfaces at rest relative to each other, while kinetic friction acts once surfaces are already sliding, and static friction's maximum value is generally greater than kinetic friction's value.
- **Coefficient of Friction (μ)** — A unitless number describing how "rough" two surfaces are together, used in the equations f_s(max) = μ_s·N and f_k = μ_k·N.
- **Tension** — The pulling force transmitted through a string, rope, or cable when it is pulled taut by forces acting from opposite ends.
- **Weight vs. Mass** — Mass is the amount of matter in an object (measured in kg, constant everywhere), while weight is the gravitational force on that mass (measured in Newtons, W = mg, and changes with gravity).
- **Equilibrium** — A state in which the net force on an object is zero, so the object has zero acceleration (either at rest or moving at constant velocity).
- **Inclined Planes and Connected Objects** — Applying Newton's second law to systems like objects on ramps or objects connected by strings over pulleys requires resolving weight into components (using sin and cos of the incline angle) and writing separate F = ma equations for each object or direction.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, students will be able to:

1. State Newton's First, Second, and Third Laws in their own words and identify real-world examples of each.
2. Distinguish between mass and weight, including correctly converting between them using W = mg.
3. Draw a correct, fully-labeled free body diagram for an object in a variety of scenarios (flat surface, incline, connected objects, pulley systems).
4. Calculate the net force on an object by summing multiple force vectors, including forces at angles.
5. Apply F = ma to solve for unknown force, mass, or acceleration in one- and two-dimensional problems.
6. Differentiate between static and kinetic friction and correctly calculate friction forces using the coefficient of friction.
7. Identify Newton's Third Law action-reaction pairs in a system and explain why they act on different objects and cannot produce equilibrium for a single object.
8. Analyze systems in equilibrium and systems of connected objects (e.g., blocks on inclines, Atwood machines) to solve for acceleration and tension.

## Assessment Criteria

| Level | Descriptor |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Can state Newton's Laws from memory but cannot apply them; struggles to identify forces acting on an object; free body diagrams are missing forces or mislabeled; confuses mass and weight. |
| **Developing** | Draws mostly correct free body diagrams for simple flat-surface problems; applies F = ma correctly only for single-force, single-object situations; inconsistently identifies friction type; needs guidance on incline problems. |
| **Proficient** | Draws accurate free body diagrams for inclines and multi-force situations; correctly sums forces before applying F = ma; distinguishes static vs. kinetic friction and selects the correct coefficient; solves basic connected-object problems with support. |
| **Mastery** | Independently draws correct free body diagrams for any configuration (inclines, pulleys, multiple connected objects); resolves forces into components accurately; solves multi-step problems involving friction, tension, and equilibrium; correctly explains why action-reaction pairs never cancel for a single object; checks answers for physical reasonableness. |

**Quantitative proficiency bar:** 80%+ on the topic quiz, with demonstrated ability to draw correct free body diagrams and solve multi-force F = ma problems (including at least one incline and one connected-objects problem) without step-by-step prompting.

## Practice Problems

1. A 5 kg box sits on a frictionless horizontal table. A 20 N horizontal force is applied. Find the box's acceleration.
2. A 1200 kg car accelerates from rest to 24 m/s in 8 seconds on a flat road. What net force was required?
3. Draw a free body diagram (describe it in words: list each force, its direction, and relative size) for a book resting motionless on a table, and explain why the normal force equals the book's weight in this case.
4. Draw a free body diagram (describe it in words) for a person standing motionless in an elevator that is accelerating upward, and explain whether the normal force is greater than, less than, or equal to the person's weight.
5. A 10 kg crate is pulled across a floor by a horizontal 40 N force. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the crate and floor is 0.25. Find the crate's acceleration.
6. A 3 kg block sits on a 30° frictionless incline. Find the block's acceleration down the incline.
7. Two blocks, mass 4 kg and 6 kg, are connected by a light string over a frictionless, massless pulley (an Atwood machine), hanging on either side. Find the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string.
8. A rocket engine pushes exhaust gas downward with a force of 50,000 N. According to Newton's Third Law, what force does the exhaust gas exert on the rocket, and in what direction? If the rocket's mass is 2,500 kg, what is its initial upward acceleration (ignoring gravity and air resistance for this estimate)?

### Answers

1. 4 m/s²
2. 3600 N
3. Normal force = weight = mg (two forces, equal and opposite, net force = 0, object in equilibrium)
4. Normal force > weight (net upward force needed to accelerate the person upward)
5. 1.55 m/s²
6. 4.9 m/s²
7. a ≈ 1.96 m/s²; T ≈ 47.0 N
8. 50,000 N upward on the rocket; a = 20 m/s²

## Real-World Applications

- **Seatbelts and inertia** — Seatbelts provide the force needed to stop a passenger's body when a car suddenly decelerates, since the passenger's body would otherwise keep moving forward at the original speed (Newton's First Law).
- **Rocket propulsion** — Rockets accelerate forward by expelling exhaust gas backward; the gas pushes the rocket forward with an equal and opposite force (Newton's Third Law).
- **Friction in car tires and braking** — The friction between tires and the road provides the force needed to accelerate, turn, and stop a car, which is why braking distance increases dramatically on wet or icy roads (lower μ).
- **Cargo on a truck bed** — Unsecured cargo will slide forward when a truck brakes suddenly because the cargo's inertia keeps it moving while the truck decelerates, illustrating the need for external forces (straps, friction) to change its motion.
- **Tug-of-war** — Each team pulls on the rope with an action-reaction pair of forces, but the outcome is determined by which team can generate greater friction (net force) against the ground, not by the tension forces "canceling."
