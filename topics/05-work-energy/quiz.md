# Quiz — Work, Energy & Power: Most Commonly Missed Questions

Take this quiz *before* checking any answers.

---

**1.** A waiter carries a tray of food at constant height across a room at constant velocity. How much work does the waiter's upward-supporting force do on the tray?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Zero. The supporting force is vertical (upward), while the displacement is horizontal — they're perpendicular, so cosθ = cos(90°) = 0, and W = F·d·cos(90°) = 0. Students often assume "carrying something requires work" because it's tiring, but the physics definition of work requires displacement *in the direction of the force*, and there is none here vertically.
</details>

---

**2.** A satellite orbits the Earth in a perfect circle at constant speed. How much work does gravity do on it per orbit?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Zero. Gravity (the centripetal force) always points toward the center of the circular orbit, which is always perpendicular to the satellite's velocity/displacement direction. Zero work means zero net change in kinetic energy, which matches the constant speed. This is the same principle as question 1, applied to circular motion.
</details>

---

**3.** If you double an object's speed, what happens to its kinetic energy?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

It quadruples. KE = ½mv², so KE depends on the *square* of velocity — doubling v multiplies KE by 2² = 4. A very common error is assuming KE simply doubles along with speed, treating the relationship as linear instead of quadratic. This matters hugely in real life (e.g., braking distance and crash severity scale similarly with speed squared).
</details>

---

**4.** A ball rolls down a frictionless curved ramp and a second identical ball rolls down a frictionless straight ramp of the same height. Which one reaches the bottom with a higher speed?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Neither — they have the same final speed. Conservation of energy (mgh = ½mv²) only depends on the height dropped, not the path or shape of the ramp (as long as it's frictionless). Students often assume the straight ramp or the curved ramp is "faster" based on intuition about the path length, but final speed depends only on height when there's no friction (though the *time* to get there can differ).
</details>

---

**5.** A box slides across a rough floor and comes to rest. Is mechanical energy (KE + PE) conserved in this process?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

No — mechanical energy is NOT conserved here, because friction (a non-conservative force) does negative work on the box, converting its kinetic energy into heat. Total energy (including heat) is still conserved, but mechanical energy specifically is not. A common mistake is stating "energy is always conserved" as if that means KE + PE must stay constant — that's only true in the *absence* of non-conservative forces doing net work.
</details>

---

**6.** Two motors each lift an identical 100 kg crate 5 m straight up. Motor A does it in 10 seconds; Motor B does it in 5 seconds. Compare the work done and the power output of each motor.

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Both motors do the *same amount of work* (W = mgh, which only depends on mass, g, and height — not time). But Motor B has *twice the power* of Motor A, since it does the same work in half the time (P = W/t). The most common error is assuming the motor working faster "did more work," when really it only delivered more power (a higher rate) — work and power are different quantities.
</details>

---

**7.** A spring is compressed to store elastic potential energy, then released to launch a ball. If you compress the spring twice as far, how does the stored energy change?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

It quadruples. PE_spring = ½kx², so like kinetic energy, it depends on the square of the displacement — doubling x multiplies stored energy by 4, not 2. This parallels the KE = ½mv² mistake in question 3 and is worth noticing as a pattern: any "one-half times a squared term" formula scales quadratically, not linearly.
</details>
