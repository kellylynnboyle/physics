# Cornell Notes — Work, Energy & Power

## Notes

| Cues | Notes |
|---|---|
| Define work | W = F·d·cosθ, where θ is the angle between the applied force and the displacement direction. Units: joules (J). |
| When is work exactly zero? | When force is perpendicular to displacement (θ = 90°, cosθ = 0), or when there is no displacement at all (d = 0). |
| Define kinetic energy | KE = ½mv². Depends on speed squared — doubling speed quadruples KE. |
| State the work-energy theorem | W_net = ΔKE. The total (net) work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy. |
| Define gravitational potential energy | PE_grav = mgh, measured relative to a chosen reference height (you pick where h = 0). |
| Define elastic potential energy | PE_spring = ½kx², where x is displacement from the spring's equilibrium (natural) length. |
| State conservation of mechanical energy | KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f, ONLY when non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance, applied pushes) do zero net work on the system. |
| What happens to energy "lost" to friction? | It's converted to heat (and sometimes sound) — total energy is still conserved overall, but mechanical energy (KE + PE) is not. |
| Define power | P = W/t = F·v (for constant force in the direction of motion). Units: watts (W) = joules/second. |
| Why doesn't the shape of a frictionless hill matter for final speed? | Because energy conservation only depends on the starting and ending height (PE = mgh), not the path taken — this is the power of energy methods over kinematics for curved paths. |
| Common mistake to avoid | Applying conservation of energy (KE+PE=constant) in a problem where friction is present and significant — you must add a term for energy lost to friction, or use the work-energy theorem with friction as one of the forces doing work. |
| Common mistake to avoid #2 | Confusing energy (a total amount, in joules) with power (a rate, in watts) — e.g., saying two motors "used the same energy" when really they delivered the same power over different times. |
