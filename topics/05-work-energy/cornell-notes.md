| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is work, and when is it zero? | Work W = Fd·cos(θ), where θ is the angle between the force and the displacement. Only the component of force *along* the direction of displacement does work. If force is perpendicular to displacement (θ = 90°), W = 0 — e.g., carrying a bag horizontally does zero work against gravity, and holding a heavy object stationary does zero work at all (no displacement). |
| What is the work-energy theorem? | W_net = ΔKE = KE_f − KE_i. The net work done by ALL forces on an object equals its change in kinetic energy. This lets you relate forces directly to speed changes without needing time or acceleration. |
| What is kinetic energy, and how does it scale with speed? | KE = ½mv² — energy of motion. Because v is squared, doubling an object's speed QUADRUPLES its kinetic energy (not doubles it). This matters a lot for stopping distances and collision severity. |
| What is gravitational potential energy? | PE_grav = mgh, where h is height above a chosen reference level. PE has no absolute value — only the CHOICE of reference point matters, and only differences in PE (or consistent use of one reference) are meaningful in a problem. |
| What is elastic (spring) potential energy? | PE_spring = ½kx², where k is the spring constant (from Hooke's law, F = -kx) and x is the displacement from the spring's equilibrium (natural) length. |
| What does conservation of mechanical energy say, and when does it fail? | When only conservative forces act (gravity, ideal springs — no friction/air resistance), total mechanical energy is constant: KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f. It "fails" (mechanical energy decreases) whenever a non-conservative force like friction or air resistance is present — that energy isn't destroyed, it converts to heat/sound, so total energy (mechanical + heat) is still conserved. |
| What is power, and what are its units? | P = W/t (rate of doing work), and for a constant force along the motion, P = Fv. Units: watts (W) = joules/second. Power is NOT the same as force or work — a small motor can do the same total work as a large one, just more slowly (less power). |
| Common trap: does more force always mean more work? | No — work requires displacement in the direction of the force. Zero displacement (holding something still) or force perpendicular to displacement (carrying something at constant height) both give zero work, even if the force is large. |

## Summary

Work is done only by the component of a force acting along the direction of an object's displacement (W = Fd cos θ), which is why perpendicular forces — and forces with no displacement at all — do zero work. The work-energy theorem ties net work directly to the change in kinetic energy (KE = ½mv², which scales with the *square* of speed). Gravitational PE (mgh) and elastic PE (½kx²) are two common ways energy is stored, and when only conservative forces act, total mechanical energy (KE + PE) is conserved from one point to another. Friction and other non-conservative forces break this conservation by converting mechanical energy into heat, so problems with friction must be solved with energy accounting rather than simple conservation. Power measures how quickly energy is transferred (P = W/t = Fv) and should never be confused with work or force themselves.
