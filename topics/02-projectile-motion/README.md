# Projectile Motion

Projectile motion describes the curved path of any object launched into the air and acted on only by gravity — a thrown football, a diver, or a cannonball. The key insight is that the horizontal and vertical parts of the motion happen independently and can be analyzed separately using the same kinematics equations from 1D motion. This topic builds directly on free fall and constant-velocity motion to explain trajectories, range, and height for objects launched horizontally or at an angle.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Independence of motion** — Horizontal and vertical motions occur simultaneously but do not affect each other; you can analyze each direction with its own separate set of equations.
- **Constant horizontal velocity** — Since gravity has no horizontal component, horizontal velocity (vx) stays the same throughout the entire flight (ignoring air resistance).
- **Vertical motion is free fall** — The vertical component of motion (vy) accelerates downward at g = 9.8 m/s², exactly like an object dropped from rest.
- **Velocity components** — An initial velocity v launched at angle θ above the horizontal splits into vx = v·cosθ (horizontal) and vy = v·sinθ (vertical).
- **Time of flight** — For a projectile launched and landing at the same height, total time in the air is t = 2vy/g, or equivalently t = (2v·sinθ)/g.
- **Maximum height** — The peak height occurs when vertical velocity momentarily equals zero, calculated as h = vy²/(2g).
- **Range formula** — Horizontal distance traveled is R = (v²·sin(2θ))/g, valid when launch and landing heights are equal.
- **45° gives maximum range** — For a fixed launch speed, a 45° launch angle produces the greatest horizontal range on level ground.
- **90° gives maximum height** — Launching straight up (θ = 90°) maximizes vertical height but produces zero range.
- **Complementary angles share range** — Two angles that add to 90° (like 30° and 60°) produce the same range, though different heights and flight times.
- **Parabolic trajectory** — The combination of constant horizontal velocity and accelerating vertical velocity produces a symmetric, parabolic path.
- **Symmetry of the path** — For equal launch and landing heights, the time to rise equals the time to fall, and the speed at a given height is the same on the way up and down.
- **Horizontal vs. angled launches** — A horizontally launched projectile (θ = 0°) starts with vy = 0, so its fall time depends only on launch height, not on horizontal speed.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, students will be able to:

1. Explain why horizontal and vertical motions of a projectile are independent of each other.
2. Decompose an initial velocity vector into horizontal and vertical components using sine and cosine.
3. Calculate the time of flight for a projectile launched horizontally or at an angle.
4. Calculate the maximum height reached by a projectile.
5. Calculate the horizontal range of a projectile launched at an angle.
6. Predict how changing the launch angle affects range and maximum height, and identify the angle that maximizes each.
7. Compare and explain the motion of objects launched horizontally versus objects dropped from the same height (same fall time).
8. Solve multi-step projectile motion problems that combine velocity components, time of flight, height, and range.

## Assessment Criteria

| Level | Descriptor |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Can state that projectiles follow a curved path but cannot separate horizontal and vertical motion; struggles to identify which formula applies to a given question. |
| **Developing** | Can decompose velocity into vx and vy with guidance, but makes frequent sign or sin/cos errors; can solve simple horizontal-launch problems but not angled-launch problems. |
| **Proficient** | Correctly decomposes velocity components, calculates time of flight, height, and range for both horizontal and angled launches; understands why 45° maximizes range. |
| **Mastery** | Solves multi-step and non-standard problems (different launch/landing heights, comparing angles, working backward from range or height) and can clearly explain the reasoning behind independence of motion and trajectory symmetry. |

**Quantitative proficiency bar:** 80%+ on the topic quiz, with demonstrated ability to correctly decompose velocity vectors (vx, vy) and solve for time of flight, maximum height, and range in both horizontal and angled-launch problems.

## Practice Problems

1. **(Easy)** A marble rolls off a horizontal table 0.80 m high with a horizontal velocity of 2.5 m/s. How long does it take to hit the ground?
2. **(Easy)** For the marble in problem 1, how far from the base of the table does it land?
3. **(Easy — conceptual)** A heavy bowling ball and a light tennis ball are launched horizontally from the same height at the same instant. Ignoring air resistance, which one hits the ground first?
4. **(Medium)** A soccer ball is kicked with an initial speed of 18 m/s at an angle of 35° above the horizontal. Find its horizontal (vx) and vertical (vy) velocity components.
5. **(Medium)** Using the soccer ball from problem 4, find its total time of flight (it lands at the same height it was kicked from).
6. **(Medium)** Using the soccer ball from problem 4, find the maximum height it reaches.
7. **(Hard)** Using the soccer ball from problem 4, find its horizontal range.
8. **(Hard — conceptual)** Two footballs are thrown with the same initial speed, one at 25° above the horizontal and one at 65° above the horizontal. (a) Which one reaches a greater maximum height? (b) Which one has the greater range?

### Answers

1. t ≈ 0.40 s
2. x ≈ 1.0 m
3. They land at the same time (vertical motion is independent of horizontal motion and of mass).
4. vx ≈ 14.7 m/s, vy ≈ 10.3 m/s
5. t ≈ 2.10 s
6. h ≈ 5.41 m
7. R ≈ 31.1 m
8. (a) The 65° launch reaches a greater maximum height. (b) Both have the same range (25° and 65° are complementary angles).

## Real-World Applications

- **Basketball free throws and shots** — Players intuitively choose a launch angle and speed to arc the ball into the hoop, balancing height and range.
- **Artillery and ballistics** — Military and engineering calculations of projectile range and impact point rely directly on projectile motion equations.
- **Long jump and high jump** — Athletes optimize their takeoff angle and speed to maximize horizontal distance or vertical height.
- **Water fountains** — Decorative fountains use nozzle angle and water pressure (launch speed) to control the height and reach of water arcs.
- **Sports like golf, football, and soccer** — Punting, driving, and passing all involve choosing a launch angle to maximize hang time, height, or distance depending on the goal.
