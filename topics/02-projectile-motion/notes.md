# Unit 2: Projectile Motion

Projectile motion is kinematics in two dimensions at once: horizontal and
vertical motion happen independently and simultaneously. This is the
single biggest idea in the unit — master it and the rest follows.

## Key Concepts

- **Independence of motion** — horizontal and vertical motion are
  independent of each other. The only thing they share is time.
- **Horizontal motion** — no horizontal acceleration (ignoring air
  resistance), so horizontal velocity (vₓ) is constant throughout the
  flight. Use x = vₓt.
- **Vertical motion** — constant acceleration due to gravity,
  a = −9.8 m/s². Use the same kinematic equations as Unit 1, applied only
  to the vertical component.
- **Launch angle decomposition** — for a projectile launched at angle θ
  with speed v₀: vₓ = v₀cosθ, v_y = v₀sinθ.
- **Time of flight** — determined entirely by the vertical motion (how
  long it takes to go up and come back down, or to fall a given height).
  Horizontal speed does not affect time of flight for a level launch/landing.
- **Range** — horizontal distance traveled; R = vₓ × (time of flight).
  Maximum range (level ground) occurs at a 45° launch angle.
- **Symmetry** — for a projectile launched and landing at the same height,
  the trajectory is symmetric: time up = time down, and the speed at a
  given height is the same on the way up and the way down (velocity
  direction differs).
- **At the peak** — vertical velocity is zero, but horizontal velocity is
  unchanged and acceleration is still −9.8 m/s² (never zero during flight).

## Common Misconceptions (most often missed)

1. **"A faster horizontal launch makes the object take longer to fall."**
   False — for a horizontally launched projectile off a table/cliff, time
   to hit the ground depends only on the height and gravity, not on
   horizontal speed. A bullet fired horizontally and a ball dropped from
   the same height hit the ground at the same time.
2. **"At the top of the arc, the projectile momentarily stops completely."**
   False — only the *vertical* velocity is zero at the peak. Horizontal
   velocity is unchanged and nonzero (assuming it launched with horizontal
   velocity), so the object is still moving.
3. **Treating launch speed as if it applies to both directions equally.**
   Students often plug v₀ directly into both x = v₀t and vertical
   equations instead of decomposing into vₓ = v₀cosθ and v_y = v₀sinθ
   first.
4. **"Maximum height and maximum range happen at the same launch angle."**
   False — maximum height increases as launch angle increases toward 90°,
   but maximum range peaks at 45° (level ground) and is symmetric about
   it (e.g., 30° and 60° give the same range).
5. **Forgetting that acceleration is still −9.8 m/s² at the peak.** Same
   misconception as Unit 1, reapplied in 2D — very common on assessments.

## Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

- Decompose an initial velocity vector into horizontal and vertical
  components using trigonometry.
- Apply 1D kinematic equations separately to the horizontal and vertical
  motion of a projectile.
- Solve for time of flight, range, and maximum height for both
  horizontally-launched and angled-launch projectiles.
- Explain why horizontal and vertical motions are independent, using
  evidence (e.g., "monkey and hunter" or dropped-vs-fired demonstrations).
- Justify why 45° maximizes range on level ground.

## Assessment Criteria

| Level | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Mastery | Correctly decomposes angled launches, solves full multi-part projectile problems (time, range, max height), and explains independence of motion with evidence. |
| Proficient | Solves horizontally-launched projectile problems correctly; needs support decomposing angled launches. |
| Developing | Attempts component decomposition but mixes horizontal/vertical equations. |
| Beginning | Treats horizontal and vertical motion as linked (e.g., believes faster launch speed changes fall time). |

## Practice Problems

1. A ball rolls off a table 1.2 m high at 3.0 m/s horizontally. Find the
   time to hit the floor and the horizontal distance traveled.
2. A soccer ball is kicked at 18 m/s at 35° above horizontal. Find the
   time of flight, maximum height, and range.
3. Compare the range of projectiles launched at 30° and 60° at the same
   speed. What do you notice, and why?
4. A rescue plane flying horizontally at 60 m/s at an altitude of 500 m
   drops a supply package. How far ahead of the target (horizontally)
   should it release the package?
5. Explain, using kinematics, why a projectile launched at 45° has equal
   horizontal and vertical velocity components at launch.

## Real-World Applications

- **Sports** — basketball free throws, long jump, javelin, golf: optimal
  launch angle and speed determine distance/accuracy.
- **Ballistics and artillery** — range tables historically calculated from
  projectile motion equations.
- **Water fountains and firefighting hoses** — nozzle angle controls
  spray distance.
- **Search-and-rescue air drops** — timing a supply drop requires solving
  exactly the "rescue plane" problem above.
