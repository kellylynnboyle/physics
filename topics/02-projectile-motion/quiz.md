# Quiz — Projectile Motion: Most Commonly Missed Questions

Take this quiz *before* checking any answers.

---

**1.** A projectile is launched at an angle. What happens to its horizontal velocity during the flight?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

It stays constant the entire flight (assuming no air resistance) — there is no horizontal acceleration. The most common error is assuming horizontal velocity decreases as the object rises, confusing it with the vertical component.
</details>

---

**2.** At the very top of a projectile's arc (angled launch), what is true about its velocity?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Vertical velocity (v_y) = 0, but horizontal velocity (vₓ) is unchanged and nonzero. The projectile is NOT momentarily at rest — only its vertical component is zero. This is confused with the "velocity = 0 at the top" idea from pure vertical (1D) motion.
</details>

---

**3.** Two balls leave a table at the same instant: one is dropped straight down, the other rolls off horizontally at high speed. Which hits the floor first?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

They hit at the same time. Vertical motion is governed only by g and the initial vertical velocity, both of which are identical for the two balls (v_y₀ = 0 for both). Horizontal velocity has zero effect on fall time — this is the clearest demonstration of independence of motion.
</details>

---

**4.** A ball launched at 40° and a ball launched at 50° (same speed) land at the same horizontal distance. Why?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

40° and 50° are complementary angles (they add to 90°), and range depends on sin(2θ), which is equal for complementary angles. Students often assume range increases smoothly with angle up to 90°, when actually it peaks at 45° and is symmetric around it.
</details>

---

**5.** For a projectile launched and landing at the same height, how does the impact speed compare to the launch speed?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

They're equal (by symmetry — energy is conserved and the vertical velocity at landing has the same magnitude, opposite sign, as at launch). A common mistake is assuming the projectile "loses speed" over the flight when there's no air resistance.
</details>

---

**6.** In a horizontal-launch problem (e.g., ball rolling off a table), what is the initial vertical velocity?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Zero. All of the initial speed is horizontal. Students sometimes incorrectly use the full launch speed as v_y₀, which comes from not recognizing "horizontal launch" as a special case of the general angled-launch setup (θ = 0°).
</details>

---

**7.** Why can't you plug a diagonal (resultant) velocity directly into the vertical kinematic equations?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

The kinematic equations for vertical motion only use the vertical *component* of velocity (v_y = v·sinθ), not the full resultant speed. Using the full speed overstates how much vertical motion is happening. Always decompose into components before applying kinematics equations to either axis.
</details>

---

**8.** What causes the parabolic shape of a projectile's path?

<details>
<summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

Constant horizontal velocity combined with constantly changing (accelerating) vertical velocity produces a parabola when you plot y vs. x. Some students think the "curve" comes from air resistance, but it happens even in a vacuum — it's purely a result of combining constant-velocity horizontal motion with constant-acceleration vertical motion.
</details>
