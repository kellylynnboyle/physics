# Cornell Notes — Kinematics

**Topic:** Describing Motion in 1D
**Date:** ______________          **Name:** ______________

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What's the difference between distance and displacement? | Distance = total path length (scalar, always ≥ 0). Displacement = straight-line change in position, `Δx = x_f − x_i` (vector, can be negative or zero). |
| What's the difference between speed and velocity? | Speed = distance / time (scalar). Velocity = displacement / time (vector — has direction). |
| How do I find velocity from a position–time graph? | **Slope** of the position–time graph at a point = instantaneous velocity. Average velocity = slope of the line connecting the start and end points. |
| How do I find acceleration and displacement from a velocity–time graph? | **Slope** of velocity–time graph = acceleration. **Area under** the velocity–time graph = displacement. |
| What are the 4 kinematic equations, and when do I use each? | `v = v₀+at` (no x); `x = x₀+v₀t+½at²` (no v); `v²=v₀²+2a(x−x₀)` (no t); `x = x₀+½(v₀+v)t` (no a). Pick the one missing the variable you don't have/need. |
| What is true about acceleration during free fall? | `a = −9.8 m/s²` (choosing up as positive) at *all* times during the flight — including the instant velocity = 0 at the top. Gravity never turns off. |
| Why do sign conventions matter? | If up/right is chosen positive, then downward velocity, leftward acceleration, etc. must be entered as negative. Mixing conventions mid-problem is the #1 source of wrong-sign answers. |
| Common trap: does negative acceleration always mean "slowing down"? | No. Negative acceleration means acceleration points in the negative direction. If velocity is also negative, the object is *speeding up* in the negative direction. Speeding up vs. slowing down depends on whether `a` and `v` have the *same* or *opposite* sign. |

## Summary
Kinematics separates *what* motion looks like from *why* it happens.
Vectors (displacement, velocity, acceleration) carry direction; scalars
(distance, speed) do not. Graphs and the four kinematic equations are two
views of the same information — slope and area on a graph correspond
directly to terms in the equations. The trickiest ideas are (1) distance
vs. displacement, (2) that acceleration can exist even when velocity is
momentarily zero, and (3) that the sign of acceleration alone doesn't tell
you if something is speeding up or slowing down — you need to compare it
to the sign of the velocity.
