# Section 1: Kinematics

## Overview
Kinematics is the description of motion — position, velocity, and
acceleration — without worrying about *why* the motion happens (that's
Newton's Laws, Section 3). This section builds the vocabulary and graph/
equation toolkit used in every later section.

## Key Concepts to Master
- **Scalars vs. vectors**: distance and speed are scalars (magnitude only);
  displacement and velocity are vectors (magnitude + direction).
- **Displacement vs. distance**: displacement is the straight-line change
  in position (can be zero even after a long trip); distance is total
  path length traveled.
- **Average vs. instantaneous velocity**: average velocity = displacement
  ÷ time; instantaneous velocity is the velocity at one moment (slope of
  the position–time graph at a point).
- **Acceleration**: rate of change of velocity, `a = Δv / Δt`. Acceleration
  is a vector — it can speed an object up, slow it down, or change its
  direction.
- **Reading graphs**: on a position–time graph, slope = velocity. On a
  velocity–time graph, slope = acceleration and the area under the curve
  = displacement.
- **The four kinematic equations** (constant acceleration only):

| Equation | Missing variable |
|---|---|
| `v = v₀ + at` | displacement |
| `x = x₀ + v₀t + ½at²` | final velocity |
| `v² = v₀² + 2a(x − x₀)` | time |
| `x = x₀ + ½(v₀ + v)t` | acceleration |

- **Free fall**: acceleration is constant at `g = 9.8 m/s²` directed
  downward, whether the object is rising, falling, or momentarily at rest
  at the top of its path.
- **Sign convention**: pick a positive direction before starting a problem
  and stay consistent — velocity, acceleration, and displacement can all
  be negative.

## Learning Objectives
Students will be able to:
1. Correctly distinguish distance/displacement and speed/velocity in
   both words and calculations.
2. Read slope and area from position–time and velocity–time graphs to
   find velocity, acceleration, and displacement.
3. Select and apply the correct kinematic equation for a given set of
   knowns/unknowns.
4. Solve free-fall problems, including objects thrown upward, showing
   that acceleration is nonzero even when velocity is momentarily zero.
5. Use consistent sign conventions across a multi-step problem.

## Practice Problems

1. A car accelerates from rest at 3 m/s² for 6 seconds. How far does it
   travel?
2. A ball is thrown straight up at 20 m/s. How long until it returns to
   the thrower's hand? What is its velocity at the very top?
3. A runner covers 400 m around a track and ends up back at the starting
   line. What is the distance traveled? What is the displacement?
4. A velocity–time graph shows a line going from (0 s, 0 m/s) to (4 s, 12
   m/s), then flat at 12 m/s from 4 s to 7 s. Find the acceleration for
   each segment and the total displacement over 7 s.
5. A train slows from 30 m/s to 10 m/s over a distance of 400 m. Find its
   acceleration.

### Answer Key
1. `x = ½at² = ½(3)(6²) = 54 m`
2. Time up = time down (symmetric, same launch/landing height):
   `t_total = 2v₀/g = 2(20)/9.8 ≈ 4.08 s`. Velocity at top = **0 m/s**
   (but acceleration is still −9.8 m/s²).
3. Distance = **400 m**; displacement = **0 m** (start = end position).
4. Segment 1: `a = (12 − 0)/(4 − 0) = 3 m/s²`. Segment 2: `a = 0 m/s²`
   (constant velocity). Displacement = area of triangle (½·4·12 = 24 m) +
   area of rectangle (3·12 = 36 m) = **60 m**.
5. `v² = v₀² + 2a·x → 10² = 30² + 2a(400) → a = (100 − 900)/800 = −1 m/s²`.

## Real-World Applications
- **Traffic safety**: stopping-distance calculations use the kinematic
  equations directly (reaction distance + braking distance).
- **Sports**: sprint-start acceleration analysis, timing splits.
- **Space launches**: rockets track velocity and acceleration profiles
  during ascent to manage g-forces on astronauts.
- **Elevators**: engineers use jerk (rate of change of acceleration) and
  the kinematic equations to keep rides smooth and within time limits.

## Assessment Criteria
- **Mastery**: correctly chooses among the four kinematic equations without
  prompting, interprets graphs fluently, and explains why acceleration is
  nonzero at the peak of a throw.
- **Proficient**: solves standard single-equation problems correctly;
  occasionally needs help with multi-step (e.g., "thrown upward, lands
  below the starting point") problems.
- **Developing**: can define terms but frequently mixes up distance/
  displacement or misreads graph slope vs. area.
