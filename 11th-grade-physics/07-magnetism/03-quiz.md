# Unit 7: Magnetism — Interactive Quiz (Most-Missed Questions)

Take this quiz without notes. 8/10 (80%) to advance to Unit 8.

---

**Q1.** A charged particle moves through a uniform magnetic field at
constant speed, curving into a circular path. What is true about the
magnetic force acting on it?

A) It speeds the particle up
B) It slows the particle down
C) It changes the particle's direction but not its speed
D) It has no effect at all

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C.**

This is the #1 most-missed idea in the unit: magnetic force is always
perpendicular to velocity, so it can never do work or change speed — only
direction. That's exactly why charged particles move in circles (constant
speed, continuously changing direction) in a uniform B field.
</details>

---

**Q2.** A positive charge sits at rest inside a strong magnetic field. What
force does it experience?

A) A force in the direction of B
B) A force perpendicular to B
C) Zero force
D) A force equal to qB

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C — zero.**

F=qvB requires velocity — a charge at rest has v=0, so F=0 no matter how
strong the field is. Students often assume any charge in a field feels a
force, forgetting the *moving* requirement that's central to magnetic
force (unlike electric force, which acts on charges whether moving or not).
</details>

---

**Q3.** Can you isolate a single north magnetic pole by cutting a bar magnet
in half?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**No — cutting a bar magnet in half produces two smaller magnets, each with its own north AND south pole.**

Magnetic monopoles (an isolated N or S with no opposite pole) have never
been observed — this is fundamentally different from electric charge,
where isolating a single + or - charge is completely normal. This
distinction is one of the most commonly missed conceptual points connecting
this unit back to Unit 6.
</details>

---

**Q4.** A current-carrying wire is placed in a magnetic field, with the
current perpendicular to B. If you double the current, what happens to the
force on the wire?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**It doubles.**

F=BIL sin(θ), and force is directly proportional to current I. Straightforward,
but included since it's frequently mixed up with the *charge* version of
the force law, where the question is about velocity, not current directly.
</details>

---

**Q5.** Two bar magnets are brought close together, north pole facing north
pole. What happens?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**They repel each other.**

Like poles repel; opposite poles attract — the magnetic analog of like
charges repelling in electricity. A common error is applying the electric
charge rule backward or forgetting poles follow the same "like repels,
opposite attracts" pattern as charges.
</details>

---

**Q6.** A loop of wire sits near a bar magnet, both completely stationary.
Is any current induced in the loop?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**No.**

Electromagnetic induction requires *changing* magnetic flux through the
loop — a magnet just sitting there, even if strong, produces a constant
(unchanging) flux, so no current is induced. Students often think "a
magnet nearby" is enough; it's the *change* that matters, not the mere
presence of a field.
</details>

---

**Q7.** Which of the following would induce a current in a nearby loop of wire?

A) Holding a stationary magnet 1 cm from the loop
B) Holding a stationary magnet 10 cm from the loop
C) Moving a magnet toward or away from the loop
D) None of the above

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C.**

Same idea as Q6 — motion (or any change in the field/orientation/area)
changes the flux through the loop, which is what actually induces current.
Distance alone (A vs B) doesn't matter if nothing is *changing*.
</details>

---

**Q8.** True or False: The right-hand rule for the force on a moving
positive charge is the same rule used to find the magnetic field direction
around a straight current-carrying wire.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**False (they're different applications of the same underlying hand geometry, but used differently).**

For force on a charge: fingers point along v, curl toward B, thumb gives F
(or use the "flat hand" cross-product version). For the field around a
wire: thumb points along the current, and curling fingers show the
*circular* direction of B around the wire. Mixing these up — applying the
wire's field rule when you need the force rule, or vice versa — is one of
the most common sources of wrong answers in this unit.
</details>

---

**Q9.** A solenoid (coil of wire) carries current that curls counterclockwise
when viewed from one end. Using the right-hand rule, is that end the north
or south pole?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**North pole.**

Curl your right-hand fingers in the direction the current flows around the
coil (counterclockwise, as viewed from this end); your thumb points out of
that end — the thumb direction is the north pole. This is the same
right-hand-rule logic as a wire loop, just applied to a coil.
</details>

---

**Q10.** An electron (negative charge) moves to the right through a
magnetic field pointing into the page. Compared to a proton moving the same
direction through the same field, how does the direction of the magnetic
force differ?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**The force is in the opposite direction — same speed and field, but the negative charge flips the direction predicted by the right-hand rule for a positive charge.**

Apply the right-hand rule as if the charge were positive to get one
direction, then flip it 180° for a negative charge. Forgetting to flip
the direction for negative charges is one of the most common errors on
force-direction problems in this unit.
</details>
