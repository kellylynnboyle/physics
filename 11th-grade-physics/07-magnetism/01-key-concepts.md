# Unit 7: Magnetism — Key Concepts

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:
1. Describe magnetic fields, poles, and field lines around bar magnets and current-carrying wires.
2. Apply the right-hand rule to find the direction of a magnetic field or force.
3. Calculate the force on a moving charge (F=qvB) and a current-carrying wire (F=BIL) in a magnetic field.
4. Explain that magnetic force does no work on a moving charge (it's always perpendicular to velocity).
5. Describe how an electromagnet works and how electromagnetic induction generates current (conceptually).
6. Distinguish magnetic poles from electric charges (poles always come in pairs; no magnetic monopoles).

## Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Magnetic field (B) | A field around magnets and moving charges/currents, measured in Tesla (T) |
| Magnetic pole | North/south ends of a magnet; like poles repel, opposite poles attract |
| Right-hand rule | Method for finding the direction of B, F, or induced current using your right hand |
| Electromagnet | A magnet created by current flowing through a coiled wire |
| Electromagnetic induction | Generating a current by changing the magnetic flux through a loop of wire |
| Solenoid | A coil of wire that produces a strong, uniform magnetic field inside when current flows |
| Magnetic flux | A measure of the total magnetic field passing through a surface |

## Core Formulas

- Force on a moving charge: F = qvB sin(θ) (θ = angle between v and B)
- Force on a current-carrying wire: F = BIL sin(θ) (θ = angle between wire and B)
- Right-hand rule for force on a positive charge: point fingers along v, curl toward B, thumb points along F (or: fingers = v, curl into B, thumb = F for the qv×B cross product)
- Right-hand rule for a wire's field: thumb along current, fingers curl in the direction of B around the wire
- Right-hand rule for a solenoid: curl fingers in the direction of current flow around the coil, thumb points toward the north pole

## The Big Idea

**Magnetic force is always perpendicular to a moving charge's velocity, so
it changes the charge's direction but never its speed — it does zero
work.** This is why charged particles move in circles (or helices) in a
uniform magnetic field rather than speeding up or slowing down, and it's
the single fact that separates magnetic force from every other force
you've studied this year (gravity, friction, tension) which all *can* do
work.

## Common Misconceptions to Address Directly

- Believing magnetic force can speed up or slow down a charged particle
  (it can't — it only changes direction, since F is always perpendicular
  to v, so it can never have a component along v).
- Confusing magnetic poles with electric charges — magnets always have
  *both* a north and south pole; you cannot isolate a single magnetic
  "monopole" the way you can isolate a single positive or negative charge.
- Applying the right-hand rule inconsistently (mixing up which rule
  version — for force vs. for field around a wire vs. for a solenoid).
- Believing a stationary charge in a magnetic field experiences a force
  (it doesn't — F=qvB requires the charge to be *moving*; a charge at rest
  feels zero magnetic force no matter how strong B is).
- Thinking electromagnetic induction requires a magnet to be *present*
  rather than the magnetic flux through a loop to be *changing* — a
  stationary magnet next to a stationary loop induces nothing.
