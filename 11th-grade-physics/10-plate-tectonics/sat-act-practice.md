# Unit 10: Plate Tectonics — SAT/ACT Practice

Plate tectonics has no formulas, so it doesn't show up in **SAT Math** at
all. It shows up in two other places:

- **SAT Reading & Writing** — as a science passage testing whether you can
  draw the correct inference/conclusion from a short text about evidence
  (e.g., magnetic striping, fossil distribution).
- **ACT Science** — as data/diagram interpretation, usually a map of
  seafloor age or a table of measured plate motion rates.

## SAT Reading & Writing Style

**Q1.** A passage describes how magnetic striping in oceanic crust is
symmetric on either side of mid-ocean ridges, and that crust age increases
with distance from the ridge. Which conclusion is *best supported* by this
passage?

A) Continents have always been in their current positions
B) New oceanic crust forms continuously at ridges and spreads outward over time
C) Earthquakes only occur at mid-ocean ridges
D) Magnetic striping proves the mantle is solid

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**B.**

This is a direct "which claim does the evidence support" reading question
— the described evidence (symmetric striping + age-distance relationship)
specifically supports ongoing crust formation and outward spreading, not
the other, unsupported claims in A, C, or D.
</details>

## ACT Science Reasoning Style

**Q2.** A table shows GPS-measured plate motion rates at three boundaries:

| Boundary | Type | Motion rate (cm/year) |
|---|---|---|
| A | Divergent | 5 |
| B | Convergent | 3 |
| C | Transform | 4 |

Based only on this table, can you conclude that divergent boundaries always
move faster than convergent boundaries?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**No — only one boundary of each type was measured, so this single comparison isn't enough to support a general claim about "always."**

This is the classic ACT Science overgeneralization trap: a true
observation about *this specific data* (5 > 3) doesn't automatically
support a universal claim about all divergent vs. all convergent
boundaries everywhere.
</details>

---

**Q3.** A cross-section diagram shows an oceanic plate sinking beneath a
continental plate, with a deep trench and a chain of volcanoes on the
continental side. What type of boundary does this diagram show?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**A convergent (subduction) boundary.**

The combination of a trench (where the oceanic plate bends downward) and a
volcanic mountain chain (from melted material rising on the continental
side) is the classic diagram signature of oceanic-continental convergence
— a very common ACT Science "identify the boundary from the diagram"
question.
</details>

---

**Q4.** Using the seafloor-age map convention (crust gets older with
distance from the ridge), a student is shown a cross-section where crust
age increases *symmetrically* moving away from a central ridge in both
directions. What does this pattern indicate about the ridge's spreading
rate on each side?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**The ridge is spreading at roughly equal rates on both sides (symmetric spreading).**

If one side spread faster, the age-vs-distance pattern would be
asymmetric (older crust closer to the ridge on the slower side). Symmetric
age patterns are strong, specific evidence for symmetric spreading rates —
a subtler data-reading skill than just recognizing spreading happens at all.
</details>

---

## Answer Key Summary

1. B  2. No — insufficient data to generalize  3. Convergent (subduction) boundary
4. Roughly equal spreading rates on both sides
