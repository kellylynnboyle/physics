# Unit 10: Plate Tectonics — Interactive Quiz (Most-Missed Questions)

Take this quiz without notes. 8/10 (80%) to advance to Unit 11.

---

**Q1.** According to plate tectonics theory, how do continents move?

A) They drift independently through the fixed ocean floor, like ships through water
B) They move as part of a rigid plate that includes the surrounding ocean floor
C) They don't actually move; only the ocean floor moves
D) They move only during earthquakes

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**B.**

This is the #1 most-missed idea in the unit — a common oversimplification
of Wegener's original "continental drift" makes it sound like continents
plow through a stationary seafloor. In modern plate tectonics theory, the
entire plate (continental crust + attached oceanic crust) moves together
as one rigid piece.
</details>

---

**Q2.** At a divergent boundary, what typically happens?

A) Plates collide, forming mountains
B) Plates slide past each other horizontally
C) Plates move apart, and new crust forms to fill the gap
D) One plate sinks beneath the other

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C.**

Divergent boundaries (like mid-ocean ridges) pull apart, and magma rises
to fill the gap, creating new oceanic crust — this is seafloor spreading.
Students often mix this up with convergent boundary features (mountains,
subduction).
</details>

---

**Q3.** At a convergent boundary where oceanic crust meets continental
crust, which one subducts (sinks beneath the other), and why?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**The oceanic crust subducts, because it is denser than continental crust.**

Oceanic crust is thinner and denser (basaltic); continental crust is
thicker and less dense (granitic) — density, not size, determines which
one sinks. This is commonly missed because students assume the "bigger" or
"thicker" plate would sink, when it's actually the denser one.
</details>

---

**Q4.** The San Andreas Fault in California is an example of what type of
plate boundary?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Transform boundary.**

Plates slide horizontally past each other with no new crust created or
destroyed — this produces frequent earthquakes but not typically volcanoes
or mountain-building, distinguishing it from convergent and divergent
boundaries.
</details>

---

**Q5.** True or False: Earthquakes and volcanoes are randomly scattered
around the globe.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**False.**

They're heavily concentrated along plate boundaries, especially the
"Ring of Fire" around the Pacific Ocean. This connects directly to Unit
11 (Earthquakes) — boundary location predicts where seismic and volcanic
activity clusters.
</details>

---

**Q6.** What is the primary evidence that mid-ocean ridges are sites of new
crust formation?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Oceanic crust gets progressively OLDER the farther you travel from a mid-ocean ridge, and magnetic striping in the rock is symmetric on both sides of the ridge — both patterns are consistent with new crust continuously forming at the ridge and spreading outward over time.**

This is one of the most commonly missed "evidence" questions — students
often list continental drift observations (matching coastlines/fossils)
without connecting the *specific* seafloor-spreading evidence to *this*
specific claim about ridges.
</details>

---

**Q7.** What layer of Earth do tectonic plates actually move across?

A) The inner core
B) The outer core
C) The asthenosphere
D) The crust itself

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C — the asthenosphere.**

The rigid lithosphere (which includes the crust) sits on top of and moves
across the ductile, partially molten asthenosphere below it. A common
error is confusing the asthenosphere (which plates move *over*) with the
lithosphere (which *is* the plate).
</details>

---

**Q8.** What is currently understood to be the primary driving mechanism
behind plate motion?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Mantle convection** (heat-driven circulation in the mantle), often
combined with additional forces like slab pull (a sinking plate pulling
the rest along) and ridge push (new crust pushing plates apart at ridges).

Simply saying "gravity" or "the plates just move" misses the actual
driving mechanism — this is a frequently tested specific vocabulary term.
</details>

---

**Q9.** What was Pangaea?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**A supercontinent that existed roughly 300 million years ago, before tectonic plate motion broke it apart into the continents we recognize today.**

Matching coastlines, fossil distributions, and rock formations across
now-separated continents are key pieces of evidence for its former
existence.
</details>

---

**Q10.** Which type of plate boundary is most directly responsible for
building the Himalayan mountain range (formed by the Indian and Eurasian
plates colliding)?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Convergent boundary** (specifically, continent-continent collision — neither plate is dense enough to subduct, so the crust crumples upward into mountains instead).

This is a useful special case: not all convergent boundaries produce
subduction — continent-continent convergence (both relatively low
density) produces mountain-building instead.
</details>

---

## Answer Key Summary

1. B  2. C  3. Oceanic crust (denser)  4. Transform  5. False
6. Age increases with distance from ridge + symmetric magnetic striping
7. C  8. Mantle convection  9. Ancient supercontinent (~300 Mya)
10. Convergent (continent-continent collision)
