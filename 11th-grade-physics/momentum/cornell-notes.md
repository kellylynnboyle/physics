# Cornell Notes: Momentum and Collisions

**Topic:** Momentum, Impulse, and Collisions | **Course:** 11th Grade Physics

---

## Notes

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is momentum? | Momentum (**p**) is a vector quantity describing "mass in motion." **p = mv**, where m = mass (kg), v = velocity (m/s). Units: **kg·m/s**. Direction of p is the same as direction of v. |
| Why does direction matter for momentum? | Because momentum is a **vector**, not a scalar. An object moving in the negative direction has negative momentum. When combining momenta of multiple objects, you must add them like vectors (with signs in 1D, components in 2D) — you cannot just add magnitudes. |
| What is impulse? | Impulse (**J**) is the product of the net force applied to an object and the time interval over which it acts: **J = FΔt**. Units: **N·s**, which is equivalent to kg·m/s. Impulse is also a vector, in the direction of the net force. |
| What is the impulse-momentum theorem? | **J = Δp**, i.e., **FΔt = mΔv = m(v_f − v_i)**. The impulse delivered to an object equals its change in momentum. This connects force and time directly to changes in motion, and is the reasoning behind safety devices like airbags and crumple zones. |
| Why do airbags and crumple zones "work" physically? | Δp (the change in momentum needed to stop a person or car) is fixed by the mass and change in velocity — it doesn't change. But since J = FΔt = Δp, if you **increase Δt** (spread the stop over more time), the **average force F must decrease** for the same Δp. Airbags and crumple zones increase collision time, lowering peak force on occupants. |
| What is the law of conservation of momentum? | In an **isolated system** (no net external force), the **total momentum before** an interaction equals the **total momentum after**: <br> **m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂'** (two-object system, primes = "after") |
| What counts as an "isolated system"? Why does it matter? | A system is isolated when the *net external force* on it is zero (internal forces between objects in the system, like the force of one car hitting another, don't count — only forces from *outside* the system, like friction from the road or an outside push, count). **You must include every object that exerts or receives a significant force during the interaction** — leaving one out, or having an unaccounted external force like friction or gravity acting during the collision, breaks the conservation equation. |
| Is momentum conserved in EVERY collision? | **Yes** — momentum is conserved in **every** collision in an isolated system, whether elastic, inelastic, or perfectly inelastic. This is the single most commonly missed idea in this unit. |
| Is kinetic energy conserved in every collision? | **No.** Kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²) is conserved **only in elastic collisions**. In inelastic and perfectly inelastic collisions, some KE converts to heat, sound, and deformation — momentum is still conserved, but KE is not. |
| momentum vs. kinetic energy — what's the difference? | Momentum: **p = mv** (vector, always conserved in isolated systems). Kinetic energy: **KE = ½mv²** (scalar, only conserved in elastic collisions). Same variables (m, v) but different formulas, different units (kg·m/s vs. Joules), and different conservation rules — do not mix them up! |
| What is an elastic collision? | A collision in which **both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved**. Objects bounce apart without any permanent deformation or heat loss (e.g., idealized billiard balls, gas molecules). |
| What is an inelastic collision? | A collision in which **momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is NOT** — some KE is lost to heat, sound, or deformation. Objects may or may not stick together. |
| What is a perfectly inelastic collision? | The extreme case of an inelastic collision where the **objects stick together** and move with one **common final velocity** after collision. Maximum KE is lost (though not necessarily all of it). |
| How do you set up a perfectly inelastic collision problem? | 1. Write conservation of momentum: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v_f <br> 2. Note that the objects move together afterward, so there is only **one** final velocity, and the final mass is the **combined mass** (m₁ + m₂). <br> 3. Solve algebraically for v_f. <br> 4. (Optional check) Compute KE before and after to confirm KE decreased — confirming it was inelastic. |
| What is an explosion / recoil problem? | An "explosion" is a collision in reverse: objects start together (often at rest) and fly apart. If the system starts at rest, **total momentum before = 0**, so **total momentum after must also = 0**. The objects fly apart with momenta of equal magnitude but opposite direction. |
| Is velocity conserved? | **No!** This is a common misconception. Only **total momentum** of an isolated system is conserved — individual velocities change constantly during collisions. Never say "conservation of velocity" — it isn't a real principle. |
| What is center of mass motion? | The center of mass of an isolated system moves at **constant velocity** before, during, and after any internal collision or explosion — because internal forces (between objects in the system) cannot change the system's total momentum, and hence cannot change the velocity of its center of mass. |
| Worked Example — Perfectly Inelastic Collision | **Problem:** A 1500 kg car moving at +8.0 m/s collides head-on and locks together with a 1000 kg car moving at −6.0 m/s. Find the common final velocity, and determine whether the collision is elastic. <br><br> **Step 1 — Conservation of momentum:** <br> m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁+m₂)v_f <br> (1500)(8.0) + (1000)(−6.0) = (2500)v_f <br> 12000 − 6000 = 2500·v_f <br> 6000 = 2500·v_f <br> **v_f = +2.4 m/s** (direction of the first car's original motion) <br><br> **Step 2 — Check kinetic energy (was it elastic?):** <br> KE_before = ½(1500)(8.0)² + ½(1000)(6.0)² = 48000 J + 18000 J = 66000 J <br> KE_after = ½(2500)(2.4)² = ½(2500)(5.76) = 7200 J <br> Since KE_after (7200 J) ≪ KE_before (66000 J), a large amount of KE was lost (~58800 J, converted to heat/sound/deformation). **This confirms the collision is perfectly inelastic — momentum was conserved (we used it to solve), but KE was clearly not conserved.** |

## Summary

Momentum (p = mv) is a vector quantity that is **always** conserved in an isolated system, regardless of whether a collision is elastic, inelastic, or perfectly inelastic — this is the single most important and most commonly confused idea in this unit. Kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²), in contrast, is conserved **only** in elastic collisions; any inelastic collision loses some KE to heat, sound, or deformation even while momentum remains exactly conserved. The impulse-momentum theorem (J = FΔt = Δp) links force and time to a change in momentum, explaining why extending collision time (airbags, crumple zones, padding) reduces the force experienced for the same change in momentum. Perfectly inelastic collisions are solved by treating the colliding objects as combining into one final mass moving at one common final velocity, while explosions/recoil problems rely on the fact that a system starting at rest has zero total momentum both before and after. Throughout, remember that momentum problems require careful attention to sign and direction (velocity is a vector, so is momentum) and that velocity itself is never conserved — only total system momentum is.
