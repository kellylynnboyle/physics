# Newton's Laws of Motion

## Overview

Newton's three laws of motion are the foundation of classical mechanics — they explain why objects at rest stay at rest, why a pushed object accelerates in proportion to the force applied, and why every push has a push back. Mastering these laws lets you predict and explain the motion of everything from a dropped ball to a car rounding a curve to a rocket leaving the launch pad. This topic also introduces the free body diagram, the single most important problem-solving tool used throughout the rest of mechanics, electromagnetism, and engineering.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Newton's 1st Law (Law of Inertia)** — an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force. Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its motion.
- **Mass vs. weight** — mass (kg) is the amount of matter in an object and does not change with location; weight (N) is the gravitational force on that mass (*W* = *mg*) and changes depending on the local gravitational field.
- **Newton's 2nd Law and net force** — the net (unbalanced) force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration: **ΣF = ma**. Acceleration is caused by and points in the same direction as the net force.
- **Free body diagrams (FBDs)** — a diagram showing only the object of interest and every force acting *on* it, drawn as arrows from the object's center, used to sum forces along convenient axes before applying F = ma.
- **Newton's 3rd Law and action-reaction pairs** — for every force one object exerts on a second object, the second object exerts an equal and opposite force back on the first. The two forces in a pair always act on **different objects**, are the same type of force, and never cancel each other out for either object individually.
- **Types of forces** — gravity (weight), normal force (surface pushing perpendicular to itself), tension (pulling force through a string/rope/cable), friction — static (resists the start of sliding, up to a maximum) and kinetic (resists sliding already in progress), and applied force (a push or pull from an external agent).
- **Equilibrium** — net force equal to zero means the forces are *balanced*, not that no forces exist and not that the object is motionless. An object in equilibrium is either at rest or moving at constant velocity (zero acceleration).
- **Friction coefficients and the friction force equation** — friction force is modeled as *f* = μ*N*, where μ is the (unitless) coefficient of friction and *N* is the normal force; μs (static) is generally greater than or equal to μk (kinetic) for the same surfaces.
- **Apparent weight** — the reading on a scale reflects the normal force on the person, not their true gravitational weight; this reading increases when accelerating upward and decreases when accelerating downward (elevators, scales, roller coasters).
- **Inclined plane force decomposition** — on a frictionless or frictional incline, gravity is broken into a component parallel to the surface (*mg* sin θ, causing acceleration down the slope) and a component perpendicular to the surface (*mg* cos θ, balanced by the normal force).

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

1. State Newton's three laws of motion precisely and explain each in everyday language.
2. Distinguish between mass and weight, including correctly converting between them using *W* = *mg* on Earth and other worlds.
3. Draw a correct, properly labeled free body diagram for an object in a variety of physical situations (level surface, incline, elevator, hanging from ropes).
4. Apply Newton's second law (ΣF = ma) to solve for unknown forces, masses, or accelerations in one and two dimensions.
5. Identify true Newton's third law action-reaction pairs and distinguish them from equilibrium force pairs acting on a single object.
6. Calculate friction forces using f = μN and determine whether an object remains static or begins to slide.
7. Decompose weight into components parallel and perpendicular to an inclined surface and solve for acceleration on the incline.
8. Analyze apparent weight in accelerating reference frames such as elevators and explain the physical reason the scale reading changes.

## Real-World Applications

- **Seatbelts and airbags** — rely on the 1st law: your body continues moving forward when a car suddenly stops, so restraints supply the force needed to decelerate you with the car.
- **Rocket propulsion** — a rocket expels exhaust gas downward/backward, and by the 3rd law the gas pushes the rocket upward/forward with equal force, even in the vacuum of space.
- **Walking** — your foot pushes backward on the ground (action), and the ground pushes forward on your foot (reaction), which is the force that actually propels you forward.
- **Traction and car tires** — static friction between tire and road allows acceleration, braking, and turning; understanding μs and μk explains why anti-lock brakes and proper tire tread matter, especially in rain or ice.
- **Elevators and apparent weight** — the changing scale reading felt in a fast elevator is a direct demonstration of apparent weight during acceleration, not a change in true gravitational mass.
- **Tug-of-war** — a clear illustration that the 3rd law pair (each team pulling on the rope) never determines the winner; the winner is decided by which team can generate more friction/traction against the ground.

## Practice Problems

**1. (Basic)** A 5.0 kg object sits on a table. What is its weight in newtons? (Use g = 9.8 m/s².)

**2. (Basic)** A net force of 12 N is applied to a 3.0 kg cart. What is its acceleration?

**3. (Basic — Conceptual)** A book rests on a table, motionless. Identify all forces acting on the book, and state whether the book is in equilibrium.

**4. (Intermediate)** A 60 kg skater is pushed by a friend with a force of 90 N on frictionless ice. What is the skater's acceleration? If the push lasts 2.0 s, what is the skater's final speed, starting from rest?

**5. (Intermediate — Conceptual, Action-Reaction Pair)** A hammer strikes a nail, driving it into a piece of wood. Identify the Newton's 3rd law action-reaction pair in this scenario, being sure to name the two objects and the two forces involved. Then explain one pair of forces in this same scenario that is *not* a 3rd law pair, even though the forces are equal and opposite.

**6. (Intermediate)** A crate weighing 200 N sits on a horizontal floor. The coefficient of static friction between the crate and floor is μs = 0.40, and the coefficient of kinetic friction is μk = 0.30. What is the maximum static friction force before the crate starts to slide? If a horizontal force of 90 N is applied, does the crate move? If so, what is the kinetic friction force once it is sliding?

**7. (Challenging — Free Body Diagram / Incline)** A 10 kg block sits on a frictionless ramp inclined at 30° above the horizontal. Draw the free body diagram (describe it in words: forces present and their directions relative to the incline). Then find the block's acceleration down the ramp and the normal force on the block.

**8. (Challenging — Apparent Weight)** A 70 kg person stands on a bathroom scale inside an elevator. Find the scale reading (in newtons and in the equivalent "apparent mass" in kg) when the elevator: (a) is at rest, (b) accelerates upward at 2.0 m/s², (c) accelerates downward at 2.0 m/s².

---

### Answer Key

**1.** W = mg = (5.0 kg)(9.8 m/s²) = **49 N**

**2.** a = F/m = 12 N / 3.0 kg = **4.0 m/s²**

**3.** Forces: gravity (weight, downward) and the normal force from the table (upward). Since the book is motionless (a = 0), the net force is zero — these two forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so the book **is in equilibrium**. (Note: gravity and the normal force here act on the *same* object, the book, so they are an equilibrium pair, not a 3rd law pair.)

**4.** a = F/m = 90 N / 60 kg = 1.5 m/s². Final speed: v = at = (1.5 m/s²)(2.0 s) = **3.0 m/s**; acceleration = **1.5 m/s²**.

**5.** 3rd law pair: the hammer exerts a force on the nail (action), and the nail exerts an equal and opposite force back on the hammer (reaction) — this is what decelerates the hammer on impact. These two forces act on two different objects (hammer and nail), are the same type of force (contact/normal force), and occur simultaneously. A pair that looks similar but is **not** a 3rd law pair: the force of gravity on the hammer and the normal/support force of the person's hand on the hammer while swinging it — these act on the *same* object (the hammer) and are an equilibrium-type pair, not action-reaction.

**6.** Maximum static friction: f_s,max = μs·N = (0.40)(200 N) = 80 N. Since the applied force (90 N) exceeds 80 N, **the crate starts to move**. Once sliding, kinetic friction = μk·N = (0.30)(200 N) = **60 N**.

**7.** FBD description: three forces act on the block — weight (mg, straight down), normal force (N, perpendicular to and away from the incline surface), and (since frictionless) no friction force. Decomposing weight along axes parallel/perpendicular to the incline: parallel component = mg sin(30°) drives the block down the slope; perpendicular component = mg cos(30°) is balanced by the normal force.
 a = g sin(30°) = (9.8 m/s²)(0.5) = **4.9 m/s² down the incline**.
 N = mg cos(30°) = (10 kg)(9.8 m/s²)(0.866) ≈ **84.9 N**.

**8.** True weight = mg = (70 kg)(9.8 m/s²) = 686 N.
 (a) At rest (a = 0): scale reads N = mg = **686 N** (apparent mass 70 kg).
 (b) Accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s²: N = m(g + a) = 70(9.8 + 2.0) = **826 N** (apparent mass ≈ 84.3 kg) — feels heavier.
 (c) Accelerating downward at 2.0 m/s²: N = m(g − a) = 70(9.8 − 2.0) = **546 N** (apparent mass ≈ 55.7 kg) — feels lighter.
