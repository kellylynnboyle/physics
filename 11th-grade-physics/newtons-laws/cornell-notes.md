# Cornell Notes: Newton's Laws of Motion

**Topic:** Newton's Three Laws of Motion, Forces, and Free Body Diagrams
**Course:** 11th Grade Physics

---

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is Newton's 1st Law? | **Newton's First Law (Law of Inertia):** An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion remains in motion at constant velocity (constant speed, straight line), unless acted on by a net external force. In other words, if ΣF = 0, then acceleration a = 0. |
| What is inertia? | **Inertia** is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of motion. Inertia is *not* a force — it's a property of matter. The amount of inertia an object has is measured by its **mass**. |
| Why doesn't a book sliding on a table go forever? | Friction (an external, unbalanced force) acts on the book, decelerating it. If friction were removed entirely, the 1st law says the book would slide at constant velocity forever. |
| What is Newton's 2nd Law? | **Newton's Second Law:** The net (unbalanced) force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. **ΣF = ma.** Acceleration is directly proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass, and points in the same direction as the net force. |
| What are the units in F = ma? | Force in **newtons (N)**, mass in **kilograms (kg)**, acceleration in **meters per second squared (m/s²)**. 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². |
| What is "net force"? | The **vector sum** of all forces acting on an object. Individual forces can be large, but if they cancel, the net force — and therefore the acceleration — is zero. |
| What is Newton's 3rd Law? | **Newton's Third Law:** For every action force one object exerts on a second object, the second object exerts a force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction back on the first object. Forces always come in pairs. |
| Key rule for 3rd law pairs? | The two forces in a pair act on **two different objects**, are the **same type** of force, and occur **simultaneously**. Because they act on different objects, they **never cancel** — each force still affects the object it acts on. |
| ⚠ Common mistake #1 | Thinking gravity on a book and the normal force holding it up are a 3rd law pair. **FALSE** — both forces act on the *same* object (the book). That's an **equilibrium pair**, not an action-reaction pair. |
| ⚠ Common mistake #2 | Believing 3rd law pairs cancel out and "nothing happens." **FALSE** — the two forces act on *different* objects, so each object still experiences its own force and can accelerate. |
| How to identify a *true* 3rd law pair | Ask: (1) What are the two forces? (2) What two *different* objects are they acting on? (3) Are they the same type of force (both contact, both gravity, etc.)? If yes to all three, it's a 3rd law pair. |
| How to identify an equilibrium pair (NOT 3rd law) | Ask: Do both forces act on the *same single* object, and do they happen to be equal/opposite because that object isn't accelerating? If yes, it's an equilibrium condition, not a 3rd law pair. |
| What is a free body diagram (FBD)? | A diagram showing **one object** as a dot/box, with arrows representing every force acting **on** that object (not forces it exerts on other things). Arrow length ≈ relative force magnitude; arrow direction = force direction. |
| Steps to draw an FBD | 1. Isolate the object of interest. 2. Identify every force touching/acting on it (gravity, normal, tension, friction, applied). 3. Draw each as an arrow from the center, correctly directed. 4. Choose convenient x/y axes (align with incline if needed). 5. Sum forces along each axis; set equal to ma. |
| What is weight? | **Weight = mg** — the gravitational force on an object's mass. Weight is a *force*, measured in newtons, and depends on local gravity (g ≈ 9.8 m/s² on Earth). |
| Mass vs. weight — key distinction | **Mass (kg):** amount of matter; constant everywhere. **Weight (N):** force of gravity on that mass; changes with location (e.g., weight on the Moon is about 1/6 of weight on Earth, but mass is unchanged). |
| What is the normal force? | The support force a surface exerts **perpendicular** to itself, preventing objects from passing through it. On a flat, horizontal surface with no vertical forces other than gravity: N = mg. This changes on an incline or in an accelerating frame. |
| What is tension? | The pulling force transmitted through a string, rope, cable, or chain, directed **along** the rope, away from the object, at both ends. |
| Friction formula? | **f = μN**, where μ is the coefficient of friction (unitless) and N is the normal force. **Static friction** (μs) resists the *start* of sliding and adjusts up to a maximum value (f_s,max = μs·N) — it matches the applied force until that max is reached. **Kinetic friction** (μk) applies once the object is already sliding, and is roughly constant: f_k = μk·N. Generally μs ≥ μk. |
| ⚠ Common mistake #3 | Forgetting that static friction has a **maximum**. Below that max, static friction exactly balances the applied force (object stays still). Only once applied force *exceeds* μs·N does the object start moving — then switch to kinetic friction, μk·N. |
| Which direction does friction point? | Friction always opposes **relative motion** (kinetic) or **attempted/impending relative motion** (static) between two surfaces — never in the direction of motion. |
| What is apparent weight? | The force a scale reads, which equals the **normal force** on the person, not their true gravitational weight. In an accelerating elevator, apparent weight ≠ mg. |
| Apparent weight formula (elevator) | Taking up as positive: **N = m(g + a)**, where *a* is the elevator's acceleration (positive if accelerating upward, negative if accelerating downward). |
| ⚠ Common mistake #4 | Assuming you feel heavier when moving up and lighter when moving down at *constant velocity*. **FALSE** — apparent weight only changes during **acceleration**, not during constant-velocity travel (where a = 0 and N = mg as usual). |
| How to decompose weight on an incline | Set x-axis parallel to the incline surface, y-axis perpendicular to it. Then: **parallel component = mg sin θ** (drives the object down the slope), **perpendicular component = mg cos θ** (balanced by the normal force, N = mg cos θ, if no other perpendicular forces). |
| ⚠ Common mistake #5 | Swapping sin θ and cos θ on an incline. **Fix:** the component *along* the slope (causing sliding) uses **sin θ**; the component *into* the slope (balanced by N) uses **cos θ**. Check with θ = 0° (flat ground): sin(0°)=0 (no sliding component, correct) and cos(0°)=1 (full weight on N, correct). |
| ⚠ Common mistake #6 | Thinking heavier objects fall faster in free fall. In the *absence of air resistance*, all objects accelerate downward at the same rate g, regardless of mass — because although gravity's force (mg) is larger for heavier objects, so is their inertia (m), and the two effects cancel in a = F/m = mg/m = g. |
| ⚠ Common mistake #7 | Thinking a moving object needs a continuous net force just to "keep going." **FALSE per 1st law** — constant velocity requires **zero** net force. A net force is only needed to *change* velocity (speed up, slow down, or turn). |

## Worked Example (Numeric, with Free Body Diagram Description)

**Problem:** A 10 kg block slides down a frictionless ramp inclined at 30° above horizontal. Find (a) the block's acceleration and (b) the normal force on the block.

**Free Body Diagram description:** Draw the block as a small box on the incline. Two forces act on it:
1. **Weight (mg)**, drawn straight down (toward the center of the Earth), magnitude = (10 kg)(9.8 m/s²) = 98 N.
2. **Normal force (N)**, drawn perpendicular to the ramp's surface, pointing away from the ramp.

There is no friction (frictionless ramp), so no friction arrow is drawn.

**Set up rotated axes:** x-axis parallel to the ramp surface (positive pointing down-slope), y-axis perpendicular to the ramp surface.

**Decompose weight:**
- x-component (along ramp): mg sin(30°) = 98 × 0.5 = 49 N (down the slope)
- y-component (into ramp): mg cos(30°) = 98 × 0.866 ≈ 84.9 N (into the surface)

**Apply Newton's 2nd Law along each axis:**

*x-axis (along incline):* ΣFx = max → mg sin(30°) = ma → a = g sin(30°) = 9.8 × 0.5 = **4.9 m/s², directed down the ramp**.

*y-axis (perpendicular to incline):* ΣFy = 0 (no acceleration perpendicular to the surface) → N − mg cos(30°) = 0 → **N = mg cos(30°) ≈ 84.9 N**.

**Check:** Units are consistent (N in newtons, a in m/s²). At θ = 0° this reduces to a = 0 and N = mg, matching flat-ground intuition. At θ = 90° (vertical drop) this reduces to a = g and N = 0, matching free fall — both sanity checks pass.

## Summary

Newton's three laws describe how forces govern motion: the **1st law** says objects resist changes in motion unless a net force acts (inertia); the **2nd law** quantifies that relationship as ΣF = ma; and the **3rd law** says forces always occur in equal-and-opposite pairs acting on *two different objects*, which is why those forces never cancel for either object individually. The most common student errors are confusing mass with weight, believing constant-velocity motion requires an ongoing force, mistaking equilibrium pairs (same object) for 3rd law pairs (different objects), forgetting friction opposes relative or attempted motion and that static friction has a maximum (μs·N) before kinetic friction (μk·N) takes over, assuming heavier objects fall faster (they don't, absent air resistance, since a = g regardless of m), mixing up sin θ and cos θ when decomposing weight on an incline, and misjudging that apparent weight only changes during acceleration (not during constant-velocity elevator travel). Free body diagrams — isolating one object and drawing every force acting on it — are the essential tool for correctly applying all three laws to real problems.
