# Unit 8: Waves — Interactive Quiz (Most-Missed Questions)

Take this quiz without notes. 8/10 (80%) to advance to Unit 9.

---

**Q1.** A cork floats on the surface of a pond as a water wave passes
underneath it. What does the cork do?

A) Travels along with the wave toward the shore
B) Bobs up and down in place, staying roughly in the same spot
C) Sinks
D) Moves backward, away from the wave source

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**B.**

This is the #1 most-missed idea in the whole unit: waves transport energy,
not matter. The cork oscillates up and down around a fixed position as the
wave's energy passes through the water beneath it — it doesn't travel
along with the wave.
</details>

---

**Q2.** A sound wave travels from air into water. What happens to its frequency?

A) It increases
B) It decreases
C) It stays exactly the same
D) It depends on the wave's amplitude

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C — stays the same.**

Frequency is set by the source (whatever is vibrating) and doesn't change
just because the wave enters a new medium. What *does* change: speed and
wavelength — sound actually travels faster in water than air, so since
v=fλ and f stays fixed, wavelength must increase to compensate.
</details>

---

**Q3.** A wave has a frequency of 5 Hz and a wavelength of 2 m. What is its speed?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**10 m/s.**

v = fλ = 5×2 = 10 m/s. Direct application — included as a baseline before
the trickier "what changes when..." conceptual questions.
</details>

---

**Q4.** If you double the frequency of a wave source while it stays in the
same medium, what happens to the wavelength?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**It's cut in half.**

Since v=fλ and v is fixed by the medium, f and λ are inversely related — if
f doubles, λ must halve to keep v constant. The most common error: assuming
wavelength stays the same or also doubles, forgetting that speed is the
one thing held constant by the medium, not frequency or wavelength.
</details>

---

**Q5.** Sound is what type of wave? Light?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Sound is longitudinal (particles vibrate parallel to the direction of travel — compressions and rarefactions). Light is transverse (the electric/magnetic field oscillates perpendicular to the direction of travel).**

A commonly missed pairing — students sometimes assume all waves are the
same type, or mix up which one is which.
</details>

---

**Q6.** Two waves of equal amplitude meet such that a crest lines up exactly
with a trough. What happens at that point?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Destructive interference — the displacements cancel, momentarily producing zero (or reduced) amplitude at that point.**

After the waves pass through each other, each continues on unaffected —
the cancellation is temporary and specific to the overlap region, which is
a commonly missed distinction from how particles behave in a collision.
</details>

---

**Q7.** A swing is pushed at exactly the same rate as its natural
back-and-forth frequency, and its amplitude grows larger and larger with
each push. What phenomenon is this?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Resonance.**

Driving a system at its natural frequency causes energy to build up
efficiently, producing large amplitude oscillations — the same principle
behind shattering a glass with sound, or (famously) the Tacoma Narrows
Bridge collapse from wind-driven resonance.
</details>

---

**Q8.** True or False: A wave with a larger amplitude travels faster than
one with a smaller amplitude, in the same medium.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**False.**

Wave speed is determined by the properties of the medium, not the wave's
amplitude. Amplitude relates to the wave's energy/intensity (a louder sound,
a brighter light) — not how fast it travels. This is one of the most
persistent misconceptions in the unit.
</details>

---

**Q9.** A guitar string vibrates and forms a standing wave with two fixed
ends and one point in the middle that doesn't move at all. What is that
stationary middle point called?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**A node.**

Standing waves have nodes (points of zero displacement) and antinodes
(points of maximum displacement), formed by the interference of waves
traveling in opposite directions along the string (the original wave and
its reflection).
</details>

---

**Q10.** A wave's period is 0.25 seconds. What is its frequency?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**4 Hz.**

f = 1/T = 1/0.25 = 4 Hz. Direct application of the inverse relationship
between period and frequency — a common error is confusing which one is
the reciprocal of the other (dividing 0.25 by 1 instead of 1 by 0.25).
</details>
