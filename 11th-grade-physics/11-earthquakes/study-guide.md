# Earthquakes — Quick-Reference Study Guide

## Definitions

- **Focus (hypocenter)** — underground origin point of the earthquake
- **Epicenter** — surface point directly above the focus
- **P-wave** — fastest, longitudinal, travels through solids AND liquids
- **S-wave** — slower, transverse, travels through solids ONLY
- **Magnitude** — energy released at the source; one fixed value per quake; logarithmic scale
- **Intensity** — felt shaking/damage at a specific location; varies by distance, geology, construction

## Quick-Reference Table

| Concept | Key fact |
|---|---|
| Magnitude vs. intensity | One earthquake = one magnitude, many intensities |
| Magnitude scale | Logarithmic — each +1 ≈ 32× more energy |
| P-waves vs. S-waves | P arrives first, travels through solids+liquids; S is slower, solids only |
| Locating an epicenter | Requires 3+ stations' distance data (triangulation) |
| Normal fault | Tension, hanging wall moves down — divergent boundaries |
| Reverse fault | Compression, hanging wall moves up — convergent boundaries |
| Strike-slip fault | Horizontal sliding — transform boundaries |

## Practice Problems (conceptual)

1. Two earthquakes differ by 3 magnitude units. Roughly how many times more energy does the larger one release? (32³ ≈ 32,000)
2. Which wave arrives first at a seismograph: P, S, or surface waves?
3. A fault shows the hanging wall moving up. Is it normal or reverse?
4. What minimum number of seismograph stations is needed to pinpoint an epicenter?
5. Which wave type creates a "shadow zone" on the far side of Earth, and why?
6. Is a magnitude 5 earthquake always felt the same way in every nearby town? Why or why not?

<details><summary>Show Answers</summary>

1. ≈32,000 times more energy
2. P-waves
3. Reverse
4. Three
5. S-waves — they can't travel through Earth's liquid outer core
6. No — intensity (felt shaking) varies by distance, local soil/geology, and building construction, even though magnitude is fixed
</details>
