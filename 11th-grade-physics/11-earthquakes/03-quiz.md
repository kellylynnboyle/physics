# Unit 11: Earthquakes — Interactive Quiz (Most-Missed Questions)

Take this quiz without notes. 8/10 (80%) to advance to Unit 12.

---

**Q1.** How many magnitudes does a single earthquake have, and how many
intensities?

A) One magnitude, one intensity
B) One magnitude, many intensities (varying by location)
C) Many magnitudes, one intensity
D) Many magnitudes, many intensities

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**B.**

This is the #1 most-missed idea in the unit: magnitude is a single,
fixed value describing energy released at the source. Intensity describes
the *felt* shaking/damage at a specific location and varies — usually
decreasing with distance from the epicenter, and also depending on local
geology and construction.
</details>

---

**Q2.** An earthquake measures magnitude 7 on the moment magnitude scale.
How does its energy release compare to a magnitude 5 earthquake?

A) About twice as much energy
B) About 100 times as much energy
C) About 1,000 times as much energy
D) About 30 times as much energy

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C — roughly 1,000 times as much energy** (each whole-number step is
about 32× more energy, so two steps ≈ 32×32 ≈ 1,000×).

The magnitude scale is logarithmic, not linear — this is one of the most
persistently missed facts in earth science. Students very often assume
magnitude 7 vs. 5 means "7/5 times stronger" or "twice as strong."
</details>

---

**Q3.** Which seismic wave type arrives first at a seismograph station?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**P-waves (primary waves)** — they're the fastest, traveling through both
solids and liquids.
</details>

---

**Q4.** Which seismic wave type cannot travel through Earth's liquid outer core?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**S-waves (secondary waves)** — they're transverse waves and can only
travel through solids. This created a "shadow zone" on the far side of
Earth where S-waves don't arrive, which is actually how scientists first
determined the outer core is liquid.
</details>

---

**Q5.** What's the difference between the focus and the epicenter of an earthquake?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**The focus (hypocenter) is the underground point where the earthquake actually originates; the epicenter is the point on Earth's surface directly above the focus.**

A commonly missed mix-up — the epicenter is what's usually reported in the
news, but it's not the actual origin point, just the surface projection of it.
</details>

---

**Q6.** Three seismograph stations each calculate their distance to an
earthquake's epicenter using the P-wave/S-wave arrival time difference.
How do scientists use this to pinpoint the actual epicenter location?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**By drawing a circle around each station with a radius equal to that station's calculated distance — the epicenter is located where all three circles intersect (triangulation).**

A single station only tells you a distance (a circle of possible
locations), not a direction — you need at least three stations to narrow
it down to one point.
</details>

---

**Q7.** A fault shows the hanging wall moving DOWN relative to the footwall.
What type of fault is this, and what kind of stress typically causes it?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Normal fault — caused by tension (pulling apart), typically at divergent boundaries.**

Contrast with a reverse fault (hanging wall moves UP, caused by
compression at convergent boundaries) — the direction of relative motion
is the key distinguishing detail most often mixed up.
</details>

---

**Q8.** True or False: A city 500 km from an earthquake's epicenter always
experiences less intense shaking than a city 50 km from the epicenter.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Not necessarily true — while intensity generally decreases with distance from the epicenter, local geology (soft soil vs. bedrock) and building construction can cause a farther city to experience more damage/shaking than a closer one.**

This targets the oversimplified "distance alone determines intensity"
assumption — distance is the dominant factor but not the only one.
</details>

---

**Q9.** What type of fault is associated with a transform plate boundary
like the San Andreas Fault?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Strike-slip fault** — blocks move horizontally past each other, matching the horizontal sliding motion at a transform boundary.
</details>

---

**Q10.** Elastic rebound theory explains earthquakes as the result of what process?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Stress builds up gradually along a locked fault (rock deforms elastically without breaking) until it exceeds the rock's strength, at which point the rock suddenly snaps/slips, releasing the stored energy as seismic waves — an earthquake.**

The key idea most often missed: the *rock itself* bends and stores energy
elastically for a long time before the sudden release, rather than the
earthquake being a single instantaneous event with no buildup phase.
</details>

---

## Answer Key Summary

1. B  2. C  3. P-waves  4. S-waves  5. Focus=underground origin, epicenter=surface point above it
6. Triangulation using 3+ stations' distance circles  7. Normal fault, tension
8. Not necessarily — local geology/construction also matter  9. Strike-slip
10. Gradual stress buildup, then sudden release (elastic rebound)
