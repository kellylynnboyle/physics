## Practice Problems

1. A seismograph station measures a 5-minute delay between P-wave and S-wave arrival, corresponding to a distance of 3,600 km from the epicenter. A second station measures a 2-minute delay, corresponding to 1,400 km. Explain how a third station's data would let scientists pinpoint the exact epicenter.
2. An earthquake has magnitude 6. A second earthquake has magnitude 8. Approximately how many times more energy does the magnitude 8 quake release? (Each whole number ≈32× energy)
3. A city reports severe damage from an earthquake, while a similarly-sized city the same distance from the epicenter reports only minor damage. Propose two possible explanations.
4. A fault shows two blocks sliding horizontally past each other with no vertical motion. Classify the fault type and the likely plate boundary.
5. Explain why S-waves create a "shadow zone" on the opposite side of Earth from an earthquake's epicenter.
6. A region has a history of earthquakes with normal faults. What does this suggest about the type of plate boundary in that region?

<details><summary>Show Answers</summary>

1. Draw a circle of radius 3,600 km around station 1 and 1,400 km around station 2; these two circles intersect at two possible points. A third station's circle intersects at only one of those two points — that intersection is the epicenter.
2. Two whole-number steps ≈ 32×32 ≈ 1,024 times, so roughly 1,000× more energy
3. Possible: the "damaged" city sits on softer soil (amplifying shaking) or has older/less earthquake-resistant construction; the "undamaged" city may sit on solid bedrock or have modern seismic building codes
4. Strike-slip fault; likely a transform plate boundary
5. S-waves cannot travel through the liquid outer core, so they don't reach seismograph stations located on the far side of Earth from the epicenter, creating a "shadow" region with no S-wave detections
6. A divergent boundary (normal faults form under tension, typical of plates pulling apart)

## Real-World Applications

- **Earthquake early warning systems:** detect fast-traveling P-waves and send alerts before slower, more destructive S-waves and surface waves arrive — giving seconds to tens of seconds of warning.
- **Building codes:** seismic engineering standards in earthquake-prone regions (like California) are directly informed by expected magnitude and local intensity data.
- **Insurance and hazard maps:** intensity maps (not magnitude alone) are used to assess regional risk and set insurance rates.
- **Studying Earth's interior:** the shadow zone created by S-waves not passing through the outer core was key evidence that led scientists to determine the outer core is liquid.
