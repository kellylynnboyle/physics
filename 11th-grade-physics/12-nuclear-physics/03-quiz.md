# Unit 12: Nuclear Physics — Interactive Quiz (Most-Missed Questions)

Take this quiz without notes. 8/10 (80%) is the final unit's target — also
the target for the full-year cumulative review.

---

**Q1.** Which type of radioactive decay is stopped by a single sheet of
paper, but is the MOST dangerous if inhaled or ingested?

A) Gamma
B) Beta
C) Alpha
D) All three are equally dangerous in every situation

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C — alpha.**

This is the #1 most-missed idea in the unit: penetrating power and ionizing
power run in *opposite* directions. Alpha particles are large and slow, so
they're easily stopped (low penetrating power) but deposit their energy
very densely over a short distance (high ionizing power) — extremely
damaging to living tissue if the source gets inside the body, even though
it can't get through skin or paper from outside.
</details>

---

**Q2.** A radioactive sample has a half-life of 10 years. After 30 years,
what fraction of the original sample remains?

A) 0
B) 1/3
C) 1/8
D) 1/6

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C — 1/8.**

30 years = 3 half-lives. N=N₀(1/2)³ = N₀/8. A very common error: dividing
the total time by the half-life and using that as a fraction directly
(30/10=3, then incorrectly answering "1/3") instead of raising 1/2 to the
power of the number of half-lives elapsed.
</details>

---

**Q3.** True or False: After exactly 2 half-lives, a radioactive sample is
completely gone.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**False.**

After 2 half-lives, 1/4 of the original sample remains (N₀(1/2)²=N₀/4) —
not zero. Radioactive decay approaches zero asymptotically but
mathematically never reaches exactly zero in a finite number of half-lives.
</details>

---

**Q4.** Which type of nuclear reaction powers the Sun?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Nuclear fusion** — light nuclei (hydrogen) combine into heavier nuclei
(helium), releasing enormous energy. This is the opposite process from
fission, which splits heavy nuclei and is used in current nuclear power
plants.
</details>

---

**Q5.** A student says: "I stood near an X-ray machine, so now I'm
radioactive and could set off a Geiger counter." Is this accurate?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**No.**

Being exposed to radiation (like X-rays) does not make a person or object
radioactive themselves, unless they actually absorbed radioactive
*material* (particles/dust). Radiation exposure and becoming a radiation
*source* are two different things — this is a commonly missed distinction.
</details>

---

**Q6.** Using E=mc², if a small amount of mass (m) is converted entirely
into energy, why is the resulting energy release so enormous even for a
tiny mass?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Because c (the speed of light, ≈3×10⁸ m/s) is squared in the equation — c² is an enormous number (≈9×10¹⁶), so even a very small mass converts into a huge amount of energy.**

This explains why nuclear reactions (which convert a small but nonzero
amount of mass into energy) release far more energy per reaction than
chemical reactions (which involve essentially no mass-to-energy
conversion, just electron rearrangement).
</details>

---

**Q7.** Two isotopes of carbon are Carbon-12 and Carbon-14. What's different
between them?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**The number of neutrons — both have 6 protons (that's what makes them carbon), but Carbon-14 has 2 more neutrons than Carbon-12 (8 vs. 6), making it heavier and radioactive.**

The most-missed piece: isotopes differ in neutron count, NOT proton count
— changing proton count would change which *element* it is entirely.
</details>

---

**Q8.** Which type of decay involves the emission of a high-energy photon,
with no change in the atom's atomic number or mass number?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Gamma decay.**

Gamma decay releases pure electromagnetic energy (a photon) without
changing the nucleus's proton or neutron count — it usually follows alpha
or beta decay as the nucleus settles into a lower-energy state. Alpha and
beta decay, by contrast, DO change the atomic number (transforming the
atom into a different element).
</details>

---

**Q9.** Compare the amount of energy released by a nuclear reaction to a
chemical reaction involving a similar amount of material.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**A nuclear reaction releases vastly more energy — often millions of times more — because it involves changes to the nucleus itself (with a measurable mass-to-energy conversion via E=mc²), while a chemical reaction only rearranges electrons and involves no meaningful mass-to-energy conversion.**

This connects Q6's E=mc² idea directly to why nuclear power/weapons are so
much more energetic than chemical combustion/explosives for a similar mass
of material.
</details>

---

**Q10.** A radioactive sample starts with 800 atoms and has a half-life of
5 minutes. How many atoms remain after 20 minutes?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**50 atoms.**

20 minutes = 4 half-lives. N=800×(1/2)⁴=800/16=50. Same skill as Q2, with
a starting quantity instead of a fraction — always find the number of
half-lives first (total time ÷ half-life), then apply (1/2) raised to that
power.
</details>
