# Unit 12: Nuclear Physics — Key Concepts

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:
1. Describe atomic structure (protons, neutrons, electrons) and define isotopes.
2. Distinguish alpha, beta, and gamma radioactive decay, including their penetrating power and ionizing ability.
3. Apply half-life calculations to find remaining quantity or elapsed time.
4. Distinguish nuclear fission from nuclear fusion.
5. Explain mass-energy equivalence (E=mc²) and binding energy conceptually.
6. Compare nuclear reactions to chemical reactions.

## Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Isotope | Atoms of the same element (same protons) with different numbers of neutrons |
| Alpha decay (α) | Emission of a helium nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons); least penetrating, most ionizing |
| Beta decay (β) | Emission of an electron (or positron); moderate penetrating power and ionizing ability |
| Gamma decay (γ) | Emission of high-energy EM radiation (a photon); most penetrating, least ionizing |
| Half-life | Time for half of a radioactive sample to decay |
| Nuclear fission | A heavy nucleus splits into smaller nuclei, releasing energy |
| Nuclear fusion | Light nuclei combine into a heavier nucleus, releasing energy (powers the Sun) |
| Binding energy | Energy required to break a nucleus apart into individual protons/neutrons; relates to nuclear stability |
| Mass-energy equivalence | E=mc² — mass and energy are interconvertible |

## Core Formulas

- Mass-energy equivalence: E = mc²
- Half-life remaining quantity: N = N₀(1/2)^(t/T½), where T½ is the half-life

## The Big Idea

**Radiation type determines the tradeoff between penetrating power and
ionizing ability — they run in opposite directions.** Alpha particles are
stopped by a sheet of paper (least penetrating) but cause the most damage
if they get inside the body (most ionizing, since they're large and slow
and deposit energy densely). Gamma rays pass through several centimeters
of lead (most penetrating) but ionize less per unit path length. This
inverse relationship — not "which one is more dangerous overall" as a
single number — is the core conceptual hurdle of the unit.

## Common Misconceptions to Address Directly

- Believing radioactivity is "contagious" — an object exposed to radiation
  doesn't become radioactive itself unless it absorbed radioactive
  material (like dust/particles), which is different from simply being
  near a radiation source.
- Believing a sample is "completely gone" after two half-lives (it's not —
  after 2 half-lives, 1/4 remains; after 3, 1/8 remains — it approaches
  zero but mathematically never reaches exactly zero).
- Confusing fission and fusion — fission *splits* heavy nuclei (used in
  current nuclear power plants and atomic bombs); fusion *combines* light
  nuclei (powers the Sun and stars; not yet practical for widespread power
  generation on Earth).
- Assuming higher penetrating power always means "more dangerous" —
  ignoring that alpha particles, despite being stopped by skin/paper, are
  extremely dangerous if inhaled or ingested because of their high
  ionizing power at close range.
- Treating nuclear reactions like chemical reactions — chemical reactions
  rearrange electrons and don't change which element you have; nuclear
  reactions change the nucleus itself (protons/neutrons), transforming one
  element into another and releasing vastly more energy per reaction.
