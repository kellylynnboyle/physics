# Unit 12: Nuclear Physics — SAT/ACT Practice

- **SAT Math** turns half-life into an **exponential function** word
  problem — a natural fit for the SAT's exponential-growth/decay question
  type.
- **ACT Science** turns radioactive decay into a **data table or decay
  curve graph**, testing whether you can extract the half-life from data
  rather than being told it directly.

## SAT Math Style

**Q1.** A radioactive sample's mass is modeled by m(t) = 200(1/2)^(t/5),
where t is in years. What is the sample's half-life, and how much remains
after 15 years?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Half-life = 5 years. After 15 years: 25 g.**

The exponent's denominator (5) is the half-life by construction. At t=15:
m=200(1/2)^(15/5)=200(1/2)³=200/8=25 g. This tests reading a half-life
directly out of a given exponential model — a very common SAT Math
"interpret the model" question.
</details>

---

**Q2.** Using the same model, after how many years will exactly 1/8 of the
sample remain?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**15 years.**

1/8 = (1/2)³, so 3 half-lives have passed: 3×5=15 years. Same idea as Q1,
solved in the other direction (given the fraction, find the time).
</details>

## ACT Science Reasoning Style

**Q3.** A student records the mass of a radioactive sample over time:

| Time (days) | Mass remaining (g) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 320 |
| 4 | 160 |
| 8 | 80 |
| 12 | 40 |

Based on this data, what is the sample's half-life?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**4 days.**

The mass halves every 4 days (320→160→80→40), so that's the half-life —
this must be extracted from the pattern in the table rather than being
given directly, which is the core ACT Science skill being tested (unlike
the SAT version, where the half-life is handed to you in the formula).
</details>

---

**Q4.** Based on the table in Q3, approximately how much mass would remain
at t = 20 days?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**≈10 g.**

20 days = 5 half-lives (4-day half-life). 320×(1/2)⁵=320/32=10 g. This
requires extending the established pattern one more step past the given
data — a modest, well-supported extrapolation given the very consistent
halving pattern already shown across 3 consecutive intervals.
</details>

---

**Q5.** A second sample (different isotope) is tested and found to halve
every 2 days instead of 4. After the same 12 days, which sample (this one
or the one in Q3) has LESS mass remaining, assuming both started at 320 g?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**The second sample (2-day half-life) has less remaining.**

12 days ÷ 2 = 6 half-lives → 320×(1/2)⁶=320/64=5 g, compared to the first
sample's 40 g at 12 days (from Q3's table). A shorter half-life means
faster decay, so less material remains after the same elapsed time — the
ACT Science "compare two experiments with one varied factor" question type.
</details>

---

## Answer Key Summary

1. Half-life=5 yr, 25 g remaining at t=15  2. 15 years  3. 4 days
4. ≈10 g  5. The second sample (2-day half-life), with 5 g vs. 40 g
