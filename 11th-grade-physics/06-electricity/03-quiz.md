# Unit 6: Electricity — Interactive Quiz (Most-Missed Questions)

Take this quiz without notes. 8/10 (80%) to advance to Unit 7.

---

**Q1.** As current flows around a simple series circuit with one battery
and one resistor, what happens to the current as it passes through the
resistor?

A) It decreases, since the resistor uses some of it up
B) It stays exactly the same
C) It increases, since energy is added
D) It becomes voltage

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**B — it stays exactly the same.**

This is the #1 most-missed idea in circuits: current is *not* "used up."
What the resistor consumes is energy (showing up as a voltage drop) — the
same charge per second flows in and out of every point in a series loop.
</details>

---

**Q2.** Two identical resistors are connected in parallel. Compared to a
single resistor of the same value, how does the parallel combination's
total resistance compare?

A) Double
B) The same
C) Half
D) Cannot be determined

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C — half.**

1/R_total = 1/R + 1/R = 2/R → R_total = R/2. Students very often assume
adding resistors always increases resistance (true for series, false for
parallel) — more parallel paths means it's *easier* for current to flow
overall.
</details>

---

**Q3.** A circuit has a 12 V battery and a single 4 Ω resistor. What is the current?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**3 A.**

I = V/R = 12/4 = 3 A. Straightforward Ohm's Law — included as a baseline
before the more commonly missed multi-resistor versions below.
</details>

---

**Q4.** Two resistors, 3 Ω and 6 Ω, are connected in series to a 9 V
battery. What is the current through the 3 Ω resistor?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**1 A.**

Series: R_total = 3+6 = 9 Ω. I = V/R_total = 9/9 = 1 A. In series, current
is the *same* through every component — so the current through the 3 Ω
resistor is the same 1 A that flows through the 6 Ω resistor and the
battery. A common wrong approach: trying to compute a separate current for
each resistor rather than recognizing they share the same current.
</details>

---

**Q5.** Using the same circuit as Q4, what is the voltage across the 6 Ω resistor?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**6 V.**

V = IR = (1 A)(6 Ω) = 6 V. Check: voltage across the 3 Ω resistor is
(1)(3)=3 V, and 3+6=9 V matches the battery — voltages in series *add up*
to the total, unlike current which stays constant.
</details>

---

**Q6.** Two resistors, 4 Ω and 4 Ω, are connected in parallel to a 8 V
battery. What is the voltage across each resistor?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**8 V across each (same as the battery).**

In parallel, every branch experiences the *same* voltage as the source —
this is the parallel-circuit analog of "current is the same in series."
The most common error is trying to "split" the voltage between branches
the way current would split, when it's actually current that splits in
parallel circuits, not voltage.
</details>

---

**Q7.** True or False: A battery is best described as a constant-current source.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**False.**

A battery is better modeled as a (roughly) constant-**voltage** source
(its EMF). The current that actually flows depends on what resistance is
connected to it (I=V/R) — connect a bigger resistor, and less current
flows, even though the battery's voltage rating doesn't change.
</details>

---

**Q8.** A 60 W light bulb and a 100 W light bulb are both connected to the
same 120 V outlet (separately, each on its own circuit). Which bulb has
more resistance?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**The 60 W bulb.**

Using P=V²/R → R=V²/P. Since V is the same (120 V) for both, resistance is
*inversely* proportional to power: the lower-wattage bulb has higher
resistance (less current flows through it at the same voltage, so it
converts energy more slowly). This surprises many students who expect
"more watts = more resistance."
</details>

---

**Q9.** A charge of +2 C is placed 3 m from a charge of +4 C. If the
distance is doubled to 6 m, by what factor does the force between them
change?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Force decreases to ¼ of its original value.**

Coulomb's Law: F ∝ 1/r². Doubling r means dividing F by 2² = 4. This is an
inverse-square relationship, the same pattern as gravity — a very
commonly missed idea is assuming doubling distance simply halves the
force (that would be true for a 1/r relationship, not 1/r²).
</details>

---

**Q10.** A hair dryer uses 1200 W of power and runs for 0.5 hours. How much
energy did it use, in kWh?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**0.6 kWh.**

E = Pt = 1200 W × 0.5 h = 600 Wh = 0.6 kWh. This connects the abstract
power formula to something students see directly on a utility bill — a
common error is forgetting to convert Watts to kilowatts (dividing by 1000)
before reporting the final answer.
</details>
