# Unit 6: Electricity — SAT/ACT Practice

- **SAT Math** turns Ohm's Law and Coulomb's Law into **direct substitution
  and inverse-proportion word problems**.
- **ACT Science** turns circuits into **data tables** — usually current or
  resistance measured across several trials — testing whether you can read
  a proportional relationship without needing the formula at all.

## SAT Math Style

**Q1.** Using V = IR, if a circuit has a resistance of 5 Ω and a current of
3 A, what is the voltage?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**15 V.** Direct substitution: V=IR=5×3=15 V.
</details>

---

**Q2.** Two charges are separated by distance r, producing a force F. If the
distance is tripled, what is the new force in terms of F?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**F/9.**

Coulomb's Law: F ∝ 1/r². Tripling r divides F by 3²=9. The most-missed
error: dividing by 3 instead of 9 (treating it as inverse, not
inverse-square).
</details>

---

**Q3.** A circuit's total resistance is 1/R = 1/4 + 1/12. What is R?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**3 Ω.**

1/R = 3/12 + 1/12 = 4/12 = 1/3 → R = 3 Ω. This is a fractions-algebra
question wearing a physics costume — the most common error is forgetting
to flip the final fraction (stopping at 1/R=1/3 and answering "1/3" instead
of R=3).
</details>

## ACT Science Reasoning Style

**Q4.** A student varies voltage across a fixed resistor and measures current:

| Voltage (V) | Current (A) |
|---|---|
| 2 | 0.5 |
| 4 | 1.0 |
| 6 | 1.5 |
| 8 | 2.0 |

What is the resistance of this resistor, based on the data?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**4 Ω** (check any row: R=V/I=2/0.5=4; 4/1.0=4; consistent throughout).

The ACT Science skill here is verifying the ratio holds across every row,
not just computing one row and assuming it's right — a resistor's value
should be constant regardless of which data point you use.
</details>

---

**Q5.** A second experiment tests three different resistors at a constant
6 V and records current:

| Resistor | Current (A) |
|---|---|
| X | 3.0 |
| Y | 1.5 |
| Z | 0.5 |

Rank the resistors from lowest to highest resistance.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**X < Y < Z.**

At constant voltage, R=V/I, so resistance is *inversely* related to
current — the resistor with the *most* current (X) has the *least*
resistance. The most-missed trap: ranking by current directly (assuming
higher current = higher resistance) instead of inverting the relationship.
</details>

---

**Q6.** Based on Experiments in Q4 and Q5, a student claims: "For any fixed
resistor, current is directly proportional to voltage." Is this supported?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Yes — Q4's data (with one resistor, voltage varied) shows current doubling every time voltage doubles, consistent with I=V/R for constant R.**

Q5 doesn't test this claim (it varies resistor, not voltage), so only Q4's
data speaks to it — but Q4 alone is sufficient support. Recognizing which
experiment actually tests the claim in question is the core ACT Science
skill being checked here.
</details>

---

## Answer Key Summary

1. 15 V  2. F/9  3. 3 Ω  4. 4 Ω  5. X < Y < Z  6. Yes (supported by Q4's data)
