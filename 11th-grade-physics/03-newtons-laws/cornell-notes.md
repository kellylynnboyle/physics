# Cornell Notes — Newton's Laws

**Topic:** Forces and Motion
**Date:** ______________          **Name:** ______________

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| State Newton's 1st Law in one sentence. | An object keeps its state of motion (rest or constant velocity) unless a net force acts on it. "Constant velocity" needs zero net force, not zero force. |
| State Newton's 2nd Law and its equation. | Net force equals mass times acceleration: `F_net = ma`. Acceleration points the same direction as net force. |
| State Newton's 3rd Law — and the key catch. | Every action force has an equal, opposite reaction force. **The two forces act on two different objects** — they can never cancel each other out because they're not both acting on the same thing. |
| What is a free-body diagram (FBD) and what belongs on it? | A diagram of ONE object showing every force acting **on** it (gravity/weight, normal, tension, applied, friction) as arrows from the object's center. Forces the object exerts on other things do NOT belong on its FBD. |
| Is normal force always equal to weight? | No. `N = mg` only on a flat surface with no other vertical forces. On an incline, `N = mg·cos(θ)`. If something also pulls up/down on the object, N adjusts to balance the vertical forces. |
| Static vs. kinetic friction — what's the difference? | Static friction (`f_s ≤ μ_s N`) resists the START of sliding and can vary up to a max value. Kinetic friction (`f_k = μ_k N`) acts once the object IS sliding, at a fixed value, usually less than max static friction. |
| Mass vs. weight — how are they different? | Mass (kg) = amount of matter, constant everywhere. Weight (N) = `mg`, the force of gravity on that mass — changes with location (e.g., the Moon). |
| Common trap: "an object moving at constant velocity needs a constant force." | False. Constant velocity means `F_net = 0` (1st Law) — any applied force must be exactly balanced by an opposing force (like friction), not unbalanced. |

## Summary
Newton's Laws form a chain: the 1st Law defines when nothing needs
explaining (zero net force → zero acceleration), the 2nd Law quantifies
what happens when forces are unbalanced (`F_net = ma`), and the 3rd Law
explains that every force comes paired with an equal, opposite one acting
on a *different* object. Free-body diagrams are the practical skill that
ties it together — get the diagram right, and F = ma becomes simple
algebra. The most common student errors are forgetting the 3rd Law pair
acts on a different object, assuming N always equals mg, and believing
constant velocity requires a constant unbalanced force.
