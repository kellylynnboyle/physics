# Unit 3: Newton's Laws — Key Concepts

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:
1. State and apply Newton's First Law (inertia) to explain why objects resist changes in motion.
2. State and apply Newton's Second Law (Fnet = ma), including setting up and solving multi-force problems.
3. State and apply Newton's Third Law, correctly identifying action-reaction pairs (same object type, opposite objects, equal magnitude, opposite direction).
4. Draw correct free-body diagrams (FBDs) for objects on inclines, being pulled/pushed, or in equilibrium.
5. Calculate weight, normal force, friction force (static and kinetic), and tension in simple systems.
6. Distinguish mass (invariant, scalar, kg) from weight (force, depends on g, Newtons).

## Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Inertia | An object's tendency to resist a change in its state of motion |
| Net force (Fnet) | The vector sum of all forces acting on an object |
| Newton's First Law | An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force |
| Newton's Second Law | Fnet = ma — acceleration is proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass |
| Newton's Third Law | For every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force, acting on a *different* object |
| Normal force (N) | Support force perpendicular to a surface |
| Friction (f) | Force resisting relative motion between surfaces; static (before sliding) vs. kinetic (during sliding) |
| Free-body diagram (FBD) | A diagram showing all forces acting ON one object, drawn as vectors from a single point |
| Weight (W) | Force of gravity on an object: W = mg |

## Core Formulas

- Fnet = ma  (Newton's Second Law — the central equation of the unit)
- W = mg
- Ff(static, max) = μₛN;  Ff(kinetic) = μₖN
- On a frictionless incline at angle θ: component of gravity along the incline = mg sin θ; normal force = mg cos θ

## The Three Laws, Plainly

1. **First Law (Inertia):** No net force → no change in velocity. Constant velocity (including zero) needs zero net force, not zero force.
2. **Second Law (Fnet = ma):** Net force causes acceleration. Bigger net force → bigger acceleration; bigger mass → smaller acceleration for the same force.
3. **Third Law (Action-Reaction):** Forces come in pairs on *different* objects. If you push a wall, the wall pushes back on you with equal force — this pair never "cancels out" for FBD purposes because they act on different objects.

## Common Misconceptions to Address Directly

- "Force is required to keep something moving." (False — First Law: constant velocity requires zero *net* force, not zero force at all; this is the single most persistent misconception in mechanics.)
- "Heavier objects fall faster." (False, ignoring air resistance — a = Fnet/m, and for gravity alone Fnet = mg, so a = g regardless of mass; the m's cancel.)
- Confusing mass and weight ("How much do you weigh in kg?" is actually a mass question).
- Believing action-reaction pairs act on the *same* object and therefore cancel (they never do — they act on two different objects, so they never appear together in one object's FBD).
- Forgetting friction only opposes *relative* or *impending* motion, and forgetting static friction is not fixed — it adjusts up to a maximum (μₛN) to prevent motion.
