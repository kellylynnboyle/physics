# Unit 3: Newton's Laws — Interactive Quiz (Most-Missed Questions)

Take this quiz without notes. 8/10 (80%) to advance to Unit 4.

---

**Q1.** A hockey puck slides across frictionless ice at constant velocity.
What net force is acting on it?

A) A force equal to its weight
B) A small forward force to keep it moving
C) Zero net force
D) A force equal to ma, in the direction of motion

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C — zero net force.**

This is the #1 most-missed idea in the whole course: constant velocity
(including "moving in a straight line at steady speed") requires **zero net
force**, not a force "keeping it going." Newton's First Law says objects in
motion stay in motion on their own, with no force needed, as long as
nothing acts to change that motion.
</details>

**Q2.** You push a stalled car and it doesn't move. According to Newton's
Third Law, the car pushes back on you with:

A) A smaller force, since it isn't moving
B) A larger force, since it's heavier
C) An equal and opposite force
D) No force, since the car is inanimate

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C.**

Third Law pairs are *always* equal in magnitude and opposite in direction,
regardless of mass, motion, or whether the object is "alive." The car not
moving is explained by the *First/Second Law* (maybe static friction plus
your push don't add up to enough net force) — not by the reaction force
being weaker.
</details>

**Q3.** Why do a bowling ball and a tennis ball, dropped from the same
height with no air resistance, hit the ground at the same time?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

Fnet on each is its weight, W = mg. Acceleration is a = Fnet/m = mg/m = g —
the mass cancels out, so a = g for *any* mass. A heavier object has more
force pulling it down, but it also has more inertia (mass) resisting that
pull, and the two effects exactly cancel.
</details>

**Q4.** A 10 kg box is pushed with 40 N of force across a floor with 15 N of
kinetic friction opposing it. What is the box's acceleration?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**2.5 m/s².**

Fnet = 40 - 15 = 25 N. a = Fnet/m = 25/10 = 2.5 m/s². Common error: using
the full 40 N instead of subtracting friction to get the *net* force first.
</details>

**Q5.** Two boxes, A (2 kg) and B (8 kg), are pushed by the same 16 N force
on a frictionless surface. Compare their accelerations.

A) Box A accelerates 4 times faster than Box B
B) Box B accelerates 4 times faster than Box A
C) They accelerate the same
D) Box A accelerates twice as fast

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**A.**

a = F/m. Box A: a=16/2=8 m/s². Box B: a=16/8=2 m/s². 8 is 4× 2 — same
force, and acceleration is *inversely* proportional to mass, so the lighter
box accelerates more.
</details>

**Q6.** A book sits at rest on a table. Identify the action-reaction pair
that includes "the table pushes up on the book (normal force)."

A) Gravity pulling the book down
B) The book pushes down on the table
C) The book pushes down on the floor
D) There is no reaction force since the book isn't moving

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**B — the book pushes down on the table.**

A very common error is pairing the normal force with gravity (A) because
they're equal in this scenario and "cancel" on the book's FBD — but that's
a coincidence of *equilibrium* (Newton's Second Law: Fnet=0), not a Third
Law pair. Third Law pairs always involve the *same two objects*, with the
forces swapped (table-on-book, book-on-table) — never gravity paired with a
normal force, which involves three different force types.
</details>

**Q7.** What is the mass of an 80 kg astronaut on the Moon (g_moon ≈ 1.6
m/s²), and what is their weight there?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Mass = 80 kg (unchanged). Weight ≈ 128 N.**

Mass doesn't change based on location — it's a property of the object
itself. Weight = mg = 80 × 1.6 = 128 N, much less than on Earth (≈784 N)
because g is smaller on the Moon. The most common error is saying the
astronaut's mass changes on the Moon.
</details>

---

**Q8.** A 5 kg block rests on a 30° frictionless incline. What is the
component of gravity accelerating it down the slope? (g=9.8 m/s²)

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**a = g sin(30°) = 9.8 × 0.5 = 4.9 m/s².**

The along-incline component of gravity is mg sin θ, giving acceleration g
sin θ (mass cancels, same idea as Q3). A common error is using cos θ
instead of sin θ for the along-incline component — remember: sin pairs with
the angle measured from the *horizontal* for the along-slope direction.
</details>

---

**Q9.** True or False: If an object is accelerating, there must be a net
force acting on it in the direction of acceleration.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**True.**

This is Newton's Second Law directly: Fnet = ma means acceleration and net
force always point in the same direction, and if a ≠ 0, then Fnet ≠ 0.
</details>

---

**Q10.** A student says: "A parked car and a car moving at constant 60 mph
on a straight highway both have zero net force acting on them, so they're
in the same physical state." Is the student's *physics* reasoning about net
force correct? What's different between the two cars?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

The net-force reasoning is correct — both have Fnet = 0 (First Law
applies to both: no acceleration in either case). What's different is their
*velocity*, not their net force — one is at rest, one is moving at constant
velocity. This question tests whether students understand that "zero net
force" describes zero *acceleration*, not zero *motion*.
</details>
