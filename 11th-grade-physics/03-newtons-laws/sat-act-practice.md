# Unit 3: Newton's Laws — SAT/ACT Practice

- **SAT Math** turns F=ma into **linear equation/proportional reasoning**
  problems (solve for a missing variable, or compare ratios).
- **ACT Science** turns it into **force/acceleration data tables and
  graphs** — usually from a described experiment with a cart and varying
  masses or forces, testing whether you can read a proportional (or
  inverse) relationship straight off the data.

## SAT Math Style

**Q1.** If F = ma, and a force of 24 N gives a 4 kg object an acceleration
of a, what is a?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**6 m/s².**

a = F/m = 24/4 = 6 m/s². Simple, but the most common error on this exact
question type is dividing the wrong way (m/F instead of F/m), especially
under time pressure.
</details>

---

**Q2.** An object experiences two forces acting in the same direction: 15 N
and 9 N. If its mass is 3 kg, what is its acceleration?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**8 m/s².**

Fnet = 15+9 = 24 N. a = 24/3 = 8 m/s². The trap: some SAT-style versions of
this question put the forces in *opposite* directions instead — always
check the problem's wording (or diagram) for direction before adding.
</details>

---

**Q3.** Object A has twice the mass of Object B. If the same net force is
applied to both, how does Object A's acceleration compare to Object B's?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Object A's acceleration is half of Object B's.**

Since a = F/m and F is the same for both, acceleration is inversely
proportional to mass. This is a proportional-reasoning question dressed up
as physics — the most-missed error is assuming "more mass → more
acceleration" (direct proportion) instead of recognizing the inverse
relationship.
</details>

## ACT Science Reasoning Style

**Q4.** A student applies different forces to a 2 kg cart and measures its
acceleration:

| Force (N) | Acceleration (m/s²) |
|---|---|
| 2 | 1.0 |
| 4 | 2.0 |
| 6 | 3.0 |
| 8 | 4.0 |

Based on this data, what is the relationship between force and acceleration?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Directly proportional — acceleration doubles when force doubles (a = F/m, with m constant at 2 kg, so a = F/2).**

This is a straightforward "identify the trend" question, but a common trap
version of this same table asks students to also verify that the mass is
consistent with the ratio in every row (F/a = 2 kg here, matching the given
cart mass) — always check the ratio holds across *all* rows, not just one.
</details>

---

**Q5.** A second experiment keeps the force constant at 6 N and varies the
cart's mass:

| Mass (kg) | Acceleration (m/s²) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 6.0 |
| 2 | 3.0 |
| 3 | 2.0 |
| 6 | 1.0 |

What relationship does this data show between mass and acceleration?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Inversely proportional — when mass doubles, acceleration is cut in half (check: mass×acceleration = 6 in every row, matching the constant force).**

The most-missed ACT Science trap on this table type: students see
"acceleration goes down as mass goes up" and correctly call it an inverse
trend, but then incorrectly describe it as "linear" or try to fit a
straight-line equation to it — an inverse relationship is a curve, not a
line, even though the *trend direction* (down as the other goes up) is
correctly identified.
</details>

---

**Q6.** Combining the results of Experiments 1 (Q4) and 2 (Q5), which
statement is best supported?

A) Acceleration depends only on force
B) Acceleration depends only on mass
C) Acceleration depends on both force and mass, in a way consistent with a = F/m
D) The two experiments contradict each other

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C.**

This is the classic ACT Science "synthesize two experiments" question.
Experiment 1 (mass held constant) shows direct proportionality to force;
Experiment 2 (force held constant) shows inverse proportionality to mass —
together, exactly what F=ma predicts. The trap answers (A, B) each ignore
one of the two experiments; D wrongly assumes the two trends conflict when
they're actually two halves of the same relationship.
</details>

---

## Answer Key Summary

1. 6 m/s²  2. 8 m/s²  3. Half of Object B's  4. Directly proportional
5. Inversely proportional  6. C
