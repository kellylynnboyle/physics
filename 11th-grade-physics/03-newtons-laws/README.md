# Section 3: Newton's Laws

## Overview
Newton's Laws explain *why* objects move the way kinematics describes.
This section connects forces to motion and introduces free-body diagrams,
the single most useful tool in all of mechanics.

## Key Concepts to Master
- **Newton's 1st Law (Inertia)**: an object at rest stays at rest, and an
  object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on
  by a net (unbalanced) force. Constant velocity does NOT require a net
  force — only a *change* in velocity does.
- **Newton's 2nd Law**: `F_net = ma`. Net force and acceleration are
  vectors and always point in the same direction.
- **Newton's 3rd Law**: for every action force, there is an equal and
  opposite reaction force. Critically, **the two forces act on two
  different objects** — they never cancel each other out on the same
  object.
- **Mass vs. weight**: mass (kg) is the amount of matter in an object and
  doesn't change with location. Weight (N) is the force of gravity on
  that mass, `W = mg`, and changes depending on the local gravitational
  field.
- **Free-body diagrams (FBDs)**: isolate a single object and draw every
  force acting *on* it (gravity, normal, tension, friction, applied) as
  an arrow. Never include forces the object exerts on something else.
- **Normal force**: the support force perpendicular to a surface. It is
  NOT always equal to weight — only in simple flat, horizontal, no-other-
  vertical-force cases.
- **Friction**: `f_s,max = μ_s·N` (static, prevents motion from starting)
  and `f_k = μ_k·N` (kinetic, acts once sliding). Static friction is
  usually larger than kinetic friction for the same surfaces.
- **Equilibrium**: `F_net = 0` means zero acceleration — the object is
  either at rest or moving at constant velocity, not necessarily
  stationary.

## Learning Objectives
Students will be able to:
1. State all three of Newton's Laws in their own words and identify
   which law explains a given scenario.
2. Draw a correct, complete free-body diagram for an object in various
   situations (flat surface, incline, hanging from ropes, accelerating).
3. Correctly identify Newton's 3rd Law action–reaction pairs.
4. Distinguish mass from weight and static from kinetic friction.
5. Use F = ma to solve for unknown force, mass, or acceleration,
   including multi-force and incline problems.

## Practice Problems

1. A 5 kg box sits on a table. What is the normal force on the box?
   (g = 9.8 m/s²)
2. A 1000 kg car accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 8 seconds. What net
   force is required?
3. A book rests on a table. Identify the Newton's 3rd Law reaction force
   to "the table pushes up on the book."
4. A 10 kg crate is pulled across a floor with 40 N of horizontal force.
   The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.3. Find the crate's
   acceleration. (g = 9.8 m/s²)
5. A person stands still on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at
   2 m/s². If their mass is 70 kg, what does the scale read?

### Answer Key
1. On a flat table with no other vertical forces: `N = mg = 5(9.8) = 49 N`.
2. `a = Δv/Δt = 20/8 = 2.5 m/s²`; `F = ma = 1000(2.5) = 2500 N`.
3. "The book pushes down on the table" — equal magnitude, opposite
   direction, acting on the *table* (not the book).
4. Friction: `f_k = μ_k N = 0.3(10)(9.8) = 29.4 N`. Net force:
   `F_net = 40 − 29.4 = 10.6 N`. `a = F_net/m = 10.6/10 = 1.06 m/s²`.
5. Scale reads the normal force: `N − mg = ma → N = m(g+a) =
   70(9.8+2) = 826 N` (more than their actual weight of 686 N).

## Real-World Applications
- **Seatbelts and airbags**: Newton's 1st Law — passengers keep moving
  forward in a crash unless a force (belt/airbag) acts on them.
- **Rocket propulsion**: Newton's 3rd Law — expelling exhaust gas
  backward pushes the rocket forward.
- **Vehicle braking distance & tire design**: friction coefficients
  determine how quickly a car can safely stop.
- **Elevators & roller coasters**: apparent weight changes (Newton's 2nd
  Law) during acceleration are what riders feel as "heavier" or "lighter."

  explains why 3rd Law pairs never cancel on one object.
