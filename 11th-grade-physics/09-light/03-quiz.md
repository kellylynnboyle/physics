# Unit 9: Light — Interactive Quiz (Most-Missed Questions)

Take this quiz without notes. 8/10 (80%) to advance to Unit 10.

---

**Q1.** A light ray hits a mirror, making a 30° angle with the mirror's
surface. What is the angle of incidence, measured the correct way?

A) 30°
B) 60°
C) 90°
D) 150°

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**B — 60°.**

This is the #1 most-missed idea in the unit: angles are always measured
from the **normal** (a line perpendicular to the surface), not from the
surface itself. If the ray makes 30° with the surface, it makes 90°-30°=60°
with the normal — that 60° is the angle of incidence.
</details>

---

**Q2.** Does light require a medium (like air or water) to travel through?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**No.**

Light is an electromagnetic wave and can travel through a vacuum — that's
how sunlight crosses the vacuum of space to reach Earth. This is a key
contrast with sound, which is a mechanical wave and absolutely requires a
medium.
</details>

---

**Q3.** A red shirt is viewed under pure blue light (no red or other
wavelengths present). What color does it appear?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Black (or very dark).**

The shirt appears red under white light because it reflects red wavelengths
and absorbs the rest. Under pure blue light, there's no red light present
to reflect, and the shirt absorbs the blue — so essentially nothing is
reflected back to your eye. This targets the misconception that color is a
fixed, inherent property of an object rather than dependent on which
wavelengths are available to reflect.
</details>

---

**Q4.** Light travels from air (n=1.00) into glass (n=1.5) at an angle of
incidence of 30°. Using Snell's Law, find the angle of refraction (sin30°=0.5).

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**≈19.5°.**

n₁sin(θ₁)=n₂sin(θ₂) → (1.00)(0.5) = (1.5)sin(θ₂) → sin(θ₂)=0.333 →
θ₂≈19.5°. The most common error: plugging into the formula backward
(dividing instead of multiplying, or swapping which index goes with which
angle).
</details>

---

**Q5.** You look at your reflection in a flat bathroom mirror. Is that
image real or virtual?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Virtual.**

The light rays only *appear* to come from behind the mirror — they don't
actually converge there, so the image cannot be projected onto a screen.
A very common error is assuming any image you can clearly "see" must be
real; visibility doesn't determine real vs. virtual, whether the light
rays actually converge does.
</details>

---

**Q6.** A converging (convex) lens is used to project an image onto a
screen — like in a movie projector. Is that image real or virtual?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Real.**

Since it can be projected onto a screen, the light rays actually converge
at that location — the defining test for a real image. This contrasts
directly with Q5's virtual image, which can never be projected.
</details>

---

**Q7.** White light passes through a glass prism and separates into a
rainbow of colors. What causes this?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Different colors (wavelengths) of light bend by slightly different amounts when refracting into and out of the glass, because each color travels at a slightly different speed inside the glass (the index of refraction depends slightly on wavelength).**

The key idea: in vacuum, all colors travel at the same speed c — the
separation only happens because each color slows down (and bends) by a
different amount *inside* a medium like glass.
</details>

---

**Q8.** True or False: All colors of visible light travel at the same speed
in a vacuum.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**True.**

In vacuum, every wavelength of light (and every other EM wave) travels at
the same speed, c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s. Speed differences between colors only show
up inside a medium (like glass or water), which is exactly what causes
prism dispersion (Q7).
</details>

---

**Q9.** Light passing through a very narrow slit spreads out and creates a
pattern of bright and dark bands on a screen behind it. What phenomenon is
this?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Diffraction (and the resulting interference pattern) — the classic double-slit / single-slit experiment demonstrating light's wave nature.**

This connects directly back to Unit 8: light, like any wave, diffracts
around obstacles and through narrow openings, and the overlapping diffracted
waves interfere constructively and destructively to produce the pattern.
</details>

---

**Q10.** A diverging (concave) lens is placed in a beam of parallel light
rays. What happens to the rays after passing through the lens?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**They spread apart (diverge), appearing to originate from a virtual focal point on the same side as the incoming light.**

This is the direct opposite behavior of a converging lens, which bends
parallel rays *toward* a real focal point on the far side.
</details>
