# Cornell Notes — Momentum

**Topic:** Momentum, Impulse, and Collisions
**Date:** ______________          **Name:** ______________

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is momentum, and is it a vector or scalar? | `p = mv`. It's a **vector** — direction matters. Two objects moving toward each other have momenta with opposite signs. |
| What is impulse, and how does it relate to momentum? | `J = FΔt = Δp`. A force applied over a time interval produces a change in momentum. Same Δp can come from a big force over a short time OR a small force over a long time. |
| Why do airbags/crumple zones reduce injury? | They increase the collision time (Δt). Since `FΔt = Δp` is fixed by the crash, a longer Δt means a smaller average force F on the passenger. |
| State conservation of momentum. | In a system with no external net force, total momentum before = total momentum after: `m₁v₁ᵢ + m₂v₂ᵢ = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f`. |
| Elastic vs. inelastic vs. perfectly inelastic — what's conserved in each? | **Elastic**: momentum AND kinetic energy conserved. **Inelastic**: only momentum conserved (some KE lost to heat/sound/deformation). **Perfectly inelastic**: objects stick together — momentum conserved, but MOST KE lost of any collision type. |
| Common trap: is kinetic energy always conserved like momentum? | **No.** Momentum is conserved in every isolated-system collision. Kinetic energy is only conserved in ELASTIC collisions — never assume KE conservation unless told the collision is elastic. |
| Common trap: does the "bigger"/heavier object always have more momentum? | No — momentum depends on BOTH mass and velocity. A small, fast object (e.g., a bullet) can have more momentum than a large, slow one. |
| How do I handle direction in a 1D collision? | Pick one direction as positive before starting. Any object moving the opposite way gets a negative velocity in the equation. |

## Summary
Momentum (`p = mv`) is conserved in every isolated collision — that's the
one guarantee that never breaks. Kinetic energy conservation is the
exception, not the rule: only true in elastic collisions. Impulse
(`J = FΔt = Δp`) explains why extending collision time reduces force,
which is the physics behind seatbelts, airbags, and athletic
follow-through. The most common student errors are treating KE as always
conserved, forgetting to assign signs by direction, and judging "more
momentum" by size/mass alone instead of mass × velocity.
