# Unit 4: Momentum — Interactive Quiz (Most-Missed Questions)

Take this quiz without notes. 8/10 (80%) to advance to Unit 5.

---

**Q1.** In an inelastic collision, which quantity is conserved?

A) Kinetic energy only
B) Momentum only
C) Both momentum and kinetic energy
D) Neither

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**B — momentum only.**

This is the single most-missed idea of the unit. Momentum is conserved in
*all* collisions (elastic or inelastic) as long as the system is closed.
Kinetic energy is conserved *only* in elastic collisions — in inelastic
collisions, some KE converts to heat, sound, or deformation.
</details>

**Q2.** A 1000 kg car moving at 20 m/s collides head-on with a 1000 kg car
moving at 20 m/s in the opposite direction, and they stick together. What is
their velocity immediately after the collision?

A) 20 m/s
B) 10 m/s
C) 0 m/s
D) -20 m/s

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C — 0 m/s.**

Using conservation of momentum with opposite directions as opposite signs:
1000(20) + 1000(-20) = (1000+1000)vf → 0 = 2000vf → vf = 0. A common error
is forgetting the sign for the opposite-direction car and adding the
speeds as if both were positive, getting 20 m/s instead.
</details>

**Q3.** Two identical airbags are compared: Airbag A stops a crash-test dummy
in 0.1 s; Airbag B (softer/more padding) stops the same dummy over 0.3 s.
Which airbag results in a smaller force on the dummy, and why?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Airbag B — the one with the longer stopping time.**

The impulse (Δp = mΔv) needed to stop the dummy is the *same* in both
cases — same mass, same velocity change. Since J = FΔt is fixed, a longer
Δt means a smaller average F. This is the core idea behind all crash
padding, airbags, and "rolling with a punch": you can't reduce the impulse,
but you can spread it over more time to reduce peak force.
</details>

**Q4.** A 0.5 kg ball moving at 6 m/s hits a wall and bounces straight back
at 6 m/s (elastic, wall doesn't move). What is the ball's change in
momentum?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Δp = -6 kg·m/s (magnitude 6 kg·m/s, direction reversed).**

Let "toward the wall" be positive. Δp = p_f - p_i = m(v_f - v_i) =
0.5(-6 - 6) = 0.5(-12) = -6 kg·m/s. A very common error is computing
Δp = m(vf) - m(vi) using only the *speed* (6 - 6 = 0), forgetting momentum
is a vector and the ball reversed direction — the ball's momentum did NOT
return to its starting value, it flipped sign.
</details>

**Q5.** A 3 kg cart moving at 4 m/s collides with a stationary 1 kg cart.
After the collision, the 3 kg cart moves at 2 m/s in the same direction.
What is the velocity of the 1 kg cart after the collision?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**6 m/s (same direction).**

3(4) + 1(0) = 3(2) + 1(v₂f) → 12 = 6 + v₂f → v₂f = 6 m/s. Check: is this
elastic or inelastic? Compute KE before (½·3·16=24 J) and after
(½·3·4 + ½·1·36 = 6+18=24 J) — KE is conserved, so this happens to be an
elastic collision.
</details>

**Q6.** True or False: A bullet has less momentum than a truck because it
has so much less mass, regardless of speed.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**False.**

Momentum depends on *both* mass and velocity (p=mv). A small mass at a
very high velocity can have significant, even large, momentum — you cannot
compare momentum from mass alone. This targets the misconception that
momentum is basically "how big/heavy something is."
</details>

---

**Q7.** A 0.15 kg baseball approaches a bat at 30 m/s and leaves the bat at
40 m/s in the opposite direction. If the bat is in contact with the ball for
0.01 s, what average force does the bat exert on the ball?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**≈1050 N.**

Let "incoming direction" be positive. Δp = m(vf - vi) = 0.15(-40 - 30) =
0.15(-70) = -10.5 kg·m/s. F = Δp/Δt = -10.5/0.01 = -1050 N (1050 N in the
direction the ball leaves). The most common error: using vf - vi = 40-30=10
instead of accounting for the direction reversal (-40 vs +30).
</details>

**Q8.** In a system of two colliding objects with no external forces, is it
possible for one object's individual momentum to change during the
collision?

A) No, momentum conservation means nothing changes
B) Yes — each object's momentum can change, but the *total* stays constant
C) No, only kinetic energy can change
D) Yes, but only in elastic collisions

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**B.**

Conservation of momentum applies to the *system total*, not to each
individual object. Internal forces during the collision absolutely change
each object's individual momentum (that's the whole point — they push on
each other) — what stays fixed is the sum across the whole system.
</details>

---

**Q9.** A skater standing still on frictionless ice throws a heavy ball
forward. What happens to the skater?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**The skater moves backward.**

Total momentum starts at zero (both at rest). For total momentum to remain
zero after the throw, the skater must gain momentum equal and opposite to
the ball's momentum — i.e., recoil backward. This is conservation of
momentum applied to a "system starts at rest, then separates" scenario
(the same principle behind rocket propulsion and recoiling guns).
</details>

**Q10.** Which pair of quantities always has the same units in this unit?

A) Momentum and impulse
B) Momentum and force
C) Impulse and kinetic energy
D) Force and kinetic energy

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**A — momentum and impulse.**

Impulse is defined as change in momentum (J = Δp = FΔt), so they share
units: kg·m/s (equivalently, N·s). Students often forget impulse and
momentum are literally the same *kind* of quantity, just describing "before
vs. after" (momentum) vs. "the cause of the change" (impulse).
</details>
