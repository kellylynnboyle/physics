# Unit 4: Momentum — Key Concepts

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:
1. Define momentum and calculate it for single objects.
2. State the law of conservation of momentum and identify when it applies (closed/isolated systems).
3. Distinguish elastic and inelastic collisions, including perfectly inelastic collisions.
4. Solve 1D collision problems (elastic and inelastic) using conservation of momentum.
5. Relate impulse to change in momentum (impulse-momentum theorem) and to force × time.
6. Explain why extending collision time (airbags, padding, follow-through) reduces force.

## Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Momentum (p) | p = mv — a vector quantity combining mass and velocity |
| Impulse (J) | J = FΔt — change in momentum caused by a force acting over a time interval |
| Conservation of momentum | In a closed system with no external net force, total momentum before = total momentum after |
| Elastic collision | Kinetic energy is conserved as well as momentum (objects bounce apart) |
| Inelastic collision | Momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not (some energy converts to heat/sound/deformation) |
| Perfectly inelastic collision | Objects stick together and move as one after collision (maximum KE loss for the given momentum) |
| Closed/isolated system | A system with no net external force acting on it (internal forces, like collision forces, don't change total system momentum) |

## Core Formulas

- p = mv
- Impulse-momentum theorem: J = FΔt = Δp = mΔv
- Conservation of momentum (1D, two objects): m₁v₁ᵢ + m₂v₂ᵢ = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f
- Perfectly inelastic collision (objects combine): m₁v₁ᵢ + m₂v₂ᵢ = (m₁+m₂)vf

## The Big Idea

**Total momentum of an isolated system never changes — collisions only
redistribute it between objects.** This is true whether the collision is
elastic or inelastic. What's *not* conserved in an inelastic collision is
kinetic energy — some of it converts to heat, sound, or permanent
deformation. Momentum conservation and energy conservation are two
*separate* tools, and inelastic collisions only guarantee one of them.

## Common Misconceptions to Address Directly

- Believing momentum is always conserved AND kinetic energy is always conserved in every collision (KE is only conserved in *elastic* collisions).
- Forgetting momentum is a vector — sign/direction matters. A head-on collision with objects moving toward each other requires opposite signs.
- Thinking a bigger force always means bigger impulse — impulse depends on both force *and* the time it acts over (J = FΔt); a smaller force over a longer time can produce the same impulse.
- Believing airbags/padding "reduce the impulse" — they don't; the impulse (change in momentum) is fixed by the mass and velocity change. What padding does is *extend the time* of the collision, which *reduces the force* for that same impulse.
- Assuming momentum is conserved for a single object in isolation without regard to the full system (momentum conservation applies to the *total* system, not each object individually unless no external/internal forces act on it).
