# Unit 5: Work & Energy — Interactive Quiz (Most-Missed Questions)

Take this quiz without notes. This is the final unit quiz — 8/10 (80%) is
also the target for the cumulative review.

---

**Q1.** A person holds a 20 kg box stationary above their head for 30
seconds. How much work do they do on the box, in the physics sense?

A) A large amount, since the box is heavy
B) Zero, since the box doesn't move
C) mgh, using the height they lifted it to
D) It depends on how tired they get

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**B — zero.**

Work requires displacement. Holding something stationary involves zero
displacement, so W = Fd = F(0) = 0, no matter how much force or how much
effort/fatigue is involved. This is the #1 most-missed idea in this unit —
"work" in physics is not the same as "effort."
</details>

---

**Q2.** A waiter carries a tray of food at constant height across a room at
constant velocity. How much work does the waiter's upward-supporting force
do against gravity?

A) A large positive amount
B) A large negative amount
C) Zero
D) Cannot be determined

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**C — zero.**

The supporting force is vertical (upward); the displacement is horizontal.
W = Fd cos(θ) with θ=90° between them → cos(90°)=0 → W=0. This tests the
cos(θ) factor directly: force and displacement being perpendicular means no
work is done by that force, even over a large distance.
</details>

---

**Q3.** A 2 kg ball is dropped from 5 m. Ignoring air resistance, what is its
speed just before hitting the ground? (g=9.8 m/s²)

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**≈9.9 m/s.**

Conservation of mechanical energy: PEi = KEf → mgh = ½mv² → v=√(2gh) =
√(2×9.8×5) = √98 ≈ 9.9 m/s. Notice mass cancels — this connects back to
Unit 3's "objects fall at the same rate regardless of mass" idea.
</details>

---

**Q4.** A roller coaster car (frictionless track) starts at rest at the top
of a 40 m hill. What is its speed at the bottom, regardless of the hill's
shape (straight, curved, looped)?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**≈28 m/s** (v=√(2×9.8×40)=√784=28 m/s).

The key insight tested here: conservation of mechanical energy depends only
on the *height* dropped, not the path/shape taken to get there — this
surprises many students who expect a steeper or longer track to change the
answer.
</details>

---

**Q5.** A crate is pushed 10 m across a floor with 40 N of applied force,
while 15 N of friction opposes the motion. Using the work-energy theorem,
what is the net work done on the crate?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**250 J.**

Wnet = (Fapplied - Ffriction) × d = (40-15) × 10 = 250 J. A common error is
computing work from the applied force alone (400 J) without accounting for
the negative work friction does (-150 J), which must be subtracted to get
net work — this net work is what actually equals ΔKE.
</details>

---

**Q6.** True or False: In a system with friction, mechanical energy (KE+PE)
is still exactly conserved.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**False.**

Friction is a non-conservative force — it removes mechanical energy from
the system, converting it into heat and sound. Total energy (including
thermal) is still conserved, but *mechanical* energy (KE+PE) alone
decreases. This targets confusing "energy is always conserved" (true, for
total energy) with "mechanical energy is always conserved" (false, unless
frictionless/no non-conservative forces).
</details>

---

**Q7.** Two motors each lift an identical 100 kg crate 5 m straight up.
Motor A takes 10 seconds; Motor B takes 2 seconds. Compare the work done and
the power output of each motor.

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Same work (4900 J each); Motor B has 5× the power of Motor A.**

W = mgh = 100×9.8×5 = 4900 J for both — identical work, since the same
force acts over the same displacement. Power = W/t: Motor A = 4900/10 = 490
W; Motor B = 4900/2 = 2450 W. This targets the misconception that "more
powerful" means "more total energy transferred" rather than "same energy,
transferred faster."
</details>

---

**Q8.** A spring-loaded toy launches a 0.1 kg ball straight up, and the ball
reaches a maximum height of 2 m. Using conservation of energy, how much
elastic potential energy was stored in the spring before launch? (Ignore air
resistance; assume all spring PE converts to gravitational PE at max
height.)

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**≈1.96 J.**

PEspring converts entirely to PEgrav at max height (KE=0 at both start —
if spring PE is the only energy — and at the top). PEgrav = mgh = 0.1 ×
9.8 × 2 = 1.96 J, so PEspring ≈ 1.96 J. This introduces the idea that
conservation of energy applies across *any* form of PE, not just
gravitational.
</details>

---

**Q9.** A 5 kg block slides down a frictionless 3 m ramp from rest. Using
the work-energy theorem, what is its speed at the bottom?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**≈7.67 m/s.**

Wnet = Wgravity (only force doing work along the ramp, frictionless) = mgh
= 5×9.8×3 = 147 J (using the *vertical drop*, not the ramp length, unless
they're the same here). Wnet = ΔKE = ½mvf² - 0 → 147 = ½(5)vf² → vf² = 58.8
→ vf ≈ 7.67 m/s. Note: if "3 m" is the ramp *length* on an incline rather
than vertical height, you'd need the angle to find vertical drop — always
check which one a given problem specifies.
</details>

---

**Q10.** A car engine does 50,000 J of work on a car in 5 seconds. What is
the average power output, in Watts and in kilowatts?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**10,000 W = 10 kW.**

P = W/t = 50,000/5 = 10,000 W. Simple plug-in, but frequently missed
because students forget power's units (Watts = Joules/second) and instead
try to compute it using a force and distance formula without first checking
whether time or velocity is actually given.
</details>
