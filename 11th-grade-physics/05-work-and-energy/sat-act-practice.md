# Unit 5: Work & Energy — SAT/ACT Practice

- **SAT Math** turns energy problems into **algebraic substitution and
  square-root equations** (KE=½mv², solve for v).
- **ACT Science** turns it into **energy-transformation data** — usually a
  roller coaster, pendulum, or spring experiment with height/speed data at
  multiple points.

## SAT Math Style

**Q1.** An object has kinetic energy KE = ½mv². If a 2 kg object has 25 J
of kinetic energy, what is its speed?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**5 m/s.**

25 = ½(2)v² → 25 = v² → v = 5 m/s. The most-missed step: forgetting to
take the square root at the end and answering "25" or forgetting that
½(2)=1 simplifies the equation before solving.
</details>

---

**Q2.** A spring stores potential energy given by PE = ½kx², where k is the
spring constant and x is the compression distance. If k = 200 N/m and the
spring is compressed 0.3 m, how much energy is stored?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**9 J.**

PE = ½(200)(0.3²) = ½(200)(0.09) = 9 J. The most common error: squaring
0.3 incorrectly (using 0.6 instead of 0.09), or forgetting the ½.
</details>

---

**Q3.** An object falls from rest and its speed just before landing is
given by v = √(2gh). If the object's speed on landing is 14 m/s and
g = 9.8 m/s², what height did it fall from (nearest meter)?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**≈10 m.**

14 = √(2×9.8×h) → 196 = 19.6h → h = 10 m. This is a "solve for the variable
inside the square root" problem — the most-missed step is squaring both
sides correctly (196, not 14×2=28) before isolating h.
</details>

## ACT Science Reasoning Style

**Q4.** A student rolls a marble down a frictionless track from different
starting heights and measures its speed at the bottom:

| Starting height (m) | Speed at bottom (m/s) |
|---|---|
| 0.2 | 2.0 |
| 0.5 | 3.1 |
| 0.8 | 4.0 |
| 1.2 | 4.8 |

Based on this trend, is the relationship between height and speed linear
or something else?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Not linear — speed increases more slowly as height increases (consistent with v=√(2gh), a square-root relationship, not a straight line).**

Check: height triples from 0.2→0.8 m (4×), but speed only doubles
(2.0→4.0). If it were linear/directly proportional, speed would have
quadrupled too. This "is it actually linear" check is one of the most
commonly missed ACT Science reasoning skills — students often assume any
steadily-increasing table is a straight-line relationship without checking
the ratios.
</details>

---

**Q5.** A second track (also frictionless) is tested with friction added
partway down. At starting height 0.8 m, the marble's speed at the bottom is
now only 3.2 m/s, compared to 4.0 m/s on the frictionless track. What
happened to the "missing" energy?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**It converted to heat and sound from friction — it wasn't destroyed, just no longer part of the marble's mechanical (kinetic) energy.**

This tests the same misconception flagged in the unit quiz — many students
answer "it was lost/destroyed," but total energy is still conserved; only
the *mechanical* energy available to the marble decreased.
</details>

---

**Q6.** Using both tracks' data (Q4 and Q5), a student claims: "Friction
always reduces a rolling object's final speed by exactly 20%, regardless of
starting height." Is this claim supported by the data given?

<details><summary>Show Answer</summary>

**No — only one height (0.8 m) was tested with friction, so there isn't enough data to claim the reduction is "always" 20% at every height.**

This is the ACT Science "overgeneralization" trap: (4.0-3.2)/4.0 = 20% is
correct arithmetic *for that one data point*, but claiming it holds "always"
or "regardless of height" goes beyond what a single comparison can support
— a hallmark most-missed reasoning error on this section.
</details>

---

## Answer Key Summary

1. 5 m/s  2. 9 J  3. ≈10 m  4. Not linear (square-root relationship)
5. Converted to heat/sound, not destroyed  6. No — insufficient data to generalize
