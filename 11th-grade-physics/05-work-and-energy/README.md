# Section 5: Work & Energy

## Overview
Work and energy provide a second, often faster, route to solving motion
problems — instead of tracking forces at every instant (Newton's Laws),
you track energy transformations from start to finish.

## Key Concepts to Master
- **Work**: `W = F·d·cos(θ)`, where θ is the angle between the force and
  the displacement. Work is a scalar and can be positive, negative, or
  zero.
- **Zero work**: if force is perpendicular to displacement (θ = 90°) or
  there is no displacement at all, work is zero — even if a large force
  is being applied (e.g., holding a heavy bag stationary does zero work
  on it).
- **Negative work**: happens when a force opposes the displacement
  (e.g., friction acting on a moving object, or the force you apply while
  lowering something slowly).
- **Kinetic energy**: `KE = ½mv²`, energy of motion. Depends on velocity
  squared — doubling speed quadruples KE.
- **Work-energy theorem**: `W_net = ΔKE`. The net work done on an object
  equals its change in kinetic energy.
- **Gravitational potential energy**: `PE_grav = mgh`, relative to a
  chosen reference height.
- **Elastic potential energy**: `PE_spring = ½kx²`, where k is the spring
  constant and x is displacement from the spring's natural length.
- **Conservation of mechanical energy**: `KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f`
  *only when non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance) do no
  work*. When friction is present, mechanical energy is not conserved —
  some converts to heat: `KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + Energy_lost`.
- **Power**: `P = W/t = ΔEnergy/Δt`, the rate of doing work or
  transferring energy, measured in watts (J/s). Power is not the same
  thing as work or energy.

## Learning Objectives
Students will be able to:
1. Calculate work given force, displacement, and angle, including
   recognizing when work is zero.
2. Apply the work-energy theorem to find final speed or displacement.
3. Calculate kinetic, gravitational, and elastic potential energy.
4. Apply conservation of mechanical energy to frictionless systems, and
   account for energy loss when friction is present.
5. Calculate power and distinguish it from work/energy.

## Practice Problems

1. A person pushes a 20 kg box 5 m across a floor with a constant
   horizontal force of 40 N. How much work is done on the box?
2. A waiter carries a tray of food at constant height and constant
   velocity across a room. How much work does the waiter do on the tray
   (vertically supporting it)?
3. A 2 kg object starts at rest and is acted on by a net force that does
   36 J of work. What is its final speed?
4. A 1500 kg roller coaster car starts from rest at the top of a 40 m
   hill (frictionless track). What is its speed at the bottom?
5. A motor lifts a 50 kg crate 10 m straight up in 5 seconds at constant
   velocity. What power does the motor supply? (g = 9.8 m/s²)

### Answer Key
1. `W = Fd·cos(θ) = 40(5)cos(0°) = 200 J`.
2. **Zero work.** The waiter's supporting force is vertical (upward), but
   the tray's displacement is horizontal — force is perpendicular to
   displacement, so cos(90°) = 0.
3. Work-energy theorem: `W = ΔKE = ½mv² − 0 → 36 = ½(2)v² → v² = 36 →
   v = 6 m/s`.
4. Conservation of energy (frictionless): `mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh) =
   √(2·9.8·40) ≈ 28.0 m/s`. (Mass cancels out.)
5. Work to lift at constant velocity = `mgh = 50(9.8)(10) = 4900 J`.
   `P = W/t = 4900/5 = 980 W`.

## Most Often Missed — see `quiz.html`
The quiz targets: assuming any applied force does work, confusing power
with energy/work, forgetting energy "lost" to friction still has to be
accounted for (not simply ignored), and misapplying `KE = ½mv²` (forgetting
the square).

## Real-World Applications
- **Roller coasters**: designed almost entirely using conservation of
  energy — hill heights determine achievable speeds elsewhere on the
  track.
- **Regenerative braking (electric/hybrid cars)**: converts a car's
  kinetic energy back into stored electrical energy instead of losing it
  all to heat via friction brakes.
- **Household appliances**: power ratings (watts) tell you the rate of
  energy use, which combined with time gives total energy (and cost) —
  the basis of electricity bills.
- **Weightlifting & biomechanics**: work done against gravity when
  lifting a weight, and power output during explosive lifts.

## Assessment Criteria
- **Mastery**: correctly identifies zero/negative work situations, applies
  energy conservation with friction losses accounted for, and
  distinguishes power from energy in context.
- **Proficient**: solves standard work-energy and conservation problems
  correctly on frictionless systems; needs support incorporating friction
  losses.
- **Developing**: assumes any applied force does positive work regardless
  of angle, or confuses power (rate) with energy (total amount).
