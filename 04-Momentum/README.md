# Unit 4: Momentum

**NGSS alignment:** HS-PS2-2 (use mathematical representations to support the claim that the total momentum of a system is conserved when there is no net force on the system), HS-PS2-3 (design/evaluate a solution to minimize forces on a macroscopic object during a collision).

**Suggested pacing:** 1.5 weeks (7–8 class periods), including 1 lab day (collision cart lab) and 1 review/assessment day.

**Prerequisite:** Unit 3 — Newton's Laws (especially the 2nd and 3rd Laws, which underlie the impulse-momentum theorem and conservation of momentum).

## 1. Key Concepts Students Must Master

| # | Concept | Why it matters |
|---|---------|-----------------|
| 1 | Momentum | `p = mv`. A vector quantity — direction matters just as much as magnitude. |
| 2 | Impulse | `J = FΔt = Δp`. A force applied over time changes momentum; impulse equals the area under a Force-vs-time graph. |
| 3 | Impulse-Momentum Theorem | Connects force, time, and change in momentum — explains why airbags and padding "work" (increasing Δt reduces peak force). |
| 4 | Conservation of Momentum | In a closed/isolated system with no external net force, total momentum before = total momentum after. |
| 5 | Elastic collisions | Both momentum AND kinetic energy are conserved; objects bounce apart. |
| 6 | Inelastic collisions | Momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is NOT (some converts to heat/sound/deformation); objects may stick together (perfectly inelastic). |
| 7 | Explosions/separations | A reverse-collision scenario — objects start together (combined momentum = 0 or some initial value) and fly apart, still conserving total momentum. |
| 8 | Sign conventions in 1-D collisions | Must assign positive/negative directions consistently — a common source of errors when objects move toward each other. |

## 5. Practice Problems (progression: easy → hard)

1. A 1200 kg car travels at 15 m/s. What is its momentum?
2. A 0.5 kg ball moving at 6 m/s is struck by a bat and leaves at 10 m/s in the opposite direction. What impulse did the bat deliver? If contact lasted 0.02 s, what average force was applied?
3. A 2 kg cart moving at 3 m/s collides and sticks to a stationary 4 kg cart (perfectly inelastic). Find their combined velocity after the collision.
4. Using problem 3, calculate the kinetic energy before and after the collision. Was kinetic energy conserved? Explain.
5. **Multi-step:** A 60 kg astronaut at rest relative to their 200 kg spacecraft pushes off, moving away at 2 m/s. Find the recoil velocity of the spacecraft.
6. **Challenge:** A 3 kg ball moving at 4 m/s (east) collides elastically with a stationary 3 kg ball. Predict and explain the resulting velocities of both balls (equal-mass elastic collision special case).

## 6. Real-World Applications

- **Car safety design:** crumple zones and airbags increase collision time (Δt) to reduce peak force on occupants (impulse-momentum theorem).
- **Rocket propulsion:** conservation of momentum explains how expelling mass (exhaust) in one direction propels the rocket in the other.
- **Sports:** follow-through in swinging a bat or golf club increases contact time, increasing impulse and ball speed.
- **Billiards/pool:** elastic-collision approximations used to predict how balls scatter after impact.
- **Rocket sled/crash testing:** engineers use momentum conservation to design safety barriers that absorb impulse.

## 7. Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- [ ] Calculate momentum and impulse, correctly treating both as vector quantities with sign.
- [ ] Apply the impulse-momentum theorem to relate force, time, and change in momentum.
- [ ] Apply conservation of momentum to solve 1-D collision and explosion problems.
- [ ] Distinguish elastic from inelastic collisions and determine whether kinetic energy is conserved in a given scenario.
- [ ] Explain real-world safety design (airbags, crumple zones) using the impulse-momentum theorem.

## 8. Assessment Criteria

| Level | Criteria |
|-------|----------|
| **Exceeds (4)** | Solves multi-object collision and explosion problems with correct sign conventions; explains and quantitatively verifies whether KE is conserved in a given collision. |
| **Meets (3)** | Correctly applies conservation of momentum to standard 1-D collision problems (elastic and perfectly inelastic); calculates impulse from force and time. |
| **Approaching (2)** | Sets up conservation of momentum equations but makes sign errors on direction, or confuses conservation of momentum with conservation of KE. |
| **Beginning (1)** | Treats momentum as a scalar; cannot set up a conservation of momentum equation without significant support. |
