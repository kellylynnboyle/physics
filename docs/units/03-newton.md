## Key concepts

- **Force:** A push or pull; a vector measured in newtons (1 N = 1 kg·m/s²).
- **Net force (ΣF):** Vector sum of all forces on an object. Determines acceleration.
- **Inertia / 1st law:** An object keeps its velocity (including rest) unless the net force is not zero.
- **2nd law:** a = ΣF / m.
- **3rd law:** Forces come in pairs: A on B equals B on A in size, opposite in direction, on different objects.
- **Weight vs. mass:** Mass (kg) is the amount of matter; weight (N) is the gravitational force: W = mg.
- **Normal & friction:** Normal force is perpendicular to the surface. Friction opposes sliding: f = μN.

## Key equations

- `ΣF = m·a`
- `W = m·g`
- `f_k = μ_k·N ;  f_s ≤ μ_s·N`
- `On an incline: W∥ = mg·sinθ ;  N = mg·cosθ`
- `Terminal velocity: when drag = weight, a = 0`

## Cornell notes

**Topic:** Newton's laws — forces cause changes in motion  
**Name:** ____________  **Date:** ____________

| Cue / question | Notes |
|---|---|
| 1st law (inertia)? | If ΣF = 0, velocity is constant (at rest OR moving in a straight line at constant speed). Forces are not needed to keep an object moving — only to change its motion. |
| 2nd law? | ΣF = ma, so a = ΣF/m. Same force: bigger mass → smaller acceleration. Direction of a = direction of net force. 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². |
| 3rd law? | Action–reaction pairs: equal magnitude, opposite direction, act on DIFFERENT objects, same type of force. Because they act on different objects they never cancel. |
| How do I draw a free-body diagram (FBD)? | Pick ONE object, draw it as a dot/box, draw only forces ON it (not forces it exerts). Label each (W or F_g, N, T, f, F_app). Choose axes; arrows' lengths show relative size. |
| Mass vs. weight | Mass: kg, doesn't change with location. Weight: W = mg in newtons, changes with g (Earth 9.8, Moon ≈ 1.6 m/s²). 60 kg on the Moon still has 60 kg of mass. |
| Normal force | Perpendicular to the surface; it adjusts to prevent objects from passing through. N = mg only on a flat surface with no other vertical forces. On an incline N = mg cosθ. In an accelerating elevator N changes (apparent weight). |
| Friction | Opposes relative motion/attempted motion. Static f_s ≤ μ_s N (adjusts up to a max). Kinetic f_k = μ_k N (constant), usually μ_k < μ_s. Does not depend on contact area. |
| Solving ΣF problems | 1) FBD. 2) Write ΣF = ma for each axis (+ direction of acceleration). 3) Substitute. 4) Solve for the unknown. Equilibrium: ΣF = 0 → a = 0. |
| Incline | Tilt axes: x along the slope, y perpendicular. Component of weight down the slope = mg sinθ; into the slope = mg cosθ. Frictionless: a = g sinθ. |

**Summary:** Forces are interactions measured in newtons. An unbalanced force changes velocity according to ΣF = ma; balanced forces mean constant velocity (including rest). Every force has an equal and opposite partner on a different object. Free-body diagrams turn a story into equations, and weight (mg) is a force that is not the same thing as mass.

## Quiz: most-missed questions

_Interactive version: open `site/index.html`. Answers are at the end of this section._

1. An object moves at constant velocity along a straight line. What must be true about the net force on it?
   - A. It points in the direction of motion
   - B. It is zero
   - C. It equals the object's weight
   - D. It is constant but not zero
2. A large truck collides head-on with a small car. During the collision, which exerts the greater force?
   - A. The truck on the car
   - B. The car on the truck
   - C. They exert equal forces
   - D. Depends on who was moving faster
3. A 60 kg astronaut travels to the Moon (g ≈ 1.6 m/s²). What happens to the astronaut's mass and weight?
   - A. Both stay the same
   - B. Mass stays 60 kg; weight ≈ 96 N
   - C. Mass ≈ 10 kg; weight ≈ 96 N
   - D. Mass stays 60 kg; weight stays 588 N
4. A 10 kg box is pulled to the right with 50 N while friction pushes left with 20 N. What is the acceleration?
   - A. 5 m/s²
   - B. 3 m/s²
   - C. 7 m/s²
   - D. 2 m/s²
5. You stand on a scale in an elevator that is accelerating upward. The scale reading (normal force) is…
   - A. less than your weight
   - B. equal to your weight
   - C. greater than your weight
   - D. zero
6. A book rests on a table. Which force is the Newton's 3rd-law partner of the book's weight (Earth pulling the book)?
   - A. The normal force from the table on the book
   - B. The book pulling up on the Earth
   - C. The table pushing on the floor
   - D. Friction
7. The same net force is applied to two carts. Cart B has twice the mass of cart A. How do their accelerations compare?
   - A. B has twice the acceleration
   - B. They are equal
   - C. B has half the acceleration
   - D. B has four times the acceleration
8. A block slides down a frictionless incline of angle 30°. What is its acceleration? (g = 9.8 m/s²)
   - A. 9.8 m/s²
   - B. 8.5 m/s²
   - C. 4.9 m/s²
   - D. 0 m/s²

<details><summary>Answer key and explanations</summary>

1. **B** — Constant velocity means a = 0, so by ΣF = ma the net force is zero. Motion does not require a net forward force. _(Misconception: Thinking force is needed to maintain motion.)_
2. **C** — Newton's 3rd law: the forces are equal and opposite. The car has the larger acceleration only because it has smaller mass. _(Misconception: Confusing force with effect (acceleration or damage).)_
3. **B** — Mass is intrinsic (60 kg). Weight = mg = 60 × 1.6 = 96 N, much less than on Earth (588 N). _(Misconception: Using 'weight' and 'mass' interchangeably.)_
4. **B** — ΣF = 50 − 20 = 30 N. a = 30/10 = 3 m/s². Use net force, not just the applied force. _(Misconception: Dividing the applied force by mass and ignoring friction.)_
5. **C** — ΣF = N − mg = ma upward, so N = m(g + a) > mg. The scale reads more than normal. _(Misconception: Assuming N always equals mg.)_
6. **B** — Action–reaction pairs are the same type of force acting on different objects: Earth pulls book ↔ book pulls Earth. The normal force just balances weight on the same object. _(Misconception: Calling the normal force the 'reaction' to weight.)_
7. **C** — a = F/m, so doubling the mass halves the acceleration (inverse relationship). _(Misconception: Mixing direct and inverse proportionality.)_
8. **C** — a = g sinθ = 9.8 × sin30° = 4.9 m/s². Only the component of weight along the slope accelerates the block. _(Misconception: Using g (or g cosθ) instead of g sinθ.)_

</details>

## Practice problems

1. A 1200 kg car experiences a net force of 3000 N. What is its acceleration?
2. A 5.0 kg block is pulled by 30 N across a surface with 10 N friction. Acceleration?
3. What is the weight of a 70 kg student on Earth? On Mars (g = 3.7 m/s²)?
4. An 80 kg person rides an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s². What does the scale read?
5. A 10 kg block on a frictionless 30° incline. Acceleration and normal force?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. a = 3000/1200 = 2.5 m/s²
2. ΣF = 20 N; a = 20/5.0 = 4.0 m/s²
3. Earth: 70 × 9.8 = 686 N; Mars: 70 × 3.7 = 259 N; mass is 70 kg in both
4. N = m(g + a) = 80(9.8 + 2.0) = 944 N
5. a = g sin30° = 4.9 m/s²; N = mg cos30° = 10(9.8)(0.866) ≈ 85 N

</details>

## Lab / investigation

Atwood machine or cart-and-hanging-mass lab: vary net force with fixed mass and vary mass with fixed force; graph a vs. F and a vs. 1/m to support ΣF = ma (HS-PS2-1 data analysis).

## Real-world applications

- Seat belts and headrests: inertia in sudden stops.
- Rocket propulsion and swimming: action–reaction pairs.
- Tire design, brakes and ABS: static vs. kinetic friction.
- Elevators and roller coasters: apparent weight changes.
- Parachutes and terminal velocity: balanced forces at constant speed.
