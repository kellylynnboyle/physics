# Unit 3: Newton's Laws

**Pacing:** 4 weeks  |  **NGSS:** HS-PS2-1

> **Essential question:** How do forces change the motion of objects?

*Core unit for HS-PS2-1 (Newton's second law from data). Third law connects to HS-PS2-2 and HS-PS2-3.*

## 1. Learning objectives

- [ ] State Newton's three laws and identify real examples of each.
- [ ] Draw free-body diagrams showing all forces on one object.
- [ ] Use ΣF = ma to find acceleration, force, or mass, including friction, tension, and normal force.
- [ ] Identify Newton's third-law force pairs (different objects, equal and opposite).
- [ ] Analyze lab data (force vs. acceleration, mass vs. acceleration) to support ΣF = ma.

## 2. Key concepts

| Concept | What students must master |
|---|---|
| **Force and net force** | A force is a push or pull (vector, newtons). Net force ΣF is the vector sum of all forces on an object. |
| **First law (inertia)** | An object keeps its velocity (including rest) unless a net external force acts on it. Mass measures inertia. |
| **Second law** | a = ΣF/m: acceleration is proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass, in the direction of net force. |
| **Third law** | If A pushes on B, B pushes on A with an equal-magnitude, opposite force. The forces act on DIFFERENT objects and never cancel. |
| **Common forces** | Weight W = mg (down); normal N (perpendicular to the surface); friction f ≤ μN (opposes sliding); tension T (along ropes). |
| **Mass vs. weight** | Mass (kg) is the amount of matter and doesn't change. Weight (N) is the gravitational force and depends on location. |
| **Free-body diagram (FBD)** | Dot for the object; arrows for every force acting ON it; none for forces it exerts on others. |

**Key formulas:** `ΣF = ma`  ·  `W = mg`  ·  `f_k = μ_k N`  ·  `a = ΣF / m`

## 3. Cornell notes (cue column / notes column)

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is Newton's 1st law? | Inertia: constant velocity (or rest) unless net force ≠ 0. 'No motion change' ≠ 'no forces' — just balanced forces. |
| What is Newton's 2nd law? | ΣF = ma. Double the force → double a. Double the mass → half a. 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². |
| What is Newton's 3rd law? | Force pairs are equal, opposite, same type, and on DIFFERENT objects. Truck–car collision: forces on each are equal in size. |
| How do I make a free-body diagram? | 1) Isolate object. 2) Draw weight down. 3) Add contact forces (normal, friction, tension, applied). 4) Pick axes. 5) Write ΣF in each direction. |
| Mass vs. weight? | Mass: kg, constant. Weight: mg in N; on the Moon g ≈ 1.6 m/s² so weight is ~1/6. |
| What is friction? | Opposes relative motion/attempted motion. Static f_s ≤ μ_s N (matches applied force until it slips). Kinetic f_k = μ_k N (constant). |
| Apparent weight in an elevator? | N = m(g + a) when accelerating upward (feel heavier), N = m(g − a) when accelerating downward. At constant velocity N = mg. |
| Terminal velocity? | Drag grows with speed until drag = weight; net force = 0 so a = 0 and speed is constant (not zero!). |

**Summary (write in your own words):** Forces cause changes in velocity (acceleration), not velocity itself. Always draw a free-body diagram, add forces as vectors to find ΣF, then apply ΣF = ma. Third-law pairs act on different objects.

## 4. Most-missed questions (self-quiz)

Try each question before opening the answer. The interactive version is in `site/index.html`.

**1. A hockey puck slides at constant velocity across frictionless ice. The net force on the puck is:**

   - A. in the direction of motion
   - B. zero
   - C. equal to its weight
   - D. decreasing over time

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Constant velocity means a = 0, so ΣF = 0. No force is needed to maintain motion.  
*Common mistake:* Believing motion requires a continuing push (Aristotelian idea).

</details>

**2. A book rests on a table. Which force is the Newton's third-law partner of the book's weight (Earth's pull on the book)?**

   - A. the table's normal force on the book
   - B. the book's pull on Earth
   - C. the book's push on the table
   - D. friction

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Third-law pairs: Earth pulls book ↔ book pulls Earth. Weight and normal force act on the same object, so they are not a pair.  
*Common mistake:* Treating the normal force as the 'reaction' to weight.

</details>

**3. A large truck collides head-on with a small car. During the collision, the force on the car is ___ the force on the truck.**

   - A. greater than
   - B. less than
   - C. equal in magnitude to
   - D. zero compared with

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Third law: forces are equal and opposite. The car's larger acceleration comes from its smaller mass.  
*Common mistake:* Thinking the bigger object exerts a bigger force.

</details>

**4. A 10 N net force acts on a 2 kg cart. What is its acceleration?**

   - A. 0.2 m/s²
   - B. 5 m/s²
   - C. 20 m/s²
   - D. 12 m/s²

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** a = F/m = 10/2 = 5 m/s².  
*Common mistake:* Multiplying instead of dividing (or inverting the ratio).

</details>

**5. An astronaut has a mass of 80 kg on Earth. On the Moon, her mass is:**

   - A. about 13 kg
   - B. 80 kg
   - C. about 130 kg
   - D. zero

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Mass doesn't change with location; weight does.  
*Common mistake:* Confusing mass with weight.

</details>

**6. You stand on a scale in an elevator that is accelerating UPWARD. The scale reading is:**

   - A. less than mg
   - B. equal to mg
   - C. greater than mg
   - D. zero

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** N − mg = ma (a up) → N = m(g + a) > mg.  
*Common mistake:* Assuming the reading always equals weight.

</details>

**7. A 20 kg crate is pushed across a floor at constant speed with a 50 N horizontal force. The friction force on the crate is:**

   - A. 0 N
   - B. 50 N
   - C. 196 N
   - D. less than 50 N

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Constant speed → ΣF = 0, so friction balances the 50 N push.  
*Common mistake:* Computing friction from weight when the problem gives balanced-force information.

</details>

**8. The same net force is applied to two carts. Cart B has twice the mass of cart A. Cart B's acceleration is:**

   - A. twice cart A's
   - B. half of cart A's
   - C. the same as cart A's
   - D. four times cart A's

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** a = F/m → doubling m halves a.  
*Common mistake:* Mixing up direct and inverse proportionality.

</details>

**9. A skydiver reaches terminal velocity. At that moment:**

   - A. the net force is downward
   - B. the net force is zero
   - C. the skydiver stops falling
   - D. air resistance is zero

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Drag equals weight, so ΣF = 0 and velocity is constant (but not zero).  
*Common mistake:* Thinking terminal velocity means no motion or no forces.

</details>

**10. Which free-body diagram for a box sliding down a rough incline is correct?**

   - A. weight, normal, and a 'force of motion' down the slope
   - B. weight, normal, and friction up the slope
   - C. weight and normal only
   - D. weight, normal, friction up the slope, and a 'force of the push' down the slope

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Include only real forces from other objects: gravity, normal, friction. There is no 'force of motion'.  
*Common mistake:* Adding a phantom 'force of motion' to an FBD.

</details>

## 5. Common misconceptions to watch for

- Motion requires a continuing force.
- Normal force and weight are a third-law pair (they act on the same object).
- Bigger objects exert bigger forces in a collision.
- Mass and weight are the same thing.

## 6. Practice problems

Show: knowns, unknown, equation, substitution with units, and a reasonableness check.

1. A 1200 kg car experiences a net force of 3000 N. Find its acceleration.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>a = 2.5 m/s²</details>

2. A 5.0 kg box is pulled with 30 N while friction is 10 N. Find a.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>ΣF = 20 N → a = 4.0 m/s²</details>

3. What is the weight of a 70 kg student on Earth and on the Moon (g = 1.62 m/s²)?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>686 N on Earth; ≈ 113 N on the Moon. Mass stays 70 kg.</details>

4. A 60 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s². What does the scale read?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>N = 60(9.8 + 2.0) = 708 N</details>

5. A frictionless 30° incline: what is a block's acceleration?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>a = g sin30° = 4.9 m/s² down the slope</details>

6. A 2 kg block touches a 3 kg block on frictionless ice. A 10 N push is applied to the 2 kg block. Find a and the contact force on the 3 kg block.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>a = 2 m/s²; force on 3 kg block = 6 N</details>

## 8. Real-world applications

- Automotive safety: seat belts (inertia), crumple zones and ABS (friction control).
- Aerospace: rocket thrust and reaction forces; astronaut weightlessness vs. mass.
- Sports: starting blocks (third law), skiing and sled friction, parachute terminal velocity.
- Amusement-park rides: apparent weight in roller coasters and drop towers.
