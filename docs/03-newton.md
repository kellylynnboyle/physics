# 3. Newton's Laws

*Suggested pacing: ~4 weeks* · *Big idea:* Forces cause changes in motion: the net force on an object determines its acceleration.

## California NGSS performance expectations

- **HS-PS2-1** — Analyze data to support the claim that Newton's second law of motion describes the mathematical relationship among the net force on a macroscopic object, its mass, and its acceleration.
- **HS-PS2-4** — Use mathematical representations of Newton's Law of Gravitation and Coulomb's Law to describe and predict the gravitational and electrostatic forces between objects.

- *Science & Engineering Practices:* Analyzing data; using mathematics; constructing explanations.
- *Crosscutting Concepts:* Cause and effect; systems; patterns.

## Learning objectives (students can…)

- [ ] Draw accurate free-body diagrams for objects at rest, at constant velocity, and accelerating.
- [ ] Apply ΣF = ma to one- and two-dimensional problems (including inclines and friction).
- [ ] Identify third-law pairs and explain why they don't cancel.
- [ ] Distinguish mass, weight, and normal force.
- [ ] Use experimental data to show a ∝ F and a ∝ 1/m (HS-PS2-1).
- [ ] Use F = Gm₁m₂/r² to predict how gravitational force changes with mass and distance (HS-PS2-4).

## Key concepts

| Concept | What to master |
|---|---|
| **Force & free-body diagrams** | A force is a push or pull (vector, newtons). FBDs show all forces acting ON one object. |
| **1st Law (inertia)** | Net force = 0 → constant velocity (including at rest). Mass measures inertia. |
| **2nd Law** | a = F_net / m, or F_net = ma. Acceleration is in the direction of the net force. |
| **3rd Law** | Forces come in equal-and-opposite pairs acting on DIFFERENT objects. |
| **Weight & normal force** | Weight W = mg (a force; changes with location). Normal force is perpendicular to the surface, not always equal to mg. |
| **Friction** | Static: up to μₛN. Kinetic: f = μₖN, opposing motion. |
| **Inclines** | Along the slope: mg sinθ. Perpendicular: N = mg cosθ. |
| **Universal gravitation** | F = Gm₁m₂/r². (Coulomb's Law has the same form: F = kq₁q₂/r², which we revisit in Semester 2.) |

**Equations:** `ΣF = ma` · `W = mg` · `f_k = μₖN` · `f_s ≤ μₛN` · `F_∥ = mg sinθ` · `N = mg cosθ (incline)` · `F_g = G m₁m₂ / r²`

**Vocabulary:** force, net force, inertia, mass, weight, normal force, friction, free-body diagram, terminal velocity, action–reaction pair

## Cornell Notes: Newton's Laws

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| Newton's 1st Law? | An object keeps its velocity (rest or constant velocity) unless a net external force acts. Motion at constant velocity does NOT require a net force. |
| Newton's 2nd Law? | ΣF = ma. Find the NET force (vector sum), divide by mass. Doubling the force doubles a; doubling the mass halves a. |
| Newton's 3rd Law? | If A pushes on B, B pushes on A with equal size, opposite direction. The pair acts on different objects, so they never cancel each other in one FBD. |
| Mass vs. weight? | Mass (kg) = amount of matter, doesn't change. Weight (N) = gravitational force = mg. Moon: g ≈ 1.6 m/s². |
| How do I draw an FBD? | Isolate one object, draw a dot, draw arrows for every force ON it (gravity, normal, friction, tension, applied). Label and pick axes. |
| What does friction do? | Opposes sliding. f_k = μₖN. N depends on the situation (incline, extra pushes, elevators), not always mg. |
| Inclines? | Tilt the axes: along the ramp and perpendicular to it. Gravity component along = mg sinθ; N = mg cosθ. |

**Summary:** Draw an FBD, resolve forces, sum them on each axis, and apply ΣF = ma. Third-law pairs act on different bodies; only forces on the same body are added.

## Most-missed questions quiz (answer key)

*Take the interactive version in `site/index.html`.*

**1. A hockey puck slides across frictionless ice at constant velocity. What is the net horizontal force on it?**

- a. Zero  ✅
- b. Equal to its weight
- c. Equal to its mass times its velocity
- d. A forward force equal to the motion

*Why:* Constant velocity means a = 0, so by Newton's 1st/2nd Law the net force is zero. No forward 'force of motion' is needed.  
*Common misconception:* Motion requires a force.

**2. A truck collides head-on with a small insect. Which statement about the collision forces is correct?**

- a. They are equal in magnitude  ✅
- b. The truck exerts a larger force
- c. The insect exerts a larger force
- d. Only the truck exerts a force

*Why:* Third-law pairs are equal and opposite. The insect's acceleration is far larger only because its mass is tiny.  
*Common misconception:* Bigger objects exert bigger forces in a collision.

**3. A 60 kg astronaut goes to the Moon, where g ≈ 1.6 m/s². What are the astronaut's mass and weight there?**

- a. 60 kg and 96 N  ✅
- b. 10 kg and 96 N
- c. 60 kg and 588 N
- d. 10 kg and 588 N

*Why:* Mass doesn't change (60 kg). Weight = mg = 60 × 1.6 = 96 N.  
*Common misconception:* Mass and weight are the same, or mass changes with location.

**4. A book rests on a table. Which pair is a Newton's Third Law pair?**

- a. The book pushes down on the table; the table pushes up on the book  ✅
- b. Gravity pulls the book down; the table pushes the book up
- c. Gravity on the book; normal force on the book
- d. Weight of the book; force of the table on the floor

*Why:* A third-law pair is the same interaction (book–table) with forces on different objects. Weight and normal are both on the book, so they're not a pair (they only cancel because a = 0).  
*Common misconception:* Weight and normal force are action–reaction pairs.

**5. A net force of 10 N acts on a 2.0 kg cart. What is its acceleration?**

- a. 5 m/s²  ✅
- b. 20 m/s²
- c. 0.2 m/s²
- d. 12 m/s²

*Why:* a = F/m = 10/2.0 = 5 m/s².  
*Common misconception:* Multiplying instead of dividing (a = Fm).

**6. A 5 kg block slides down a frictionless 30° incline. What is its acceleration?**

- a. 4.9 m/s²  ✅
- b. 9.8 m/s²
- c. 8.5 m/s²
- d. 24.5 m/s²

*Why:* a = g sinθ = 9.8 × 0.5 = 4.9 m/s². The mass cancels out.  
*Common misconception:* Acceleration on a ramp equals g, or depends on mass.

**7. A 70 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s². What does the scale read?**

- a. About 826 N  ✅
- b. 686 N
- c. 546 N
- d. 140 N

*Why:* N − mg = ma → N = m(g + a) = 70(9.8 + 2.0) = 826 N. Upward acceleration makes you feel heavier.  
*Common misconception:* The scale always reads mg.

**8. A 20 kg crate is pushed on a floor with a 50 N force. μₖ = 0.20. What is its acceleration?**

- a. About 0.54 m/s²  ✅
- b. 2.5 m/s²
- c. 1.96 m/s²
- d. 0 m/s² (it won't move)

*Why:* f = μₖmg = 0.20 × 20 × 9.8 = 39.2 N. Net = 50 − 39.2 = 10.8 N. a = 10.8/20 ≈ 0.54 m/s².  
*Common misconception:* Ignoring friction or forgetting to subtract it from the applied force.

## Practice problems

1. A 1200 kg car's engine produces 3000 N of forward force against 600 N of friction. Find the acceleration.
   - *Answer:* a = 2.0 m/s².
2. Find the mass and weight of a 70 kg astronaut on Earth (g = 9.8) and on the Moon (g = 1.6).
   - *Answer:* Mass 70 kg both; weight 686 N on Earth, 112 N on the Moon.
3. A 10 kg crate slides down a frictionless 25° ramp. Find its acceleration and the normal force.
   - *Answer:* a ≈ 4.14 m/s²; N ≈ 88.8 N.
4. A 20 N horizontal push is applied to a 3 kg block touching a 2 kg block on frictionless ice. Find a and the force the 3 kg block exerts on the 2 kg block.
   - *Answer:* a = 4 m/s²; contact force = 8 N.
5. A skydiver (80 kg) reaches terminal velocity. Draw the FBD and state the drag force.
   - *Answer:* Net force = 0; drag = weight = 784 N upward.

## Labs & activities

- Cart + hanging mass: vary force and mass, graph a vs. F and a vs. 1/m to test Newton's 2nd Law (HS-PS2-1).
- Friction lab: measure μₛ and μₖ by pulling a block with a spring scale; or tilting a ramp.
- Elevator or phyphox lab: record apparent weight changes.
- Balloon/bottle-rocket: identify 3rd-law pairs.

## Real-world applications

- Seat belts and headrests (inertia).
- Rocket and jet propulsion (3rd Law).
- Elevator and roller-coaster 'g-forces'.
- Tire tread, ABS brakes, and wet-road friction.
- Orbits and satellites: gravity as a force at a distance.
