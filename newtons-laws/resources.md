# Newton's Laws — Practice Problems, Resources & Applications

## Practice problems

### Level 1 — Direct substitution
1. A 5.0 kg box sits on a level, frictionless table. A horizontal force of
   15 N is applied. Find its acceleration.
2. Calculate the weight (in N) of a 60 kg student (a) on Earth
   (g = 9.8 m/s²) and (b) on the Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²). State their mass in
   both cases.
3. A 2.0 kg book rests on a level table. Draw its free-body diagram and
   find the normal force.

### Level 2 — Multi-step / choose your approach
4. A 10 kg crate on a level floor has a coefficient of static friction
   μₛ = 0.4 and kinetic friction μₖ = 0.3 with the floor. (a) What is the
   maximum static friction force before it starts to slide? (b) Once
   sliding, what is the kinetic friction force? (c) If a 30 N horizontal
   push is applied, does the crate move — and if so, what's its
   acceleration?
5. A 70 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator. Find the scale
   reading (apparent weight) when the elevator (a) is at rest, (b)
   accelerates upward at 2.0 m/s², (c) accelerates downward at 2.0 m/s².
6. A 4.0 kg block slides down a frictionless 30° incline. Find (a) the
   normal force, (b) the acceleration down the slope, (c) the speed after
   sliding 2.0 m from rest.

### Level 3 — Connected systems
7. Two blocks, 3.0 kg and 5.0 kg, are connected by a light string over a
   frictionless, massless pulley (an Atwood machine), hanging on either
   side. Find the acceleration of the system and the tension in the
   string.
8. A 6.0 kg block on a frictionless table is connected by a string over a
   pulley at the table's edge to a hanging 2.0 kg block. Find the
   acceleration of the system and the tension in the string.

### Challenge
9. A 4.0 kg block is on a 20° incline with coefficient of kinetic
   friction μₖ = 0.25 between block and surface. Find the block's
   acceleration as it slides down the incline, showing both the parallel
   and perpendicular gravity components and the friction force.

*(Answer keys are intentionally omitted here — have students show full
free-body diagram/axis choice/ΣF = ma/solve-with-units work and check
answers with an instructor or the quiz in `quiz.html`.)*

## Real-world applications

- **Car crash safety and crumple zones**: crumple zones extend the time
  over which a car decelerates in a collision, and by Newton's Third Law,
  the car's structure absorbing and deforming reduces the force
  transmitted back to the occupants.
- **Rocket propulsion**: a rocket expels exhaust gas downward/backward at
  high speed; by the Third Law, the gas pushes the rocket forward with
  equal and opposite force — no air or ground to "push off" is needed.
- **Tire design and ABS brakes**: tire tread patterns and ABS systems are
  engineered to keep a tire in the higher-friction static (rolling, not
  sliding) regime during braking rather than letting it slip into the
  lower kinetic-friction regime, which shortens stopping distance.
- **Elevator design**: engineers must account for apparent weight
  (normal force from the floor) changing during acceleration/
  deceleration phases, both for passenger comfort and for structural and
  cable-tension specifications.
- **Inclined conveyor belts**: the maximum incline angle a belt can use
  without packages sliding back depends on the balance between mg sinθ
  (pulling the package down the slope) and the available static friction
  force.
- **Seatbelts and inertia**: a seatbelt provides the unbalanced force
  needed to decelerate a passenger's body during a sudden stop; without
  it, Newton's First Law means the body keeps moving forward at the
  pre-crash speed.

## Study tips for the most commonly missed question types

- **"Constant velocity means no forces are acting"** — wrong; it means
  the forces are *balanced* (net force = zero). Check for equilibrium,
  not the absence of any forces.
- **Third Law pairing** — ask "what type of force is this, and what two
  objects are touching (or interacting at a distance)?" The reaction is
  always the *same type* of force, on the *other* object. Gravity on the
  book and the normal force from the table are two *different* force
  types acting on the *same* object (the book) — that's a balanced pair,
  not a Third Law pair.
- **Normal force is not automatically mg** — redraw the free-body diagram
  every time and sum forces in the direction perpendicular to the
  surface; solve for N, don't assume it.
- **Static friction is a range, not a fixed value** — it equals whatever
  force is needed to prevent sliding, up to μₛN. Only use f = μₛN when
  the problem specifies the object is on the verge of slipping.
- **sinθ vs. cosθ on an incline** — the component of gravity *along* the
  slope (causing acceleration down the incline) is mg sinθ; the component
  *into* the slope (balanced by the normal force) is mg cosθ. Sketch the
  right triangle each time rather than memorizing which is which.
