# Unit 3: Newton's Laws

Newton's Laws connect **forces** to **motion** — the "why" behind the kinematics
you already studied.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

1. State and apply Newton's 1st, 2nd, and 3rd Laws.
2. Draw correct free-body diagrams for objects in a variety of scenarios
   (inclines, connected objects, elevators, applied forces at angles).
3. Apply F_net = ma to solve for unknown forces, masses, or accelerations.
4. Distinguish mass from weight, and calculate friction forces (static and kinetic).
5. Correctly identify Newton's 3rd-law force pairs and explain why they never
   cancel each other out (they act on different objects).

## Key concepts to master

- **1st Law (Inertia)**: an object's velocity stays constant unless acted on by
  a net external force. "At rest" and "constant velocity" are both equilibrium
  states — zero net force does **not** mean zero velocity.
- **2nd Law**: F_net = ma. This is the **net** (vector sum of all) force — you
  must add up every force acting on the object, not just one of them.
- **3rd Law**: for every force object A exerts on object B, object B exerts an
  equal and opposite force on A. These two forces act on **different objects**
  and therefore **never cancel** in a single free-body diagram.
- **Mass vs. weight**: mass (kg) is the amount of matter (constant everywhere);
  weight (N) = mg, a force that changes with local gravity.
- **Normal force is not always equal to weight** — only in the simple case of a
  flat surface with no other vertical forces. On an incline, or with an applied
  force with a vertical component, or in an accelerating elevator, normal force
  differs from mg.
- **Friction**: f_s(max) = μ_sN (static, prevents motion up to this max),
  f_k = μ_kN (kinetic, while sliding). Friction depends on the normal force and
  the surface materials — **not** on contact area.

## Cornell notes

### Cues / Questions
- Does zero net force mean the object is at rest?
- Why don't Newton's 3rd law force pairs cancel out?
- Why isn't normal force always equal to weight?
- Does a heavier object accelerate faster in free fall?
- What force provides the "push" in a car accelerating forward — the engine,
  or something else?
- What's the difference between static and kinetic friction?

### Notes
- **1st Law**: "zero net force" = constant velocity, which includes v = 0
  *and* any nonzero constant velocity. A car cruising at steady speed on a
  level road has **zero net force** even though it's moving.
- **2nd Law problems always start with a free-body diagram.** List every force
  (gravity, normal, applied, friction, tension), resolve any at an angle into
  components, then sum per axis: ΣF_x = ma_x, ΣF_y = ma_y.
- **3rd Law pairs act on different objects**, e.g., "Earth pulls the ball down"
  pairs with "the ball pulls Earth up" — not with the normal force on the ball.
  Never draw both members of a 3rd-law pair on the *same* free-body diagram.
- **Mass ≠ weight.** In free fall, all objects accelerate at the same g
  regardless of mass — a bowling ball and a tennis ball dropped together hit
  the ground at the same time (ignoring air resistance) because although the
  bowling ball has more weight (more force), it also has more mass (more
  inertia to overcome), and the two effects cancel exactly: a = F/m = mg/m = g.
- **Normal force adjusts** to whatever is needed to prevent the object from
  accelerating through the surface — it is a *response* force, not a fixed
  value. On an incline: N = mg cos(θ). In an accelerating elevator: N = m(g ± a).
- **Friction acts parallel to the surface**, opposing relative motion (kinetic)
  or impending motion (static), and depends only on μ and N — a wider tire
  doesn't inherently mean more friction force if N is unchanged.
- A car accelerates forward because of the **friction force the road exerts on
  the tires** (reaction to the tire pushing backward on the road) — not
  directly from the engine, which only makes the tires push against the ground.

### Summary (write in your own words)
Every Newton's-Laws problem is really: (1) draw all forces, (2) resolve into
components, (3) apply ΣF = ma per axis. Watch for normal force ≠ weight, and
never let a 3rd-law pair "cancel" within one object's free-body diagram.

## Commonly missed question types (what the quiz targets)

1. Believing Newton's 3rd-law pairs cancel each other out on the same object.
2. Assuming normal force always equals weight.
3. Assuming heavier objects fall faster in free fall.
4. Confusing net force direction with velocity direction (an object can move
   right while a net force acts left, e.g., a ball tossed and caught).
5. Thinking friction depends on contact area.
6. Forgetting to include all forces (e.g., missing an applied force's vertical
   component) in a free-body diagram before applying F = ma.

## Practice problems

1. A 5.0 kg box sits on a horizontal floor. A 20 N force is applied at 30°
   above horizontal, pulling the box forward. μ_k = 0.25. Find the box's
   acceleration. *(Answer: N ≈ 39.0 N, f_k ≈ 9.75 N, a ≈ 1.55 m/s²)*
2. A 1000 kg elevator accelerates upward at 2.0 m/s². Find the tension in the
   supporting cable. *(Answer: T = m(g + a) = 11,800 N)*
3. Explain, using Newton's 3rd Law, how a rocket accelerates in the vacuum of
   space with nothing to "push off" of.
4. A 2.0 kg block on a frictionless 30° incline. Find its acceleration down
   the slope. *(Answer: a = g sin30° = 4.9 m/s²)*
5. Two blocks (3 kg and 5 kg) are connected by a string over a frictionless
   pulley (Atwood machine). Find the system's acceleration and string tension.
   *(Answer: a = 2.45 m/s², T ≈ 36.75 N)*

## Real-world applications

- **Seatbelts and crumple zones**: 1st Law (inertia) explains why unrestrained
  passengers keep moving forward in a sudden stop.
- **Rocket propulsion**: 3rd Law — expelling exhaust mass backward pushes the
  rocket forward, even in a vacuum.
- **Vehicle traction control / ABS**: managing static vs. kinetic friction
  between tires and road.
- **Elevators and roller coasters**: apparent weight changes (normal force)
  during acceleration.
