# Unit 3 — Newton's Laws

**Standards:**
- HS-PS2-1 Analyze data to support the claim that Newton's second law of motion describes the mathematical relationship among the net force on a macroscopic object, its mass, and its acceleration.
- HS-PS2-4 Use mathematical representations of Newton's Law of Gravitation and Coulomb's Law to describe and predict forces (gravity portion here; Coulomb's law in Semester 2).

## Learning objectives
Students can:
1. State and apply Newton's three laws to everyday situations.
2. Draw accurate free-body diagrams (FBDs) and find net force along each axis.
3. Use ΣF = ma to find acceleration, force, or mass, including on inclines and with friction.
4. Distinguish mass from weight; calculate weight and normal force (including apparent weight).
5. Identify action–reaction pairs and explain why they do not cancel.
6. Use F = Gm₁m₂/r² to describe how gravity changes with mass and distance.

## Key concepts and formulas

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| 1st law (inertia) | ΣF = 0 ⇒ constant velocity (including at rest) |
| 2nd law | ΣF = ma (vector; use components) |
| 3rd law | F_A on B = −F_B on A (different objects, same type of force) |
| Weight | W = mg (g = 9.8 N/kg) |
| Friction | f_s ≤ μ_s N; f_k = μ_k N |
| Incline (angle θ) | along slope mg sinθ; perpendicular N = mg cosθ |
| Universal gravitation | F = Gm₁m₂/r², G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² |

## Cornell notes

| Cues | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is inertia? | Resistance to change in motion; measured by mass. |
| What does the 1st law say? | With zero net force an object keeps its velocity. Moving at constant speed needs no net force. Friction is why things stop. |
| What is net force? | Vector sum of all forces on one object. |
| What does the 2nd law say? | a = ΣF/m. Direction of a = direction of net force. More mass, less acceleration. |
| How to draw an FBD? | 1) Isolate one object as a dot. 2) Draw only forces on it (gravity, normal, tension, friction, applied). 3) No acceleration arrow, no forces the object exerts. |
| Mass vs. weight? | Mass: amount of matter (kg), constant. Weight: gravitational force (N), changes with g. |
| What is the normal force? | Contact force perpendicular to the surface. Not always equal to mg (ramps, elevators, extra pushes). |
| What does the 3rd law say? | Forces come in pairs on *different* objects, equal magnitude, opposite direction. Pairs never cancel because they act on different objects. |
| How does friction work? | Opposes sliding. Kinetic friction = μN. Static friction adjusts up to a maximum. |
| How does gravity scale? | Double the distance, ¼ the force. Double one mass, double the force. |

**Summary:** _Net force on an object causes acceleration inversely proportional to its mass. I draw an FBD, sum forces on each axis, and solve ΣF = ma. Third-law pairs act on different objects._

## Common misconceptions
- "Motion needs a force to continue." (Only changes in motion do.)
- "The heavier object exerts a bigger force in a collision." (Equal and opposite.)
- "Weight and normal force are a third-law pair." (Both act on the same object.)
- "Mass and weight are the same thing."
- "Normal force always equals mg."

## Practice problems
1. A 10 kg cart is pushed with a net force of 30 N. Acceleration? [3 m/s²]
2. A 60 kg person: weight on Earth, on the Moon (g = 1.6)? [588 N; 96 N]
3. A 20 kg box is dragged at constant velocity by a 50 N horizontal force. Find f and μ_k. [50 N; 0.26]
4. A 5 kg block sits on a frictionless 30° ramp. Find a. [4.9 m/s²]
5. A 70 kg rider in an elevator accelerating up at 2 m/s². Normal force? Down at 2 m/s²? [826 N; 546 N]
6. Two blocks, 2 kg and 3 kg, touch on a frictionless table; push the 2 kg with 10 N. a and the force between the blocks? [2 m/s²; 6 N]
7. Draw FBDs: a book at rest, a skydiver at terminal velocity, a car braking, a hanging lamp.
8. If the distance between two masses triples, how does gravitational force change? [1/9]
9. Data analysis (HS-PS2-1): given a–F data for a 0.50 kg cart, graph and show the slope equals 1/m. [slope ≈ 2 (m/s²)/N]

## Real-world applications
- Seat belts and headrests (1st law), rocket propulsion (3rd law), elevators and amusement-park rides (apparent weight).
- Car braking and tire design (friction), anti-lock brakes.
- Satellites and GPS orbit (gravitation).

## Lab idea: Newton's second law
Hang a mass from a string over a pulley to pull a cart (Atwood-style). Keep total mass constant while moving masses between cart and hanger to vary F. Plot a vs. F (slope = 1/m_total). Then fix F and vary m, plot a vs. 1/m. Write a claim–evidence–reasoning (CER) paragraph.
