# Unit 3: Newton's Laws

**Standards:** HS-PS2-1, HS-PS2-3. **Essential question:** How do forces cause changes in motion, and how can we predict the acceleration of an object from the forces on it?

## Learning objectives
- [ ] N1 State and apply the first law (inertia, equilibrium).
- [ ] N2 Draw correct free-body diagrams.
- [ ] N3 Apply F_net = ma in 1-D, including friction, tension and inclines.
- [ ] N4 Identify third-law pairs; explain why they do not cancel.
- [ ] N5 Distinguish mass and weight; explain apparent weight in an elevator.
- [ ] N6 Use data to show a is proportional to F_net and inversely proportional to m.

## Key concepts and equations
| Concept | Statement / equation |
|---|---|
| First law | An object keeps its velocity (including rest) unless a net outside force acts. Equilibrium: ΣF = 0. |
| Second law | ΣF = m a (vector). 1 N = 1 kg·m/s^2. |
| Third law | If A pushes on B, B pushes on A with equal size and opposite direction. Pairs act on **different objects**. |
| Weight | F_g = m g (N). Mass is in kg and does not change with location. |
| Friction | Kinetic: f_k = μ_k N. Static: f_s ≤ μ_s N. Static is usually larger than kinetic. |
| Incline (angle θ) | Along slope: mg sin θ. Perpendicular: N = mg cos θ. |
| Apparent weight | N = m(g + a) with up positive and a the acceleration of the elevator. |
| Terminal velocity | Air drag equals weight, so ΣF = 0 and v is constant. |

## Cornell notes
| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What does the first law say, and what is inertia? | Without a net force, a moving object keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed and a resting object stays at rest. **Inertia** is resistance to change in motion, measured by mass. |
| If an object moves at constant velocity, what is the net force? | **Zero**. Constant velocity needs no net force. Motion does not require a force; *changing* motion does. |
| What are the steps for a free-body diagram? | (1) Pick one object. (2) Draw it as a dot or box. (3) Draw only forces *on* that object (weight, normal, tension, friction, applied, drag), each from the object, arrow length ∝ size. (4) Never draw the acceleration or the forces the object exerts on others. |
| What does F_net = ma mean in words? | Acceleration points the way the net force points, grows with force, and shrinks with mass. Double the net force → double a. Double the mass → half a. |
| Mass vs weight? | Mass (kg) is the amount of matter and is the same everywhere. Weight (N) is the gravitational force, mg. 60 kg is 588 N on Earth and about 98 N on the Moon. |
| What are Newton's third-law pairs, and why don't they cancel? | Equal and opposite forces between two objects, such as Earth pulls on you / you pull on Earth. They act on **different objects**, so they cannot cancel on one free-body diagram. |
| A book rests on a table. What is the third-law partner of the book's weight? | The book's gravitational pull *on Earth*. The normal force is not the partner; it only balances weight because the book is in equilibrium (a second-law result). |
| In a truck-bug collision, which feels the larger force? | Equal force on each. The bug has the far larger acceleration because its mass is tiny. |
| How do I find friction? | Kinetic: f = μN, with N from the vertical balance (not always mg). Static friction matches the push until the limit μ_s N. Friction opposes sliding (or attempted sliding). |
| Why does a person feel heavier in an elevator that accelerates upward? | Normal force is the scale reading: N - mg = ma, so N = m(g + a) > mg. Speeding up while going up *or* slowing down while going down feel heavier. In free fall, N = 0 (weightless). |
| Worked: 10 kg box, 50 N push right, 20 N friction. Find a. | Right is positive. ΣF = 50 - 20 = 30 N. a = 30/10 = **3 m/s^2** right. |
| Worked: 4.0 kg hanging mass, at rest, then accelerating up at 2.0 m/s^2. Find tension. | At rest: T = mg = 39 N. Accelerating: T - mg = ma, T = 4.0(9.8 + 2.0) = **47 N**. |
| Worked: 5.0 kg block on a frictionless 30° incline. Find a. | Along the slope: mg sin 30° = 24.5 N. a = 24.5/5.0 = **4.9 m/s^2** down the slope (independent of mass: a = g sin θ). |

**Memory tricks:** "**Third-law pairs: two objects, never one**." "**Free-body: forces ON the object only.**" "**Constant v ⇒ ΣF = 0**." "**Normal means perpendicular**, not 'equal to weight'."

**Summary:** Forces are interactions that come in pairs on different objects. On one object, the vector sum of forces sets its acceleration through ΣF = ma. Draw the free-body diagram first, choose axes along the motion, then apply the second law in each direction.

## Practice problems (answers at the end)
1. A 1200 kg car accelerates at 2.5 m/s^2. What is the net force?
2. A 20 kg crate on a floor with μ_k = 0.30 is pushed by 100 N. Find friction and the acceleration.
3. Atwood machine: masses 3.0 kg and 2.0 kg on a light string over a frictionless pulley. Find a and T.
4. A 70 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s^2. What does the scale read?
5. A 2.0 kg cart is pulled with 6.0 N and then 12 N on a frictionless track. Predict how acceleration changes and plot F vs a.
6. A skydiver of 80 kg reaches terminal velocity. What is the drag force?

**Answers:** (1) 3000 N. (2) f = 0.30 × 20 × 9.8 = 59 N; a = (100 - 59)/20 = 2.1 m/s^2. (3) a = (3.0 - 2.0)(9.8)/5.0 = 2.0 m/s^2; T = 2.0(9.8 + 1.96) ≈ 24 N (check: 3.0(9.8 - 1.96) = 24 N). (4) N = 70(9.8 + 2.0) = 826 N (about 830 N). (5) a = 3.0 m/s^2 and 6.0 m/s^2: doubling force doubles acceleration; slope of a-vs-F is 1/m = 0.50 kg^-1. (6) Drag = weight = 784 N upward.

## Real-world applications
- Seat belts, airbags, headrests (inertia and force over time; links to Unit 4).
- Vehicle safety and braking; friction and tire design; ABS.
- Rocket propulsion and jet engines (third law).
- Elevators and amusement rides (apparent weight).
- Skydiving, parachutes and drag; terminal velocity.
- Engineering: bridge cables and trusses (equilibrium).

## Lab ideas
- **Second-law cart lab:** vary hanging mass (force) and cart mass; graph a vs F and a vs 1/m.
- **Friction lab:** measure μ_s and μ_k with a force sensor or an incline.
- **Spring scales in an elevator** (or a phone's accelerometer).
- **Third law:** two force sensors pull on each other; show equal and opposite readings.
