# Unit 2: Projectile Motion

**Standards:** supports HS-PS2-1. **Essential question:** How can two independent motions (horizontal and vertical) be combined to predict where a launched object lands?

## Learning objectives
- [ ] P1 Explain that horizontal and vertical motion are independent.
- [ ] P2 Resolve a launch velocity into components.
- [ ] P3 Solve horizontal-launch problems.
- [ ] P4 Solve angled-launch problems (peak, time of flight, range).
- [ ] P5 Predict how angle and speed change range; identify complementary angles.
- [ ] P6 Test a prediction and compare with measurement, with sources of error.

## Key concepts and equations
Ignore air resistance. Choose up as positive.

| Idea | Equation |
|---|---|
| Components | v_x = v cos θ ; v_y = v sin θ |
| Horizontal (a_x = 0) | x = v_x t (v_x constant) |
| Vertical (a_y = -g) | v_y = v_0y - g t ; y = v_0y t - ½ g t^2 ; v_y^2 = v_0y^2 - 2 g Δy |
| Horizontal launch from height h | t = √(2h/g), range = v_x t |
| Level ground: time of flight | T = 2 v_0y / g |
| Level ground: peak height | H = v_0y^2 / (2g) |
| Level ground: range | R = v^2 sin(2θ) / g |
| Speed at any point | v = √(v_x^2 + v_y^2), angle tan φ = v_y / v_x |

The only link between the two motions is **time**.

## Cornell notes
| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| Why can I treat x and y motion separately? | Perpendicular components do not affect each other. Gravity acts only vertically, so it changes v_y and leaves v_x alone. |
| What is the acceleration of a projectile at every point? | 9.8 m/s^2 downward (a_x = 0, a_y = -g), including at the peak. |
| A ball rolls off a table while another is dropped. Which lands first? | They land **together**. Both start with v_y = 0 and fall the same height. Horizontal speed does not change fall time. |
| What happens to v_x and v_y during flight? | v_x is constant. v_y decreases by 9.8 m/s every second (up positive), is 0 at the peak, and the object lands with the same v_y magnitude as launch (level ground). |
| How do I split a launch velocity into components? | v_x = v cos θ, v_y = v sin θ, with θ measured from the horizontal. Draw the triangle first. |
| What is the force on a projectile at its peak? | Only gravity (mg downward). There is no "force of the throw" after release. |
| Which launch angle gives maximum range on level ground? Which angles give equal range? | **45°** gives the maximum. Complementary angles (θ and 90° - θ, such as 30° and 60°) give the same range. Higher angle = more time in the air, lower angle = more horizontal speed. |
| What is the recipe for any projectile problem? | (1) Sketch and pick axes. (2) Split into x and y tables of given values. (3) Solve vertical for time. (4) Use time in x. (5) Recombine if speed is needed. |
| Worked: a ball is launched at 20 m/s at 30° on level ground. Find T, H and R. | v_x = 20 cos30° = 17.3 m/s; v_y = 20 sin30° = 10 m/s. T = 2(10)/9.8 = **2.04 s**. H = 100/19.6 = **5.1 m**. R = 17.3 × 2.04 = **35 m** (check: 400 × sin60° / 9.8 = 35). |
| Worked: a plane at 80 m/s level flight, 500 m altitude, drops a package. Where does it land? | Vertical: t = √(2×500/9.8) = 10.1 s. Horizontal: x = 80 × 10.1 = **about 810 m** ahead of the drop point. The package stays below the plane (same v_x) until it lands. |

**Memory tricks:** "**Time is the bridge**" between x and y. "**x is cruise control, y is gravity.**" "**Cos for x, Sin for y**" (when θ is from the horizontal).

**Summary:** A projectile is a constant-velocity motion horizontally combined with free fall vertically. Solve the vertical part to get time, then use that time for the horizontal part.

## Practice problems (answers at the end)
1. A ball is kicked at 20 m/s at 30° above horizontal on level ground. Find the range.
2. A package is dropped from a plane flying level at 80 m/s at 500 m. Find the horizontal distance traveled.
3. A ball is thrown horizontally at 15 m/s from a 20 m cliff. Find the time, horizontal distance, and the speed at impact.
4. A projectile is launched at 25 m/s at 53° (sin 53° ≈ 0.80, cos 53° ≈ 0.60). Find the maximum height.
5. At 25° a ball lands 40 m away. What other angle gives the same range at the same speed?
6. Why does the range of a golf ball in real life come out shorter than the ideal equation?

**Answers:** (1) ≈ 35 m. (2) ≈ 810 m. (3) t = 2.02 s; x = 30 m; v_y = 19.8 m/s, v = √(15² + 19.8²) ≈ 25 m/s. (4) v_y = 20 m/s, H = 400/19.6 ≈ 20 m. (5) 65°. (6) Air resistance reduces speed both horizontally and vertically; spin and wind also matter.

## Real-world applications
- Sports: basketball free throws, soccer kicks, long jump, javelin, golf, and why the best long-jump angle is under 45° (takeoff and landing heights differ, and the athlete cannot convert all speed to angle).
- Firefighting and irrigation: aiming a stream of water.
- Military and rocketry: ballistic trajectories (with drag corrections).
- Film stunts and video-game physics engines.
- Search and rescue: air-drop of supplies.

## Lab ideas
- **Launcher range test:** predict range for a given angle and speed, then test (compare percent error).
- **Horizontal launch:** measure the speed of a rolling ball with photogates, predict landing spot on a target.
- **Angle vs range graph** with a spring or pneumatic launcher; find the maximum angle experimentally.
