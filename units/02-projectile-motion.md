# Unit 2 — Projectile Motion

**Standards:** HS-PS2-1 (Newton's second law applied to motion under gravity). Practices: developing and using models, using mathematics.

## Learning objectives
Students can:
1. Explain that horizontal and vertical motion are independent for a projectile.
2. Resolve an initial velocity into x and y components.
3. Solve horizontal-launch and angled-launch problems for time, range, and maximum height.
4. Describe velocity and acceleration at every point of the path, including the peak.
5. Use a model (equations, simulation, or video) to predict a landing point, then test it.

## Key concepts and formulas

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Components | v₀ₓ = v₀cosθ; v₀ᵧ = v₀sinθ |
| Horizontal (a = 0) | x = v₀ₓ t (constant velocity) |
| Vertical (a = −g) | y = y₀ + v₀ᵧt − ½gt²; vᵧ = v₀ᵧ − gt |
| Time of flight (level ground) | T = 2v₀ᵧ / g |
| Max height | H = v₀ᵧ² / 2g |
| Range (level ground) | R = v₀² sin2θ / g |
| Horizontal launch from height h | t = √(2h/g); x = v₀ₓ t |

## Cornell notes

| Cues | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is a projectile? | An object moving only under gravity after launch (ignore air drag). Path is a parabola. |
| Why split into x and y? | Gravity acts only vertically, so horizontal and vertical motions are independent. Time is the link. |
| What is happening in x? | a = 0, constant velocity. v₀ₓ never changes. |
| What is happening in y? | a = −9.8 m/s² always, same as free fall. |
| What about the top of the path? | vᵧ = 0, but vₓ ≠ 0 and a = 9.8 m/s² down. Speed is at its minimum, not zero. |
| Dropped vs. shot horizontally from the same height? | Hit the ground at the same time. Same vertical motion. |
| Which angle gives max range? | 45° on level ground. Complementary angles (30°/60°) give equal range. |
| How do I solve a problem? | 1) Components. 2) Solve y for time. 3) Use time in x. Keep x and y variables separate. |

**Summary:** _A projectile has constant horizontal velocity and constant downward acceleration. Time connects the two directions, so I find time from the vertical motion and use it for horizontal distance._

## Common misconceptions
- A force keeps pushing the ball forward (there is no forward force after launch).
- The ball has zero acceleration at its peak.
- A faster horizontal launch means a longer fall time (no: fall time depends only on height).
- Mixing x and y quantities in one equation.

## Practice problems
1. A ball rolls off a 1.25 m table at 2.0 m/s. Time in the air and landing distance? [0.50 s; 1.0 m]
2. A rock is thrown horizontally at 10 m/s from a 19.6 m cliff. Where does it land and how fast is it going on impact? [20 m; 22.0 m/s]
3. A soccer ball is kicked at 20 m/s at 30°. Find components, time of flight, max height, range. [17.3, 10 m/s; 2.04 s; 5.1 m; 35.3 m]
4. Show that launches at 25° and 65° with the same speed have equal range. [sin50° = sin130°]
5. A cannon on level ground fires at 40 m/s at 45°. Range? [163 m]
6. Design: a basketball is released 2.0 m above the floor. What launch angle and speed reach a hoop 3.05 m high and 5 m away? Use a simulation to test your prediction. [open-ended; one solution θ = 50°, v ≈ 7.8 m/s with release at 2.0 m]
7. Explain why a baseball thrown at 45° with air resistance lands shorter than the no-drag prediction. [drag reduces both components; peak range angle drops below 45°]

## Real-world applications
- Sports: basketball shot arc, long jump, football punts, javelin.
- Emergency response: firefighting water streams, supply drops from aircraft.
- Safety and engineering: fountain design, golf-course and stadium netting.
- Forensics: estimating vehicle speed from the distance a car leaves the road.

## Lab idea: Marble ramp predict-and-test
Roll a marble down a ramp off a table. Measure launch speed with the ramp end photogates (or from a prior trial), predict landing spot on the floor, and place a cup there. Score: hit/miss, plus a written analysis of uncertainty.

## Unit assessment criteria
Mastery: separates components correctly, uses time as the linking variable, labels velocity/acceleration at three points on a path diagram, and the lab prediction lands within 10% of measured range. See [`../assessment/rubrics-and-criteria.md`](../assessment/rubrics-and-criteria.md).
