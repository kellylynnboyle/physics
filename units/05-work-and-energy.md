# Unit 5: Work and Energy

**Standards:** HS-PS3-1, HS-PS3-2, HS-PS3-3. **Essential question:** How does energy move and change form, and how can conservation of energy predict motion without tracking forces?

## Learning objectives
- [ ] W1 Calculate work for forces parallel and at an angle to displacement.
- [ ] W2 Calculate kinetic, gravitational potential and elastic potential energy.
- [ ] W3 Apply the work-energy theorem.
- [ ] W4 Apply conservation of mechanical energy; account for friction as thermal energy.
- [ ] W5 Calculate power and efficiency.
- [ ] W6 Build an energy bar chart or model for a system.
- [ ] W7 Design a device that converts energy and evaluate its efficiency.

## Key concepts and equations
| Concept | Equation | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Work | W = F d cos θ (θ between force and displacement) | J = N·m |
| Kinetic energy | KE = ½ m v² | J |
| Gravitational PE | PE_g = m g h | J |
| Elastic PE | PE_s = ½ k x² | J |
| Work-energy theorem | W_net = ΔKE | J |
| Conservation | KE_i + PE_i + W_other = KE_f + PE_f | J |
| Power | P = W / t = F v | W = J/s |
| Efficiency | useful output / total input × 100% | % |

## Cornell notes
| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is work in physics? | Energy transferred by a force acting through a displacement: W = Fd cos θ. Only the force component along the motion counts. Units: joule (J). |
| When is the work done by a force zero? | If there is no displacement, or if the force is perpendicular to the motion (carrying a box at constant height; the normal force on a sliding block; the centripetal force in uniform circular motion). |
| Can work be negative? | Yes. When the force opposes the displacement (θ = 180°), such as friction on a sliding object, work is negative and the object loses energy. |
| What is kinetic energy, and what happens if speed doubles? | KE = ½mv². Doubling speed **quadruples** KE, which is why stopping distance grows with the square of speed. |
| What is gravitational potential energy, and what sets the zero? | PE_g = mgh, relative to a chosen reference height. Only *changes* in PE matter, so you can choose h = 0 anywhere. |
| What does the work-energy theorem say? | Net work on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: W_net = ½mv_f² - ½mv_i². |
| When can I use conservation of mechanical energy? | When only conservative forces (gravity, springs) do work. KE + PE stays constant. With friction or air drag, mechanical energy decreases and the "lost" energy becomes thermal energy; total energy is still conserved. |
| Does the shape of a frictionless slope matter for speed at the bottom? | No. v = √(2gh) depends only on height drop, not on mass or path. |
| What is the difference between power and work? | Power is the *rate* of doing work (P = W/t). Lifting the same weight the same height takes the same work, but a faster lift needs more power. 1 W = 1 J/s; 746 W ≈ 1 horsepower. |
| How do I find the efficiency of a machine? | Efficiency = useful energy out / energy in. It is always less than 100% because some energy is lost as heat, sound and friction. |
| Worked: 2.0 kg object dropped from 5.0 m. Find v at the bottom. | PE_i = mgh = 2.0(9.8)(5.0) = 98 J → all KE at the bottom. ½(2.0)v² = 98 → v² = 98, v = **9.9 m/s**. Check: v = √(2gh) = √98. |
| Worked: 1200 kg car brakes from 20 m/s to rest in 40 m. Find the average stopping force. | W_net = ΔKE = 0 - ½(1200)(20²) = -240,000 J. F = W/d = -240,000/40 = **-6000 N** (opposite the motion). |
| Worked: spring k = 500 N/m compressed 0.20 m launches a 0.10 kg ball. Find its speed. | PE_s = ½(500)(0.20)² = 10 J. KE = 10 J = ½(0.10)v² → v = √200 = **14 m/s**. |

**Memory tricks:** "**W = F d cos θ**: only the parallel part works." "**Energy never disappears; it changes form.**" "**Speed squared in KE**." "**Bar chart: initial = final bars**" for conservation.

**Summary:** Work transfers energy. Kinetic and potential energy describe stored and moving energy; the work-energy theorem links net work to changes in motion, and conservation of energy lets us skip the details of the path. Friction converts mechanical energy to thermal energy, so total energy is conserved even when mechanical energy is not.

## Practice problems (answers at the end)
1. A 50 N force pushes a crate 4.0 m along the floor. How much work? How much if the 50 N force is at 60° to the floor (pulling at an angle)?
2. A frictionless roller coaster starts from rest at a height of 40 m. Find the speed at the bottom.
3. A spring (k = 500 N/m) is compressed 0.20 m and launches a 0.10 kg ball. Find its speed.
4. A motor lifts a 200 kg elevator 10 m in 20 s. Find the work and the power.
5. A 1200 kg car moving at 20 m/s brakes to a stop in 40 m. Find the average stopping force.
6. A machine takes in 400 J and produces 100 J of useful work. Find the efficiency and say where the rest goes.

**Answers:** (1) 200 J; 50 × 4.0 × cos 60° = 100 J. (2) v = √(2 × 9.8 × 40) = √784 = 28 m/s. (3) 14 m/s. (4) W = 200 × 9.8 × 10 = 19,600 J; P = 980 W. (5) 6000 N. (6) 25%; the remaining 300 J becomes thermal energy and sound.

## Real-world applications
- Roller coasters and ski jumps: converting PE to KE.
- Hybrid and electric cars: regenerative braking turns KE into stored electrical energy.
- Hydroelectric dams, wind turbines, solar and batteries: energy conversion chains and efficiency.
- Sports: pole vault (KE → elastic PE → gravitational PE), trampoline.
- Stopping distance and speed limits (KE ∝ v²).
- Appliance power ratings and energy bills (kWh).

## Lab ideas
- **Ramp and cart:** compare PE lost with KE gained and estimate friction loss.
- **Spring launcher:** measure k from a force-extension graph, predict launch speed, test.
- **Power climb:** time yourself climbing stairs and compute your power.
- **Device design (HS-PS3-3):** build a simple energy converter (rubber-band car, hand-crank generator, solar-powered fan) and measure its efficiency.
