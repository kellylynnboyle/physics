# Unit 3: Newton's Laws of Motion

**NGSS:** HS-PS2-1, HS-PS2-4 (gravitation) | **Quiz:** `../../quizzes/index.html` → Newton's Laws | **Pacing:** ~4 weeks

## Learning objectives (I can...)
1. State Newton's three laws and apply each to everyday situations.
2. Draw free-body diagrams (FBDs) with correctly labeled forces.
3. Use F_net = ma to find acceleration, force, or mass (1-D, then ramps).
4. Distinguish mass from weight; calculate weight W = mg.
5. Identify action-reaction (third law) pairs and explain why they never cancel.
6. Analyze lab data to show a ∝ F_net and a ∝ 1/m.
7. Use Newton's law of gravitation qualitatively and quantitatively (HS-PS2-4).

## Key concepts
- **First law (inertia):** with zero net force, an object keeps its velocity (rest *or* constant velocity).
- **Second law:** a = F_net / m, so F_net = ma. Unit: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². Acceleration points the same way as F_net.
- **Third law:** if A pushes B, then B pushes A equally and oppositely. The two forces act on *different* objects.
- **Forces to know:** weight (F_g = mg, down), normal (⟂ surface), friction (parallel to surface, opposes sliding), tension, applied push/pull, air resistance.
- **Friction:** f_k = μ_k N for sliding; static friction adjusts up to f_s,max = μ_s N.
- **Incline:** along the slope mg sinθ, perpendicular mg cosθ; frictionless a = g sinθ.
- **Gravitation:** F = G m₁m₂ / r², G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². Double the distance → ¼ the force.

## Cornell notes

| Cue | Notes |
|---|---|
| What does Newton's first law say? | Without a net force, velocity doesn't change. No force is needed to keep moving at constant velocity. Inertia = resistance to change; more mass, more inertia. |
| What does "net force" mean? | The vector sum of all forces on one object. Balanced forces: F_net = 0, so a = 0 (could be at rest or constant velocity). |
| State the second law and its units. | F_net = ma. N = kg·m/s². Double the force → double a; double the mass → half the a. |
| How do I draw an FBD? | Dot for the object. Arrows from the dot for each force *on that object only*, labeled (F_g, N, f, T, F_app). Longer arrow = bigger force. Don't draw the object's own velocity as a force. |
| What is the difference between mass and weight? | Mass = amount of matter (kg), same everywhere. Weight = gravitational force (N), W = mg. 60 kg: W ≈ 588 N on Earth; mass is still 60 kg on the Moon. |
| What is the normal force? | Contact force perpendicular to a surface. It equals weight only when nothing else pushes vertically and there's no vertical acceleration. |
| What does the third law say? Why don't the pair forces cancel? | Equal size, opposite direction, same type, on different objects. They can't cancel because they act on different objects. A truck and a bug feel the same force; the bug's acceleration is huge because its mass is tiny. |
| Is the normal force the third-law partner of weight? | No. Earth pulls book down (weight); the partner is the book pulling Earth up. The table pushes book up (normal); the partner is the book pushing the table down. |
| How do I solve a force problem? | 1) Pick the object. 2) FBD. 3) Choose + direction. 4) Write F_net = ma along each axis. 5) Solve, with units. 6) Sense check. |
| Worked example: 5 kg object, net force 20 N | a = 20/5 = 4 m/s² in the force direction. |
| Worked example: 10 kg box, push 40 N, friction 15 N | F_net = 40 − 15 = 25 N; a = 25/10 = 2.5 m/s². Normal = weight = 98 N (vertical balanced). |
| Worked example: box pushed at constant velocity with 50 N | a = 0 → F_net = 0 → friction = 50 N backward. |
| Elevator accelerating upward: apparent weight? | N − mg = ma → N = m(g + a) > mg. You feel heavier. In free fall N = 0 (weightless feeling). |
| How does gravity depend on distance? | Inverse-square: 2× the distance → ¼ the force; 3× → 1/9. Proportional to each mass. |

## Most-missed questions
1. "A force is needed to keep an object moving." (False: only to change velocity.)
2. Treating the normal force as the third-law partner of weight.
3. "The bigger object exerts a bigger force in a collision." (Equal forces.)
4. Confusing mass and weight, or using kg as a force.
5. Putting forces on the FBD that aren't acting on the object (e.g., "force of motion").
6. Forgetting that constant velocity means F_net = 0, so friction equals the push.
7. Elevator apparent-weight sign errors.

## Practice problems
1. 1200 kg car, net force 3000 N. a? [2.5 m/s²]
2. A 2.0 kg cart accelerates at 3.0 m/s². Net force? [6.0 N]
3. Weight of a 75 kg person on Earth? On a planet where g = 3.7 m/s²? [735 N; 278 N]
4. A 5.0 kg block on a frictionless 30° incline. a? [4.9 m/s²]
5. A 20 kg crate is pulled with 100 N, friction 60 N. a? [2.0 m/s²]
6. μ_k = 0.30 for a 10 kg sled sliding on level ground. Friction force? [29.4 N]
7. A 70 kg person in an elevator accelerating up at 2.0 m/s². Normal force? [826 N]
8. Two masses, 3 kg and 2 kg, hang over a pulley (Atwood). a? [1.96 m/s²]
9. Draw FBDs: book at rest; skydiver at terminal velocity; box sliding to a stop; car braking.
10. If the Earth–Moon distance doubled, how does gravity change? [¼]

## Labs

- **Friction lab:** drag a block with a spring scale; compare static and kinetic friction; find μ from f vs N graph.

- Seat belts and headrests (inertia).

- Tire design and anti-lock braking (friction).
