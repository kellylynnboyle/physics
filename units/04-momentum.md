# Unit 4: Momentum

**Standards:** HS-PS2-2, HS-PS2-3. **Essential question:** Why is the total momentum of a system conserved, and how can we use that to predict the outcome of collisions and to protect people in them?

## Learning objectives
- [ ] M1 Calculate momentum and impulse; relate impulse to change in momentum.
- [ ] M2 Explain how extending collision time reduces force.
- [ ] M3 Apply conservation of momentum to collisions and explosions in 1-D.
- [ ] M4 Classify collisions as elastic, inelastic or perfectly inelastic.
- [ ] M5 Design and test a device that protects an object in a collision.

## Key concepts and equations
| Concept | Equation | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Momentum | p = m v (vector) | kg·m/s |
| Impulse | J = F_avg Δt = Δp = m(v_f - v_i) | N·s = kg·m/s |
| Conservation | Σp_before = Σp_after when net external force is zero | |
| Perfectly inelastic | m1 v1 + m2 v2 = (m1 + m2) v_f | objects stick |
| Elastic | momentum **and** kinetic energy conserved | |
| Explosion / recoil | total p = 0 before, so m1 v1 = -m2 v2 | |
| Area under F-t graph | impulse | |

## Cornell notes
| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is momentum, and is it a vector? | p = mv, "mass in motion". It points in the direction of velocity, so signs matter: +right, -left. |
| What is impulse and how is it related to momentum? | Impulse J = FΔt is the push over a time. By Newton's second law, **J = Δp**. A bigger impulse changes momentum more. |
| Why do airbags and crumple zones reduce injury? | The change in momentum is fixed (the car and person must stop). Increasing Δt lowers the average force: F = Δp/Δt. Catching a ball by pulling your hands back works the same way. |
| Which gives a bigger impulse: a ball that stops, or the same ball that bounces back? | The bounce, because Δp = m(v_f - v_i) is bigger when v_f is reversed. For a 0.50 kg ball hitting at 10 m/s, stopping gives 5.0 N·s; rebounding at 10 m/s gives 10 N·s. |
| When is momentum of a system conserved? | When the net **external** force on the system is zero (collisions are short, so friction and gravity are usually negligible). Internal forces cancel in pairs (third law). |
| How do I solve a collision problem? | (1) Define the system and a positive direction. (2) Write p_before = p_after with signs. (3) Solve for the unknown velocity. (4) Compare kinetic energy before and after to classify. |
| Is kinetic energy conserved in collisions? | Only in **elastic** collisions. In inelastic collisions some KE becomes thermal energy, sound and deformation. Momentum is conserved in all of them (isolated system). |
| What happens when a gun fires or a skater pushes off? | Total momentum starts at 0 and stays 0. The lighter object gets the larger speed in the opposite direction. The momenta are equal in size, not the speeds. |
| How do I get impulse from a graph? | Impulse is the area under the force-time graph. A triangle of height 200 N and width 0.1 s has area ½(200)(0.1) = 10 N·s. |
| Worked: 1500 kg car at 10 m/s hits a stationary 1000 kg car and they lock together. Find v and the KE lost. | p = 1500(10) = 15,000 = (2500) v → v = **6.0 m/s**. KE before = ½(1500)(10)² = 75,000 J; after = ½(2500)(6)² = 45,000 J. Lost **30,000 J**. |
| Worked: a 0.145 kg baseball arrives at 40 m/s and leaves at 45 m/s the other way. Contact time 0.002 s. Find F. | Take the arrival direction as +: Δp = 0.145(-45 - 40) = -12.3 kg·m/s. F = Δp/Δt = -12.3/0.002 ≈ **-6.2 × 10³ N** (6200 N back toward the pitcher). |

**Memory tricks:** "**Impulse = Force × Time = Change in momentum**" (FΔt = Δp). "**Before = After**" for total p. "**Stretch the time, shrink the force.**" "**Signs! Left is negative.**"

**Summary:** Momentum measures how hard it is to stop a moving object. A force acting for a time changes it (impulse). In a system with no net external force the total momentum stays constant, which lets us predict collisions and recoil without knowing the forces involved.

## Practice problems (answers at the end)
1. A 0.145 kg baseball moves at 40 m/s. Find its momentum.
2. A 60 kg skater at rest throws a 3.0 kg tool at 8.0 m/s. Find the skater's recoil velocity.
3. A 2.0 kg cart at 3.0 m/s collides with a stationary 1.0 kg cart and they stick together. Find the final velocity.
4. A 1000 kg car moving at 20 m/s stops in 0.50 s in a crash, and in 4.0 s while braking. Compare average forces.
5. A 2 kg cart moving at +3 m/s collides head-on with a 2 kg cart at -3 m/s and they stick. Find v.
6. Two identical carts: A moves at +4 m/s and hits a stationary B in an elastic collision. Predict the final velocities.

**Answers:** (1) 5.8 kg·m/s. (2) v = -(3.0 × 8.0)/60 = **0.40 m/s opposite** to the tool. (3) v = 6.0/3.0 = 2.0 m/s. (4) Δp = -20,000 kg·m/s: crash F = 40,000 N; braking F = 5,000 N, eight times smaller. (5) Total p = 0 so they stop (v = 0). (6) They exchange velocities: A stops, B moves at +4 m/s.

## Real-world applications
- Car safety: airbags, crumple zones, seat belts, padded dashboards, helmets.
- Sports: follow-through in baseball and golf, boxing gloves, catching and landing technique.
- Rockets and jets: thrust from expelled mass.
- Forensics: reconstructing crashes from skid marks and final positions.
- Billiards and Newton's cradle.
- Space: astronaut tool recoil, satellite docking.

## Lab ideas
- **Cart collisions on a track:** measure velocities with photogates in elastic (magnet or spring) and sticky collisions; compute p and KE before and after.
- **Egg-drop / crumple-zone design (HS-PS2-3):** design a package that keeps the egg intact from a set height, record data, estimate impulse with a phone accelerometer, and refine.
- **Force sensor impulse:** drop a ball on a force plate; integrate F-t for J.
