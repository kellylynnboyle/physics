# Unit 4 — Momentum

**Standards:** NGSS HS-PS2-2 — "Use mathematical representations to support the claim that the total momentum of a system of objects is conserved when there is no net force on the system." HS-PS2-3 — "Apply scientific and engineering ideas to design, evaluate, and refine a device that minimizes the force on a macroscopic object during a collision." [CDE NGSS reference](https://www.cde.ca.gov/pd/ca/sc/ngssstandards.asp)

## Key concepts

- Momentum: p = mv (vector)
- Impulse: J = FΔt = Δp (impulse-momentum theorem)
- Conservation of momentum in isolated systems
- Elastic, inelastic, and perfectly inelastic collisions
- Real-world impulse applications (safety engineering)
- Conservation along independent x/y axes in 2D collisions

## Cornell notes

| Cue | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Momentum** | p = mv (kg·m/s). A vector — its direction is the same as the velocity's. |
| **Impulse-Momentum Theorem** | J = FΔt = Δp = mΔv. A small force over a long time can produce the same Δp as a large force over a short time — the basis of airbags and crumple zones. |
| **Conservation of Momentum** | In an isolated system (no external net force), total momentum before = total momentum after: Σp_before = Σp_after — true even when kinetic energy is lost in the collision. |
| **Elastic vs. Inelastic** | Elastic = kinetic energy conserved (objects bounce apart). Inelastic = KE not conserved (some converts to heat/sound/deformation). Perfectly inelastic = objects stick together (maximum KE loss). Momentum is conserved in **all** of these cases. |
| **2D Collisions** | Momentum is conserved separately along the x-direction and the y-direction. |

**Summary:** Momentum conservation applies whenever the external net force
on a system is zero — regardless of whether kinetic energy is conserved.
That distinction (momentum always conserved; KE only in elastic collisions)
is the single most commonly confused point in this unit.

## Practice problems

1. A 1200 kg car moving at 15 m/s collides with a stationary 1500 kg car, and they stick together. Find their combined velocity after the collision.
2. A 0.15 kg baseball moving at 40 m/s is hit by a bat and leaves at 50 m/s in the opposite direction. Find the impulse delivered by the bat.
3. Two skaters (50 kg and 70 kg), initially at rest, push off each other. If the 50 kg skater moves at 3.5 m/s, find the speed of the 70 kg skater.
4. A 0.5 kg ball hits a wall at 10 m/s and bounces straight back at 8 m/s over a 0.05 s contact time. Find the average force exerted on the ball by the wall.

## Real-world applications

- Vehicle safety engineering: crumple zones, airbags, seatbelts.
- Rocket and jet propulsion (momentum conservation with expelled mass).
- Sports: bat/ball and club/ball collisions, boxing glove padding, padded helmets.
