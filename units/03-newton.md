# Unit 3: Newton's Laws of Motion

## NGSS Learning Goals
- HS-PS2-1: Analyze data to support the claim that Newton's second law of motion describes the mathematical relationship among the net force on a macroscopic object, its mass, and its acceleration.
- Science & Engineering Practices: Analyzing and Interpreting Data; Constructing Explanations
- Crosscutting Concepts: Cause and Effect; Systems and System Models

## Learning Objectives
Students will be able to:

- [ ] State Newton's three laws and give examples of each.
- [ ] Draw free-body diagrams and determine net force along each axis.
- [ ] Use ΣF = ma to solve problems with multiple forces, including friction and inclines.
- [ ] Distinguish mass from weight and explain normal force and apparent weight.
- [ ] Analyze experimental data (force vs. acceleration, mass vs. acceleration) to support the claim that a = F_net/m.
- [ ] Identify third-law force pairs and explain why they never cancel.

## Key Concepts
- Inertia and Newton's first law
- Net force and ΣF = ma (units: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s²)
- Newton's third law: force pairs act on different objects
- Weight W = mg vs. mass
- Normal force, tension, friction (f = μN)
- Free-body diagrams
- Equilibrium and terminal velocity
- Inclined planes (components of weight)

## Cornell Notes — Newton's Laws

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| **1st Law (Inertia)?** | An object keeps its velocity (including rest) unless a NET external force acts. Zero net force ≠ zero motion — constant velocity is allowed. |
| **2nd Law?** | a = F_net / m, i.e., ΣF = ma. Acceleration is in the direction of net force; larger mass means smaller acceleration. Apply separately in x and y. |
| **3rd Law?** | If A pushes on B, B pushes on A with equal magnitude, opposite direction. The pair acts on DIFFERENT objects, so they do not cancel. |
| **Mass vs. weight?** | Mass (kg) = amount of matter/inertia, same everywhere. Weight (N) = gravitational force = mg; Moon g ≈ 1.6 m/s². |
| **How to draw a free-body diagram?** | Dot for object; draw only forces ON it (weight, normal, tension, friction, applied). Label each. Choose axes (tilt along an incline). Sum forces per axis. |
| **Normal force & friction?** | Normal force is perpendicular to the surface and equals whatever is needed — not always mg (elevators, inclines, extra pushes). Kinetic friction f = μ_k N opposes motion; static friction adjusts up to μ_s N. |
| **Terminal velocity?** | Air drag grows with speed until it equals weight; net force = 0, so acceleration = 0 and velocity is constant. |

**Summary (write in your own words):** Forces are interactions. Net force (vector sum) causes acceleration in proportion: ΣF = ma. Always isolate one object, draw the free-body diagram, and sum forces along each axis.

## Practice Problems

1. A 2.0 kg cart is pulled with a net force of 10 N. Find its acceleration.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>a = 10/2.0 = 5.0 m/s².</details>
2. A 3.0 kg crate is pushed right with 20 N while friction pulls left with 8 N. Find its acceleration.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>F_net = 12 N; a = 4.0 m/s² to the right.</details>
3. A 60 kg student stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s². What does the scale read?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>N = m(g + a) = 60(11.8) ≈ 708 N.</details>
4. A 5.0 kg object on the Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²): give its mass and weight there.
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>Mass 5.0 kg; weight 8.0 N.</details>
5. A 10 kg box slides at constant speed when pushed with 30 N. What is the friction force and μ_k?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>f = 30 N (net force zero); μ_k = 30/(10×9.8) ≈ 0.31.</details>

## Most-Missed Questions (interactive version in `site/index.html`)

1. An object moves at constant velocity. The net force on it is:  
   A) In the direction of motion; B) Zero; C) Equal to its weight; D) Greater than friction  
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>**B** — Constant velocity means a = 0, so the net force is zero (first law). A force is NOT needed to maintain motion.</details>
2. A large truck collides head-on with a small car. During the impact, the force of the car on the truck is:  
   A) Smaller than the force of the truck on the car; B) Larger than the force of the truck on the car; C) Equal in magnitude to the force of the truck on the car; D) Zero  
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>**C** — Third-law pairs are always equal and opposite. The car has the greater acceleration because of its smaller mass.</details>
3. A 10 kg object is taken from Earth to the Moon. Its mass on the Moon is:  
   A) 10 kg; B) About 1.6 kg; C) About 62 kg; D) 0 kg  
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>**A** — Mass does not change with location. Only the weight changes (≈16 N on the Moon).</details>
4. A 3 kg block has a 20 N force to the right and an 8 N force to the left. Its acceleration is:  
   A) 9.3 m/s²; B) 4 m/s²; C) 6.7 m/s²; D) 2.7 m/s²  
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>**B** — F_net = 20 − 8 = 12 N, so a = 12/3 = 4 m/s². Forces must be combined as a net force before dividing by mass.</details>
5. A 60 kg person is in an elevator accelerating upward at 2 m/s². The scale reading (normal force) is closest to:  
   A) 588 N; B) 468 N; C) 708 N; D) 120 N  
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>**C** — N − mg = ma, so N = 60(9.8 + 2) ≈ 708 N. The person feels heavier.</details>
6. A box is pushed across a floor at constant speed with a 30 N horizontal force. The friction force is:  
   A) Less than 30 N; B) Exactly 30 N; C) More than 30 N; D) Equal to the box's weight  
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>**B** — Constant speed means zero net force, so friction equals the push (30 N).</details>
7. A skydiver reaches terminal velocity. At that moment:  
   A) Gravity stops acting; B) Air resistance equals her weight; C) Air resistance is zero; D) She is accelerating at 9.8 m/s²  
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>**B** — Drag balances weight, so net force and acceleration are zero while velocity stays constant.</details>
8. A book rests on a table. Which force is the third-law (reaction) partner of the Earth's gravitational pull on the book?  
   A) The table's normal force on the book; B) The book's pull on the Earth; C) The book's push on the table; D) Friction from the table  
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>**B** — Reaction pairs swap the two objects: Earth on book ↔ book on Earth. The normal force is a separate interaction that merely balances weight here.</details>
