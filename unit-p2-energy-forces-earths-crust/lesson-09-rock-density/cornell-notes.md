## Why this lesson comes right after Lesson 8

Lesson 8 used rock age to find evidence that oceanic crust is *created* at ridges. Lesson 9 asks a different question about the *same two crust types*: oceanic crust (basalt) and continental crust (granite) aren't just different ages — they're made of **different material**, and that material difference has consequences you can already predict using ideas from Lesson 6 (density and buoyancy) and Lesson 4 (Krakatoa's Fp-vs-Fw balance). This lesson sets up Lesson 10 directly: density is what decides which plate goes under which at a convergent boundary.

## Cue / Question | Notes

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| Basalt (oceanic crust) | Dark, fine-grained, **mafic** rock (rich in iron and magnesium). Forms from magma erupting at mid-ocean ridges, which cools quickly on contact with seawater. Density ≈ **3.0 g/cm³**. |
| Granite (continental crust) | Light-colored, coarse-grained, **felsic** rock (rich in silica, aluminum, and lighter elements). Forms from magma that cools slowly, deep underground. Density ≈ **2.7 g/cm³**. |
| Why does this density difference matter? | Both types of crust "float" on the denser, plastic asthenosphere below them (Lesson 5's layer diagram). Just like objects floating in water, **denser material floats lower** and **less-dense material floats higher**. This is exactly why continents sit higher in elevation than ocean basins — it's not that continents are "piled up," it's that granite is less dense than basalt to begin with. |
| Isostasy (the floating-crust idea) | The principle that Earth's less-dense lithosphere floats in gravitational balance on the denser, deformable asthenosphere beneath it — the same buoyancy-vs-gravity balance from Lesson 6's convection M-E-F box, just applied to whole plates instead of small parcels of mantle rock. |
| The prediction this sets up for Lesson 10 | At a boundary where oceanic crust (denser, basalt) meets continental crust (less dense, granite), the **denser oceanic plate will be forced underneath** the less-dense continental plate — never the reverse. Density decides the direction of subduction, the same way density decided which way a warm/cool parcel moves in the convection tank. |

## Connecting the M-E-F triangle to density-driven sinking/floating

| Lens | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Matter** | Mineral composition differs — mafic (iron/magnesium-rich, basalt) vs. felsic (silica-rich, granite) — and that composition difference is *why* the densities differ. |
| **Energy** | A denser plate sitting on top of a less-dense one is a higher-gravitational-potential-energy (less stable) arrangement; subduction is the system moving toward lower potential energy, same as any object sinking toward a more stable position. |
| **Forces** | Buoyant force (upward, from the surrounding denser asthenosphere) vs. gravity (downward, proportional to the plate's own density). For basalt at a subduction zone, gravity wins over buoyancy relative to the lighter granite beside it, so it's forced down. |

## Worked example — comparing densities

A sample of basalt has a mass of 300 g and a volume of 100 cm³. A sample of granite has a mass of 270 g and the same volume, 100 cm³.

1. Basalt density: `ρ = m/V = 300 g ÷ 100 cm³ = 3.0 g/cm³`
2. Granite density: `ρ = m/V = 270 g ÷ 100 cm³ = 2.7 g/cm³`
3. Since 3.0 > 2.7, basalt is denser — consistent with oceanic crust always being the plate that subducts when it meets continental crust.

Try it yourself: which would displace more water — an equal *mass* of basalt or an equal *mass* of granite? (Hint: think about which one needs more volume to reach that mass, at its given density.)
