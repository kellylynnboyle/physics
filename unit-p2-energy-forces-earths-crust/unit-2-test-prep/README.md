## The whole unit in one paragraph (memorize this — it's the backbone of the short answer)

Radioactive decay in unstable nuclei (**L7**) converts a small amount of mass into a large amount of kinetic energy, heating the mantle. That heat drives **convection** (**L6**): hot, less-dense mantle rock rises; cool, denser rock sinks, in slow-moving cells. Rising currents pull plates apart at **divergent boundaries**, creating new, dense basaltic crust (**L8, L9**) that ages and cools as it spreads away from the ridge — evidence you can date radiometrically. Where plates **converge**, density differences (**L9**) decide which plate subducts, or whether neither can and the crust crumples into mountains instead (**L10**). At boundaries locked by friction, elastic stress accumulates for years to centuries (**L4**) — apparent stability that is really energy loading — until it exceeds the elastic limit and releases suddenly as an earthquake or eruption, with **seismic waves** (**L5**) carrying the signal of that release to the surface we observe.

**Short-answer structure, every time:** Matter / Energy / Forces — three labelled sentences. That's the M-E-F triangle the whole unit is built on.

| 5 | Earth's interior | How do we investigate the connection between matter in Earth's interior and surface features? | P-waves and S-waves reveal Earth's layers by how their speed changes; S-waves vanishing at the outer core proves it's liquid. **See [`../lesson-05-earths-interior/`](../lesson-05-earths-interior/).** |
| 6 | Convection | How is temperature related to the behavior of matter in the mantle? | Convection: warm = less dense = rises; cool = denser = sinks. **See [`../lesson-06-convection/`](../lesson-06-convection/).** |
| 7 | Radioactive decay | Where does the energy that drives convection come from? | Radioactive decay: unstable nuclei release mass as energy (E=mc²), heating the mantle. **See [`../lesson-07-radioactive-decay/`](../lesson-07-radioactive-decay/).** |
| 8 | Age of rocks | How do we know how old a rock is? | Radiometric dating (half-life). Seafloor age pattern (young near ridges) is evidence for plate tectonics. **See [`../lesson-08-age-of-rocks/`](../lesson-08-age-of-rocks/).** |
| 9 | Rock density | Why do continents and ocean basins sit at different elevations? | Basalt (oceanic, ~3.0 g/cm³) is denser than granite (continental, ~2.7 g/cm³) — isostasy. Density predicts which plate subducts. **See [`../lesson-09-rock-density/`](../lesson-09-rock-density/).** |
| 10 | Plate boundaries | What happens at convergent, divergent, and transform boundaries? | Divergent = new crust; convergent = subduction (direction set by density) or mountain-building; transform = friction, stress, sudden slip, earthquakes only. **See [`../lesson-10-plate-boundaries/`](../lesson-10-plate-boundaries/).** |
| 11–12 *(optional)* | Friction & forces on an incline | What determines whether something on a slope stays put or slides? | Friction coefficient, normal force on an incline — the physics behind the Krakatoa rainstorm-landslide transfer question. |

## Lessons 8–10 vocabulary (extends the Lessons 4–7 vocab guide)

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| Relative dating | Determining the *order* rock layers/events occurred, without a numeric age (e.g., superposition: oldest on bottom). |
| Absolute (radiometric) dating | Determining a numeric age using a radioactive isotope's known, constant half-life. |
| Half-life | The time for half of a radioactive sample to decay from parent to daughter isotope. |
| Basalt | Dense (~3.0 g/cm³), dark, mafic (iron/magnesium-rich) rock that forms oceanic crust. |
| Granite | Less dense (~2.7 g/cm³), light-colored, felsic (silica-rich) rock that forms continental crust. |
| Isostasy | The principle that less-dense crust "floats" higher on the mantle than denser crust, the way objects float at different heights in water based on their density. |
| Divergent boundary | Plates move apart; new crust forms as magma rises to fill the gap. |
| Convergent boundary | Plates move together; denser plate subducts, or (if neither can) crust crumples into mountains. |
| Transform boundary | Plates slide past each other; no crust created/destroyed; friction + stress + sudden slip = earthquakes. |
| Subduction | One tectonic plate forced downward beneath another, usually the denser of the two. |
