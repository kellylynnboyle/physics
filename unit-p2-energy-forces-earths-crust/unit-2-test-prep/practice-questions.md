## Short Answer

**Explain, using Matter / Energy / Forces as three labeled parts of your answer:** why does a region above a subduction zone (like the Sunda Arc, where Krakatoa sits) experience both earthquakes and explosive volcanic eruptions, while a region at a transform boundary (like the San Andreas Fault) experiences earthquakes but essentially no volcanic activity?

*A complete answer should address: what's different about the two boundary types in terms of what happens to the plate material (Matter); where the energy comes from and how it's released in each case (Energy); and what forces are involved — including the role of friction/elastic bonds in both, and the additional role of density/buoyancy at the subduction zone (Forces).*

|---|---|---|
| 1 | B | Newton's 1st law: constant velocity (including rest) means zero *net* force, not zero force. |
| 2 | B | Elastic = reversible deformation within the elastic limit (Lesson 4). |
| 3 | B | Held-but-not-bending-further = balanced; applied force matches restoring bond force (Lesson 4 consensus model). |
| 4 | B | Bonds are electric attraction between particles (protons/electrons) — Lesson 4. |
| 5 | C | Push particles closer than equilibrium separation and repulsion dominates (Lesson 4, Model 2). |
| 6 | B | Springs resist both stretch and compression, matching real bond behavior; strings only pull (Lesson 4). |
| 7 | B | P-waves are faster (~11 km/s vs. ~3 km/s in rock) and always arrive first (Lesson 5). |
| 8 | B | S-waves cannot travel through liquid — their disappearance is the direct evidence for a liquid outer core (Lesson 5). |
| 9 | B | Lithosphere = crust + rigid uppermost mantle = the plates (Lesson 5). |
| 10 | B | Red/negative anomaly = slower P-waves = warmer, less-dense material (Lesson 5). |
| 11 | B | Warm material is less dense; buoyant force exceeds gravity, so it rises (Lesson 6). |
| 12 | C | Radioactive decay is the mantle's heat source driving convection (Lesson 7). |
| 13 | B | Strong force is short-range; more protons means more electric repulsion it can't fully counteract as the nucleus grows (Lesson 7). |
| 14 | B | E=mc²: c² is huge, so small mass loss → large energy release (Lesson 7). |
| 15 | B | 100% → 50% → 25% → 12.5% is three halvings = 3 half-lives (Lesson 8). |
| 16 | B | Constant rate means the parent/daughter ratio is a reliable function of time alone (Lesson 8). |
| 17 | B | Denser basalt subducts beneath less-dense granite (Lesson 9). |
| 18 | B | Only divergent boundaries create new crust, as magma rises to fill the gap (Lesson 10). |
| 19 | A | No subduction/crust creation means no magma source, but friction still locks the fault and lets stress build (Lesson 10 + Lesson 4). |
| 20 | C | "Apparent stability" is energy loading (friction holding the fault) until the elastic limit is exceeded (Lesson 4, applied to Lesson 10). |

**Short answer — target points:** *Matter:* at the subduction zone, oceanic crust is physically forced down into the mantle and can melt into new magma; at a transform boundary, no crust is created, destroyed, or melted — the plates just grind past each other. *Energy:* at the subduction zone, gravitational PE converts to heat as the denser plate sinks, and that heat + trapped water can melt rock into magma, which stores its own chemical/thermal energy until an eruption releases it; at the transform boundary, the only energy in play is elastic energy stored in the locked, stressed rock, released as an earthquake when it exceeds the elastic limit. *Forces:* both settings involve the Lesson 4 bond/friction model (stress builds while a boundary is "locked," then releases suddenly) — but the subduction zone *additionally* involves density-driven buoyancy forces (Lesson 9) forcing one plate beneath the other and generating magma, a force relationship that simply doesn't exist at a transform boundary.
