# Krakatoa Case Study — Anak Krakatau (with Answers & Reasoning)

**OpenSciEd High School Physics · Unit P.2, Lesson 4**

Answer the following questions to explain what causes a volcano to (1) appear stable over long periods of time and then (2) experience sudden catastrophic change.

**The whole idea in one sentence:** A volcano looks stable when the forces on it are balanced, and it changes suddenly when something makes them unbalanced.

---

## Background

Anak Krakatau is a volcano off the coast of Indonesia. It lies near the boundary between the Indo-Australian Plate and the Eurasian Plate. Interactions between these two plates transfer energy into the rock near the boundary. This causes pockets of rock to melt and become magma. Gases in the hot magma put pressure on the solid rock above as a result of particle collisions. The upward force from this pressure can cause the crust to deform over time and eventually break, allowing magma to reach the surface.

### Cue | Notes — quick reference before answering

| Cue | Notes |
|---|---|
| What holds rock together? | Bonds — the electric attraction between the particles. Electric force gets **weaker** as particles get farther apart. Gravity is far too weak to matter at particle scale, and gravity is never a push. |
| What does "stable" mean here? | The forces are balanced, so the net force is about zero and nothing changes its motion. It does **not** mean nothing is happening — energy can load the whole time. |
| What makes it change suddenly? | The forces stop being balanced. That can happen two ways: the upward force grows, or the downward force shrinks. At Anak Krakatau it was the second one. |

## Q1. Which particle-scale interactions explain why the crust above the magma will break?

Which interactions at the particle scale do we need in order to explain why the crust above the magma will break, allowing magma to reach the surface?

- The attraction due to electric forces (bonds) between the particles of rock will **weaken** when the particles are forced apart by the upward forces.
- The attraction due to electric forces (bonds) between the particles of rock will get stronger when the particles are forced apart by the upward forces.
- The attraction due to gravity between the particles of rock and Earth will get stronger when the particles are forced apart by the upward forces.
- The repulsion due to gravity between the particles of rock and Earth will weaken when the particles are forced apart by the upward forces.

> **✅ Answer: Bonds WEAKEN (first option).**
> **Why:** Bonds are electric. Electric attraction gets weaker as particles are pushed farther apart, so once the magma forces them apart far enough, the rock can no longer hold together and it breaks. The two gravity options are out — gravity between particles is far too weak to matter at this scale, and gravity never repels.

## Q2. Stability during the growth period (1927–2018)

The dome of Anak Krakatau first appeared above the ocean in 1927. For almost 100 years it grew very slowly as magma seeped and sputtered from the top — until 2018, it grew taller at a rate of about 2 meters per year.

A physics student modeled the rock/dirt on the volcano's surface as cubes of matter (mass *m*) with two forces: (1) downward weight of the rocks/dirt above (**Fw**), and (2) upward pressure force from heated magma gases below (**Fp**). Using this model, which do you predict about stability and change in the system at this scale?

- There are many forces acting on the matter at this scale, so the system will be unstable.
- The forces on each cube of matter are roughly balanced at this scale, so the system will be stable.
- The upward forces from the magma are much stronger than the forces from the weight of the rock at this scale, so the system will be unstable.
- There are no forces acting on the system, so the system will be stable.

> **✅ Answer: Forces roughly balanced, so stable (second option).**
> **Why:** It grew about 2 m a year for roughly 90 years. Almost no change in motion means the net force was almost zero, so **Fp ≈ Fw**. If the upward force had really been much stronger, it would have erupted long before 2018 — the slow, steady growth *is* the evidence of balance.

## Q3. The December 22, 2018 landslide

A quarter of a square kilometer of land slipped off the side of Anak Krakatau and into the ocean in a massive landslide. Within hours, the side of the volcano exploded, causing a massive wave of water that devastated nearby communities.

Scientists initially thought the eruption triggered the collapse — it turned out to be the reverse: *"At first we thought that, OK, the eruption triggered the collapse,"* said Edo Nurshal, a research assistant at the Bandung Institute of Technology in Indonesia. *"Actually, the condition is vice versa."*

Which free-body diagram (A, B, C, or D) can help explain why the landslide triggered the explosion?

> **✅ Answer: Diagram B.**
> **Why:** The landslide took material away, so the block is drawn smaller (**½m**) and **Fw** is short. **Fp** did not change, so it's still long. Up beats down — unbalanced upward.
> - Diagram A is the volcano *before* the landslide (full mass, balanced).
> - Diagram D has the right arrows but keeps the full mass, so it misses what the landslide actually did.
> - Diagram C points the imbalance the wrong way.

## Q4. What triggered the explosive energy transfer?

Which best explains what might have happened to trigger the explosive transfer of energy after the landslide, using the model you chose above?

- The magnitude of the forces upward from the magma became larger (Fp). The resulting unbalanced forces upward from the magma transferred energy out of the system.
- The magnitude of the forces downward from the weight of the crust (Fw) became larger. The resulting unbalanced forces downward transferred energy out of the system.
- The magnitude of the forces upward from the magma became smaller (Fp). The resulting unbalanced forces downward transferred energy out of the system.
- The magnitude of the forces downward from the weight of the crust (Fw) became smaller. The resulting unbalanced forces upward from the magma (Fp) transferred energy out of the system.

> **✅ Answer: Fw got smaller (fourth option).**
> **Why:** Nothing was added to the magma that morning. A quarter of a square kilometer of the flank slid into the sea, which took the lid off. Less weight pressing down, same push from below, so the net force became upward and energy transferred out of the system.

**Say it this way:** Taking weight off the top is the same as pushing harder from below. Either one breaks the balance.

| Years | Minutes | Hours |
|---|---|---|
| Magma slowly builds the volcano. Fp ≈ Fw. Balanced. Stable. | A quarter square km of the flank slides into the sea. Fw drops. Fp unchanged. | The side of the volcano explodes. Net force up. Unbalanced. Energy out. |

## Q5. Transfer — choose a sudden Earth process

Geysers, landslides, and ice chunks breaking off of a glacier are all processes on Earth's surface that happen very suddenly, like volcanic eruptions do. Choose one of these processes that you think you could explain using ideas from the M-E-F triangle:

1. Geysers that erupt every few hours
2. **Landslides right after a rainstorm** ← the worksheet tells you to use this one, so pick it. (Happens in California.) As the rain falls on a hillside, what happens to the mass of the soil? What would this do to the stability of the soil on the slope?
3. Ice chunks breaking off of a glacier a few days after the warmest month on record

### What happens to the mass of the soil?

Rain soaks in, so the soil takes on water and its mass goes up. More mass means more weight pulling it down the slope.

### What does that do to the stability of the soil on the slope?

Two things happen at once, and both push toward failure:
- The force pulling the soil downhill gets **BIGGER**, because the soil now weighs more.
- The force holding it in place gets **SMALLER**, because water gets between the grains, cuts the friction, and pushes particles slightly apart so they grip each other less.

The forces were balanced during the dry months. Rain pushes one up and the other down until the downhill pull wins, and then the hillside moves all at once.

> **Connects to Lesson 11–12 (Friction & Forces on an Incline):** this rainstorm-landslide question *is* the physics behind that optional extension lesson — friction on an incline, and what reduces the friction coefficient.

### Which lens(es)?

**Stability over time or space** and **Change over time or space.** The question is literally about a long stretch of no change followed by sudden change, and those two lenses name those two states. You can add "thinking across different scales" if you want credit for explaining it with particles, since the real mechanism is grain-to-grain.

### How do those lenses help?

The stability lens says that all through the dry months the forces were balanced. Gravity was pulling the soil downhill the entire time, but friction between the grains matched it, so nothing moved. The change lens says a system only changes when forces become unbalanced. The rain changed both sides of that balance at the same time. The moment the downhill pull was bigger than the friction holding it, the forces were unbalanced and the slope failed. The scale lens ties the two together: what you see as a hillside collapsing is billions of tiny grain contacts letting go at once.

## The pattern, every time

**Same pattern every time:** Foam bending, a volcano growing, a hillside in the rain. Load slowly, hold while the forces stay balanced, then one change tips the balance and it all goes at once.
