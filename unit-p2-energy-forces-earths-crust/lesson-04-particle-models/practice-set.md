## Answer Key

*Check your reasoning, not just your conclusion.*

### Part A — Recall

**A1.** Deformation is any change in shape or size caused by an applied force. Elastic behavior is the specific case where the matter returns to its original shape once the force is removed. So deformation is the change; elastic is a description of whether that change reverses. All elastic behavior involves deformation, but not all deformation is elastic.

**A2.** The elastic limit is the point past which matter no longer returns to its original shape. Beyond it, the solid does not spring back — bonds are stretched past the point where they can pull the particles back to their equilibrium separation, and pieces of the solid can break off.

**A3.** (1) Stability or change over time or space. (2) Thinking across different scales. (3) Cause and effect in matter–energy–force (M-E-F) relationships.

**A4.** Particles are the smallest pieces of matter the model represents — here, atoms and molecules. The word is kept general on purpose, because the same reasoning applies at whatever scale the model happens to be working at.

**A5.** Material, sample length, sample thickness, and magnitude of external force.

**A6.** All changes in matter, at the particle level or the visible level, are the result of changes in motion, and those changes in motion are caused by unbalanced forces.

### Part B — Explain the Mechanism

**B1.** Far apart: attraction dominates, so the net force pulls them together and gets stronger as they approach. Very close: repulsion dominates and rises steeply, pushing them apart. In between there is one separation where attraction and repulsion are equal in magnitude and the net force is zero. That equilibrium separation is the normal bond distance — where particles in an undisturbed solid actually sit. Pull them past it and the net force pulls back; push them closer and the net force pushes back. That restoring behavior in both directions is the whole reason solids hold their shape.

**B2.** Springs. A spring resists being stretched AND resists being compressed, and stores energy either way — which matches the net-force behavior in B1 exactly. A string only pulls: it does nothing when you push the particles together, so it cannot explain why solids resist compression. A rigid stick does neither: it holds particles at a fixed distance with no give, so it cannot explain elastic deformation or stored energy at all.

**B3.** No external force: balanced, net force zero, nothing about the motion is changing. Forces begin bending it: unbalanced, the shape is changing so particles are moving. Held bent without bending further: balanced, the applied force is matched by the restoring force of the stretched bonds. Bending continues and cracks appear: unbalanced, bonds at the crack are being pulled past equilibrium and particles are moving apart. Breaks in two: unbalanced at the instant of release, because the stored energy accelerates the pieces apart, and only balanced again once the pieces have settled into a new stable state. The class phrasing "balanced again once a new balance is reached" is correct, but the qualifier is the important half.

**B4.** It breaks at the thin middle. The internal force has to be carried across whatever cross-section is there. Fewer particles across that section means each one carries a larger share of that force, so the bonds there are stretched furthest and reach the elastic limit first. Same total force, less material to spread it over.

**B5.** (a) Force being applied: energy is transferred into the system by the external force, and as particles are pulled away from their equilibrium separation it is stored in the electric fields between them. (b) Held bent: the energy is sitting in those stretched fields; nothing is flowing in or out and the amount stored is steady. (c) Bonds break: the stored field energy is released, mostly as motion (kinetic energy of the pieces and of the particles), sound, and thermal energy. Note that "elastic deformation" is not one of the release forms — it is the storage mechanism.

**B6.** Because what the whole sample does is the sum of what its particles do. The sample only changes shape if particles move, and particles only move if the net force on them is not zero. Switching scales shows that the visible behavior — holding, bending, breaking — is the same balanced/unbalanced story playing out on every individual particle. It is what connects the macroscopic model to the particle model.

**B7.** Model 1 (foam panels) — advantage: it shows stability and change at the scale you can actually see, and shows that breaking starts at the edges where contact and force concentrate. Limitation: it cannot show a single particle or a bond, so it never explains WHY the foam holds together. Frame that exposes it: thinking across different scales. Model 2 (inverter magnets) — advantage: it shows the cause-and-effect mechanism, that net force depends on separation distance and reverses direction. Limitation: it is only two objects, so it cannot show a whole sample breaking, and magnetic force between poles is not the same as electric force between charges. Frames: stability or change over time or space, and thinking across scales.

### Part C — Transfer

**C1.** The quiet period is the foam being held bent. Friction locks the fault while the plates keep moving, so elastic energy accumulates in the rock for years or centuries without the fault slipping. When the accumulated stress finally exceeds what friction can hold, the fault slips suddenly and releases the stored energy as seismic waves. "Plates move slowly" only explains the rate at which energy loads. It does not explain why release is sudden rather than gradual — that requires friction holding the fault while stress builds. Apparent stability is not nothing happening; it is energy loading.

**C2.** The applied force is magma rising and pressurizing the chamber, driven by subduction of the Indo-Australian Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate. The bonds holding are the strength of the surrounding rock plus the viscous, silica-rich magma trapping its own dissolved gas instead of letting it escape. The break is the point where the confining rock could no longer contain the pressure, releasing it explosively on 26–27 August. Same structure as the foam: steady loading, a limit, then sudden release — with roughly three months between the first precursor activity on 20 May and the climax.

**C3.** Model 1 answers WHERE. It shows that plate edges are rough and irregular, that contact happens at limited points, and that force concentrates there — so breaking starts at the edge rather than in the middle of a plate. But it cannot say why the rock held for a century and then let go in seconds. Model 2 answers WHEN. It shows that particles are held at an equilibrium separation by a net force that pulls back when stretched, and that this holding has a limit. Once you have both, you get a complete answer: force concentrates at the boundary (Model 1) and bonds there hold until they reach their limit, at which point they release all at once (Model 2).

**C4.** Energy is conserved — it is never destroyed, only transferred or converted. Before the break, energy is stored in the stretched electric fields between particles. At the break it converts into kinetic energy of the moving pieces and particles, sound (which is why you hear a snap), and thermal energy. The total is the same before and after; only the form changed. If it really were destroyed, the pieces would not fly apart and there would be no sound.

### Part D — Challenge

**D1.** It predicts that a solid resists being compressed just as it resists being stretched, and that squeezing it stores energy too. Push particles closer than their equilibrium separation and repulsion rises steeply, pushing back. That does match experience: solid rock is extremely hard to compress, and the repulsion climbs much faster with distance than the attraction does, which is why solids are far easier to stretch or break than to squash. It is also why the string analogy fails — a string would predict no resistance to compression at all.

**D2.** It suggests the explosions and the waves were produced by two different mechanisms rather than the blast directly pushing the water. The likely wave sources are the collapse of the volcano into its emptied magma chamber, and pyroclastic flows pouring huge volumes of material into the sea — both of which displace water on their own timescale, offset from the explosions. To test it: compare tide-gauge arrival times against the recorded explosion times and work backward to the origin time and location of each wave; if the back-calculated source times cluster with collapse and flow events rather than with the blasts, that supports the two-mechanism explanation. Deposit mapping around the caldera would give independent evidence of how much material entered the water and when.
