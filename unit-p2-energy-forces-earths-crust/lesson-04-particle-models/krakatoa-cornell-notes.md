**Unit question:** How do forces in Earth's interior determine what will happen to the surface we see?
**Question for these notes:** Krakatoa built pressure for three months and released it in one morning. What was loading it, what was holding it, and where did the energy go?

## Cue / Question | Notes

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| Where is Krakatoa, and why is there a volcano there at all? | In the Sunda Strait, between Java and Sumatra in Indonesia. It sits on the Sunda Arc, directly above the subduction zone where the Indo-Australian Plate descends beneath the Eurasian Plate. The descending slab carries water down with it; that water lowers the melting point of the mantle above it, so rock melts and the melt rises. |
| Why was it explosive instead of flowing lava? | The melt there is silica-rich (andesitic to rhyolitic), which makes it viscous. Viscous magma traps its dissolved gas instead of letting it bubble out, so pressure builds until the confining rock fails. Runny basaltic magma lets gas escape continuously, which is why Hawaii gets lava flows rather than blasts. Same planet, different magma chemistry, completely different behavior. |
| What was the timeline? | 20 May 1883: first ash column, explosions heard 160 km away in Batavia. Activity died down by the end of May, then resumed 19 June and intensified through the summer. 26 Aug, about 1 pm: blasts roughly every ten minutes, ash column near 27 km. 27 Aug: four enormous explosions, the third at 10:02 am being the largest. The eruption was not declared over until 21 October. |
| How does this connect to Lesson 4? | The structure is identical to the foam. Steady loading, a long stretch of apparent stability, a limit, then sudden release. Roughly three months of energy loading was released in a single morning. The foam is held bent for a while and looks like nothing is happening; the same was true here. |
| What actually caused the biggest explosions? | Contested. The exit-ticket answer is **phreatomagmatic** — seawater reaching the magma and flashing to steam. Two rival explanations are over-pressuring from volatile saturation, and magma mixing, where hot basaltic magma was injected into cooler, lighter magma. The evidence against seawater acting alone is that the pumice and ignimbrite deposits are not the kind a magma–seawater interaction produces. Give the expected answer, but know it is a hypothesis. |

> **Read the loading-and-release shape carefully:** the underlying loading curve is a conceptual model, not measured data — nobody instrumented the magma chamber in 1883. What's real is the *shape*: slow loading, apparent stability, sudden release. That shape is the whole reason Krakatoa belongs in this unit.

## What happened, in numbers

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| What happened to the island itself? | Over 70% of it was destroyed. It did not simply blow apart — it collapsed into the emptied magma chamber beneath it, forming a caldera. Only the cone of Rakata was left, and half of that slid underwater. A new island, Anak Krakatau ("child of Krakatoa"), emerged in the caldera in 1928 and is still active — see [`krakatoa-case-study.md`](krakatoa-case-study.md) for its 2018 eruption. |
| How loud was it, and how far was it heard? | It is the loudest sound in recorded history, audible across more than 10% of Earth's surface. Heard in Perth, Australia, 3,110 km away, and on Rodrigues near Mauritius, 4,800 km away, where it was mistaken for distant cannon fire. Sailors 64 km away had their eardrums ruptured. The barometer at the Batavia gasworks, 160 km away, spiked 2.5 inches of mercury (8.5 kPa) and went off the scale. |
| What did the pressure wave do? | Recorded on barographs worldwide, detected seven separate times over five days — four passes travelling outward to the antipodal point and three coming back. That works out to the wave circling the globe three and a half times. It radiated outward at about 1,086 km/h. |
| What did the tsunamis do? | Run-up reached 42 m on the west coast of Java and 24 m on the south coast of Sumatra. More than 300 villages and settlements were destroyed. Official death toll 36,417, of which roughly 90% were caused by the waves and about 10% by pyroclastic flows. The waves were generated by caldera collapse and by pyroclastic flows entering the sea, which is why their arrival times do not match the explosions. |
| What were the global effects? | About 20 million tons of sulfur formed sulfate aerosols in the stratosphere. NOAA records global temperatures falling by as much as 0.5 °C in the following year, not returning to normal until 1888. Vivid red sunsets appeared worldwide for months — intense enough that fire engines were called out in New York and New Haven for what looked like a distant blaze. |
| How much energy was released? | Roughly 200 megatons of TNT, about 840 petajoules (8.4 × 10¹⁷ J). That is around four times Tsar Bomba, the most powerful thermonuclear weapon ever detonated, and it makes Krakatoa one of the largest explosions in recorded history. |

## Frequently Asked in Physics Class

These are the questions that actually come up when a physics class covers Krakatoa — the ones about mechanism and numbers rather than dates. Several have answers that surprise people.

**Q1. Why do some sources say 172 dB and others say 180 dB?**
Both come from the same barometer at Batavia, 160 km away. The disagreement is over which pressure value to use, not the physics. Sound pressure level is `L = 20 log₁₀(p / 20 µPa)`. Using the gauge spike of 8.5 kPa gives `20 log₁₀(8,500 / 0.00002) ≈ 173 dB`. Using the 20 kPa figure some sources cite gives exactly 180 dB. Run both and see for yourself — this is the single best worked example in the whole event.

**Q2. Why is about 194 dB the loudest a sound can be?**
A sound wave is pressure oscillating above and below atmospheric pressure, roughly 101 kPa. The trough cannot go below a vacuum, so the largest possible amplitude is about 101 kPa. That gives `20 log₁₀(101,325 / 0.00002) ≈ 194 dB`. Past that the disturbance stops being a symmetrical oscillation and becomes a shock wave: the air is pushed along rather than just vibrated in place.

**Q3. Then how can anyone claim 310 dB at the source?**
They cannot, not as sound. That number is shock-wave overpressure pushed through the decibel formula for comparison. The arithmetic still works; the thing being described is no longer a sound wave. Spotting this is a better answer than memorizing the number.

**Q4. Why did it get so much quieter with distance?**
Spreading, mostly. Energy from a point source spreads over a sphere, so intensity falls as 1/r² — about −6 dB for every doubling of distance, before atmospheric absorption. Going from 160 km to 4,800 km is 30 times the distance, which is roughly −29 dB from spreading alone. That is why it went from bursting eardrums to sounding like far-off gunfire.

**Q5. If the wave was 42 m at the coast, how big was it in the open ocean?**
Under a metre. In deep water a tsunami is a very long, very fast, very low wave — ships at sea often do not notice one pass. As it reaches shallow water it slows down, and because energy flux is conserved the height has to rise. Green's law gives wave amplitude proportional to water depth raised to the power −1/4, so a 400-fold drop in depth amplifies the wave about 4.5 times on its own; the rest of the growth comes from bay funnelling and run-up. Green's law itself breaks down right at the shore, where the wave goes nonlinear.

**Q6. Why did the tsunami not arrive at the same moment as the bang?**
Different mechanisms and different media. The pressure wave moved through air at about 1,086 km/h. A tsunami travels at `√(gd)`, where d is water depth — roughly 700 km/h across a 4 km-deep ocean, but only tens of km/h through the shallow Sunda Strait. More importantly the waves were not made by the bang — they came from caldera collapse and from pyroclastic flows dumping cubic kilometres of material into the sea, on their own timing. The mismatch is the evidence for two separate mechanisms.

**Q7. Why did seawater make the eruption worse instead of putting it out?**
Phase change. Liquid water flashing to steam expands by a factor of roughly 1,700 at atmospheric pressure, and far more violently against magma at over 1,000 °C. The energy driving that expansion is thermal energy already stored in the magma. The water is not acting as a coolant, it is acting as a working fluid — a way of converting stored thermal energy into kinetic energy very fast.

**Q8. Why did the ash cool the planet instead of trapping heat like a greenhouse gas?**
Because of what it does to incoming light rather than outgoing heat. Sulfate aerosols sit in the stratosphere, above the weather, and scatter incoming sunlight back to space. They are reflective, not absorbing, and being above the rain they are not washed out, so they persist for years. Greenhouse gases work the other way round, on the outgoing infrared.

**Q9. Why were the sunsets red?**
Scattering, exaggerated. At sunset light takes a long slant path through the atmosphere, so short wavelengths are scattered out of the beam and what reaches your eye is the long-wavelength end. The aerosol layer added a second, much higher scattering layer with particles comparable in size to visible wavelengths, which is why the effect was so intense and lasted for months rather than minutes.

**Q10. How can we say the sound "circled the globe" — does sound not die out?**
Ordinary sound dies out, but this was an atmospheric pressure wave, and the atmosphere behaves like a resonant shell. Once the wave dropped below the threshold of hearing it kept propagating as a measurable pressure disturbance. Barographs, not ears, tracked it — seven passes in five days.

**Q11. Could we predict an eruption like this today?**
Partly. Seismometers pick up earthquake swarms as magma moves, GPS and satellite radar (InSAR) detect the ground bulging as a chamber inflates, and gas sensors track rising SO₂. We can often say an eruption is likely within weeks. We still cannot reliably say which day or how big. That is exactly the Lesson 4 problem: you can measure the loading, but the release depends on when the material reaches its limit.

**Q12. Is this an earthquake question or a volcano question?**
Both, and that is the point of the unit. The same particle-level story — forces holding matter together, energy stored in fields, a limit, sudden release — explains an earthquake at a plate boundary and an eruption above a subduction zone. Only the material and the timescale change.
