## Cue Column | Notes

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| **L3 recap** — What happens to matter when force is applied? What happens to the energy while the force is applied, and when it is removed? | **Elastic behavior:** matter deforms under an applied force but returns to its original shape once the force is removed, as long as it stays within its elastic limit. **Deformation:** a change in shape or size caused by an applied force. While matter is held deformed, energy transferred in by the applied force is stored in the fields between particles; when the force is removed, that stored energy transfers back out and the shape returns. |
| Why look at more than one model? | Every model has advantages (merits) **and** limitations. Knowing both lets you move flexibly between models and build a more complete explanation than any single model gives alone. Each model is judged against **three frames**: (1) stability or change over time/space, (2) thinking across different scales, (3) cause and effect in **M-E-F** (matter–energy–force) relationships. |
| **Model 1** — two foam panels slipping/breaking. What question does it help answer? | How the edges of the foam (image A) better represent the structure of real plates along their boundaries than a simpler image (B). It links the rough, irregular shape of a real plate boundary to where breaking actually starts. |
| What does Model 1 say happens right before plates slip (causing an earthquake)? | Force concentrates where the panels actually touch. Breaking, fracturing, and cracking start **at the edge** rather than farther back, because the contact area there is small, so the force carried per unit of area is highest there. |
| What are "particles" in our models? | The general name for the smallest pieces of matter a model represents — here, atoms and molecules. Same idea used throughout the course; only the **scale** changes. |
| Two ideas about solids at the particle level | (1) All solids elastically deform up to a point. (2) Pieces of a solid can break off once matter reaches its **elastic limit**. |
| **Model 2** — inverter magnets. What does it model, and what's the key relationship? | Ring ("inverter") magnets stand in for the electric attraction/repulsion between particles. The key relationship: particles attract at larger separations, repel strongly when pushed very close, and rest at an **equilibrium separation** where net force is zero. (Video 2: predict the net force as magnets are brought together, then compare repulsive vs. attractive force magnitude at that point.) |
| What is a bond, and what were the three analogies? | A bond is the net attractive interaction holding two particles near their equilibrium separation. Modeled as **strings, springs, or rigid sticks**. **Springs** is strongest: a spring pulls back when stretched *and* pushes back when compressed, storing energy either way. A string only pulls; a rigid stick does neither. |
| What did the class decide the simulation needed to include? | Parts/interactions to investigate which characteristics of matter affect particle interactions, plus something to visualize/measure — force, distance between particles, and energy stored. |
| **Model 3** — the particle-level simulation. What were the four investigations? | Choose one independent variable: material, sample length, sample thickness, or magnitude of external force. **Inv. 1:** test your IV against your chosen dependent variable. **Inv. 2:** run to a new stable state, then REMOVE EXTERNAL FORCE and record what happens to matter and stored energy. **Inv. 3:** switch scales — inspect a single particle and track forces/net force as matter moves between stable states. **Inv. 4:** push matter just beyond its elastic limit and record what happens to the particles. |
| Why keep the other variables constant? | So any change in the dependent variable can be attributed to the one variable you changed. Change two things at once and you can't tell which one caused the effect. |

## Consensus Model — Balanced vs. Unbalanced Forces on the Foam

*(Worked out together as a class — this is the confirmed sequence, not a guess.)*

| Condition | Balanced or unbalanced? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| No external forces acting on the foam | **Balanced** | Net force is zero, so nothing about the motion is changing. |
| Forces start bending the foam | **Unbalanced** | Net force is not zero — the shape is changing, so particles are moving. |
| Forces stop bending it but hold it bent | **Balanced** | The applied force is matched by the restoring force of the stretched bonds. The shape is held, not changing. |
| Forces continue bending it and cracks appear | **Unbalanced** | Bonds at the crack are being pulled past equilibrium and breaking; particles there are moving apart. |
| The foam breaks in two pieces | **Unbalanced at the instant of release, then balanced** | Class consensus: "balanced again, once a new balance is reached." The moment bonds let go, stored energy accelerates the pieces apart (unbalanced) — forces are balanced again only once the pieces settle into a new stable state. |

**Big idea:** All changes in matter — at the particle level or the macroscopic (visible) level — result from changes in **motion**, caused by unbalanced forces.

**Where does the foam break, and why?**
- It breaks where the cross-section has **fewer particles** than the rest of the sample.
- Fewer particles across that section → each particle carries a **larger share of the internal force** → that section reaches its elastic limit first.

**What held the matter together before it cracked?**
- Bonds between particles, produced by **electric attraction/repulsion between protons and electrons** — fields generated by matter result in forces.
- These same forces explain why *every* solid — tectonic plates included — elastically deforms up to a point before breaking.

**Where does the energy go?**
- While matter is stretched/bent, energy transferred in by the external force is stored in the **electric fields between particles**. That storage is **not unlimited** — the ceiling is tied to the elastic limit.
- When bonds break, the stored field energy converts mostly into **motion (kinetic energy)**, **sound**, and **thermal energy**.
- ⚠️ **Watch this one:** "elastic deformation" is how energy is *stored*, not a form it turns into when released. The macroscopic energy outputs in this course are motion, sound, light, and thermal energy.

**Connecting to real plates:**
- Class answer: real plates move very slowly and involve enormous numbers of particles, so there are long periods of apparent stability between earthquakes even though stress is building the whole time.
- Sharper version: **friction locks the fault** while the plates keep moving. Elastic energy accumulates in the rock for years or centuries. When accumulated stress finally exceeds what friction can hold, the fault slips suddenly and releases that stored energy as seismic waves.
- "Apparent stability" does **not** mean nothing is happening — it means energy is loading. That's exactly the foam being held bent.

## Krakatoa Exit Ticket — How It Connects

Krakatoa's 1883 eruption is a large-scale, real-world example of matter reaching — and violently exceeding — its limits under stress. Precursor activity began 20 May 1883, quieted by end of May, resumed 19 June, and built to the climax on 26–27 August — roughly **three months of loading before release**. (Note: this unit's own anchoring phenomenon is the 2005 rift in the Afar region of Ethiopia; Krakatoa is an added real-world example — see the dedicated [Krakatoa case study](krakatoa-case-study.md) and [Krakatoa Cornell notes](krakatoa-cornell-notes.md) in this folder.)

| # | Question focus | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tectonic cause of the volcanism | **Subduction zone** — the Indo-Australian Plate moves beneath the Eurasian Plate (Sunda Arc) |
| 2 | Cause of the violent explosions | **Phreatomagmatic explosions** — seawater reaching the magma and flashing to steam *(leading hypothesis, not settled — see case study)* |
| 3 | Deadliest secondary event | **Tsunamis** striking the coasts of Java and Sumatra |
| 4 | Global atmospheric phenomenon afterward | **Vivid sunsets** worldwide, from volcanic aerosols in the stratosphere |
