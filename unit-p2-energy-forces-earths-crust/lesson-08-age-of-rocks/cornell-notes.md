## Why this lesson comes right after Lesson 7

Lesson 7 established that radioactive decay happens at the *nuclear* scale, converting a small amount of mass into a large amount of energy (E = mc²) — that's what powers convection. This lesson uses the **same decay process** for a completely different purpose: since decay happens at a **fixed, predictable rate** for a given isotope, it can be used as a **clock** to measure how long a rock has existed. Same phenomenon (Lesson 7's cause-effect chain), new question.

## Cue / Question | Notes

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| Relative age vs. absolute age | **Relative dating** tells you the *order* things happened (this layer is older than that one) but not a number. **Absolute dating** (radiometric dating) gives an actual age in years. |
| Key relative-dating idea: superposition | In an undisturbed sequence of rock layers, the oldest layer is on the **bottom** and the youngest is on **top** — newer material is deposited over older material. |
| What is a half-life? | The time it takes for **half** of a sample of a radioactive ("parent") isotope to decay into its stable ("daughter") isotope. Half-life is a fixed property of each isotope — it doesn't speed up or slow down with temperature, pressure, or chemical state. |
| Why is a constant half-life what makes this a "clock"? | If you know the half-life, and you can measure the current ratio of parent isotope to daughter isotope in a rock, you can calculate how many half-lives have passed since the rock formed — and therefore its age. This is exactly the same logic as the seismic-wave-speed clock in Lesson 5: measure something in the present, use a known constant rate to work backward to a hidden fact about the past. |
| Common isotope pairs used for rocks | **Potassium-40 → Argon-40** (half-life ≈ 1.25 billion years) — good for dating volcanic rock, since argon gas escapes molten rock but gets trapped once it solidifies. **Uranium-238 → Lead-206** (half-life ≈ 4.5 billion years) — good for very old rocks, like Earth's oldest minerals or meteorites. *(Carbon-14, half-life ≈ 5,730 years, is only useful for once-living material and far too short-lived for dating rock — a common mix-up.)* |
| How this connects to plate tectonics (the unit's big question) | Radiometric dating of oceanic crust reveals a striking pattern: crust gets **progressively older** the farther it is from a mid-ocean ridge, and **younger** near the ridge. That age pattern is direct evidence that new crust is continuously created at ridges and spreads outward — one of the strongest pieces of evidence for plate tectonics (setting up Lesson 10). |

## Half-life practice

If a rock sample originally had 100% parent isotope, and now contains 25% parent isotope (75% has decayed into daughter isotope), how many half-lives have passed?

> 100% → 50% (1 half-life) → 25% (2 half-lives). **Two half-lives have passed.**

If the isotope's half-life is 1.25 billion years, how old is the rock?

> 2 half-lives × 1.25 billion years = **2.5 billion years old.**

Try it yourself: a sample now contains 12.5% of its original parent isotope. How many half-lives is that, and how old is the sample if the half-life is 1.25 billion years?
