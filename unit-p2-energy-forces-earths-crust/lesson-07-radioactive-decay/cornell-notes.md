**Unit question:** How do forces in Earth's interior determine what will happen to the surface we see?
**Lesson 7 question:** Where does the energy that drives convection come from?
**What we figured out (in one line):** Radioactive decay.

---

## The cause-effect chain (memorize this order — it's the backbone of the whole unit)

**Larger atoms** → forces in the nucleus become unbalanced (attract and repel forces are different strengths) → **the nucleus breaks apart** (repel wins) → protons/particles leave the nucleus → **the atom becomes a new atom** (identity = number of protons) → some mass has turned into kinetic energy (**KE = ½mv²**) → fast particles collide with surrounding atoms → **temperature rises** (temperature is a measure of particle motion) → hot material is less dense and rises relative to cooler material → **CONVECTION**.

---

## M-E-F for radioactive decay

| Lens | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Matter** | Nuclei release particles and change identity. |
| **Energy** | Temperature increases, more particle motion; energy transfers by collisions from one particle to the next. |
| **Forces** | The **strong force** holds the nucleus together; as nuclei get larger the forces become unbalanced and it breaks apart. |

**Scale:** decay is tiny and fast. Particle movement in the mantle is the middle step. Convection is enormous and takes millions of years. **Micro-scale motion causes macro-scale change** — the same "switch scales to explain the whole system" move from Lesson 4 (particle bonds → foam breaking → plate boundaries).

## Cue / Question | Notes

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| Why do large nuclei become unstable? | Two forces act inside a nucleus: the **electric force** (protons repel each other — same charge) and the **strong nuclear force** (attracts protons and neutrons at very short range, strong enough to overcome the electric repulsion). As a nucleus gets bigger, the strong force's short range means it can't reach across the whole nucleus as effectively, while every added proton adds more electric repulsion. Eventually the balance tips and the repulsion wins. |
| What actually happens during decay? | The unstable nucleus ejects particles (this course covers it qualitatively — alpha, beta, and gamma types exist, though the exact type isn't the focus). Losing protons changes *which element* the atom is — identity is defined by proton count, so the atom becomes a genuinely different element. |
| Where does the released energy come from? | **E = mc².** A tiny amount of mass doesn't survive the reaction — it converts directly into energy. Because c² is an enormous number (9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s²), even a minuscule mass loss releases a huge amount of energy — enough, multiplied across countless decay events over billions of years, to keep heating the mantle the whole time. |
| How does that energy become heat? | The energy first appears as **kinetic energy** of the ejected particles (KE = ½mv²) — they're moving fast. Those fast particles collide with surrounding atoms, transferring motion outward collision by collision. More particle motion **is** higher temperature — temperature is a measure of average particle kinetic energy, not a separate thing. |
| Why is this the missing piece from Lesson 6? | Lesson 6 showed *that* uneven heating drives convection, using an external burner in the tank. Earth has no external burner — Lesson 7 answers where the mantle's own heat source is: radioactive decay of unstable isotopes (like uranium, thorium, potassium) distributed through the mantle and crust, acting as billions of tiny, distributed heat sources instead of one burner at the bottom. |
