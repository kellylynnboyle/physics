## Lesson 8 — Age of Rocks

**1.** A rock sample now contains 25% of its original parent isotope. How many half-lives have passed?

**2.** If that isotope has a half-life of 700 million years, how old is the rock?

**3.** Explain why a radioactive isotope's half-life being *constant* (not affected by heat, pressure, or chemistry) is essential for radiometric dating to work.

**4.** Seafloor rock gets older the farther it is from a mid-ocean ridge. What does this pattern provide evidence for?

## Lesson 9 — Rock Density

**5.** A sample of granite has a mass of 135 g and a volume of 50 cm³. Calculate its density and state whether it's consistent with typical continental crust.

**6.** Explain, using the word "buoyancy," why continents sit at a higher elevation than ocean basins.

**7.** At an oceanic–continental convergent boundary, which plate subducts, and why — using density alone?

**8.** Connect this lesson's buoyancy idea back to Lesson 6's convection M-E-F box. What's the same about the two situations?

## Lesson 10 — Plate Boundaries

**9.** Name the three plate boundary types and give a real-world example of each.

**10.** Explain all three sub-cases of convergent boundaries (oceanic–continental, oceanic–continental, continental–continental) and what each produces at the surface.

**11.** Why do transform boundaries produce earthquakes but not volcanoes? Use ideas from Lesson 4.

**12.** Write the whole unit's cause-effect chain in one connected paragraph: start at radioactive decay and end at an earthquake or eruption at the surface.

## Answer Key

**1.** 100% → 50% (1 half-life) → 25% (2 half-lives). **Two half-lives.**

**2.** 2 × 700 million years = **1.4 billion years old.**

**3.** If the decay rate could change with conditions, the same parent-to-daughter ratio could mean different ages depending on the rock's history (how hot it got, what pressure it experienced) — you couldn't use it as a reliable clock. A constant rate means the ratio depends on time alone, so it can be inverted to solve for age.

**4.** Evidence that new crust is continuously created at mid-ocean ridges and spreads outward over time (seafloor spreading) — a core piece of evidence for plate tectonics.

**5.** `ρ = m/V = 135 g ÷ 50 cm³ = 2.7 g/cm³`. Yes — this matches typical granite/continental crust density (~2.7 g/cm³).

**6.** Both continental and oceanic crust float on the denser asthenosphere below, similar to objects floating in water. Continental crust (granite, ~2.7 g/cm³) is less dense than oceanic crust (basalt, ~3.0 g/cm³), so the buoyant force relative to its weight pushes it to "float" higher — resulting in continents sitting at higher elevation than ocean basins.

**7.** The oceanic plate subducts, because it's denser (basalt, ~3.0 g/cm³) than the continental plate (granite, ~2.7 g/cm³). Gravity pulls the denser material down relative to the less-dense material beside it, the same way a denser object sinks relative to a less-dense one in water.

**8.** Both are a buoyancy-vs-gravity balance driven by density differences. In Lesson 6, warmer (less dense) mantle rock rises and cooler (denser) rock sinks. Here, less-dense granite "floats" higher and denser basalt is forced down at convergent boundaries. Same physical principle — density difference creates a net force — applied at two different scales (small mantle parcels vs. entire plates).

**9.** **Divergent** (plates move apart) — mid-ocean ridges, or the Afar region, Ethiopia. **Convergent** (plates move together) — the Andes (oceanic-continental), Japan (oceanic-oceanic), or the Himalayas (continental-continental). **Transform** (plates slide past each other) — the San Andreas Fault, California.

**10.** Oceanic–continental: the denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the continental plate, producing volcanic arcs, deep trenches, and sometimes explosive eruptions (e.g., Krakatoa) if the magma is silica-rich. Oceanic–oceanic: the older, denser oceanic plate subducts beneath the younger one, producing volcanic island arcs and trenches (e.g., Japan). Continental–continental: neither plate is dense enough to subduct, so the crust crumples and thickens instead, producing very tall mountain ranges with no volcanic activity (e.g., the Himalayas).

**11.** No crust is created or destroyed at a transform boundary, and no plate is forced down into the mantle where it could melt into magma — the plates simply grind past each other horizontally. Friction locks the fault while the plates keep trying to move (Lesson 4's foam-being-held-bent model), so elastic stress builds up until it exceeds what friction can hold, at which point the fault slips suddenly, releasing the stored energy as an earthquake. Without subduction or new crust formation, there's no mechanism to generate the magma that would produce a volcano.

**12.** Radioactive decay in unstable nuclei (Lesson 7) converts a small amount of mass into a large amount of kinetic energy, heating the mantle. That heat drives convection (Lesson 6): hot, less-dense mantle rock rises, cool, denser rock sinks, in slow-moving cells. Rising convection currents pull plates apart at divergent boundaries, creating new, dense basaltic crust (Lessons 8 and 9) that ages and cools as it spreads away from the ridge. Where plates converge, density differences (Lesson 9) determine which plate is forced beneath the other, or whether neither can subduct and the crust crumples instead (Lesson 10). At boundaries where plates are locked by friction, elastic stress accumulates over years to centuries (Lesson 4) — an apparent stability that is really energy loading — until the accumulated stress exceeds the elastic limit and releases suddenly as an earthquake or, above a subduction zone, a volcanic eruption, with seismic waves (Lesson 5) carrying the signal of that release out to the surface we observe.
