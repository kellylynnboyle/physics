## Cue / Question | Notes

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| P-waves vs. S-waves | Both are waves that transfer energy. **P-waves:** compression (push-pull), move through **ALL** materials (solid, liquid, gas), faster through solid, ~11 km/s in rock. **S-waves:** side-to-side (transverse), **cannot** move through liquid, ~3 km/s in rock. **P-waves always arrive first** — that's how the P–S arrival-time gap tells you distance to the source. |
| The speed calculations (v = d ÷ t) | S-wave at 2,000 km in 11 min → **182 km/min**. P-wave at 2,000 km in 3 min → **667 km/min**. P-wave through the liquid core: 12,100 km in 22 min → 550 km/min ÷ 60 → **9.17 km/s**, which matches the ~9 km/s given for liquid iron. |
| What is an anomaly, and what does it tell us? | Data that differ from what we'd expect. A change in pattern in the wave data indicates a new material. The big one: **S-waves disappear and P-waves slow down at the outer core → the outer core is liquid.** |
| Earth's layers | Crust · Mantle (solid magnesium-iron silicate) · Outer core (liquid iron) · Inner core (solid iron). Earth has layers because wave speeds change at each boundary. |
| Moho, lithosphere, asthenosphere | **Moho** = the crust/mantle boundary (Mohorovičić, 1909). **Lithosphere** = crust + the rigid top of the mantle = the tectonic plates. **Asthenosphere** = softer solid mantle below that, which the plates float on. Lower mantle beneath. |
| Reading a tomography image (SubMachine) | **Positive velocity anomaly = blue = P-waves travel faster = cooler matter.** **Negative velocity anomaly = red = slower = warmer matter.** |
| What the six locations showed | **Afar:** hot spot in the mantle (mountains around a desert plain, volcanoes). **Central US / Kansas:** cooler in general (flat farmland). **Costa Rica:** hot spot near the surface (mountains, volcanoes). **Hawaii:** HUGE hot spot under the surface. **Himalayas:** cold at the surface (mountains). **Our location:** hot spot near the surface (Shasta etc.). |
| Pattern across locations | Volcanoes and active surface sit above **warm** mantle. Old mountain belts and stable plains sit above **cool** mantle. |

## Why does this method even work? (the logic behind seismic tomography)

1. Seismic wave *speed* depends on the temperature, density, and phase (solid/liquid) of the material it's passing through.
2. We can't drill to the mantle or core — the deepest hole ever drilled (Kola Superdeep) barely scratches the crust.
3. But earthquakes send P- and S-waves through the *whole* Earth, and seismometers all over the world record when each wave arrives.
4. Comparing actual travel times to what a uniform Earth would predict reveals **anomalies** — and those anomalies map out hot vs. cool regions and solid vs. liquid boundaries, the same way an X-ray or ultrasound images the inside of something you can't cut open.

This is the same "particle interactions explain macroscopic behavior" logic from Lesson 4, just run in reverse: instead of watching particles to predict wave behavior, we watch wave behavior to infer what the particles (and the material) must be doing.

## Worked example — practice the v = d ÷ t skill

A seismic station records an S-wave arriving from an earthquake 2,000 km away, 11 minutes after the quake.

1. `v = d ÷ t = 2,000 km ÷ 11 min ≈ 182 km/min`
2. Convert to km/s: `182 km/min ÷ 60 ≈ 3.03 km/s` — matches the ~3 km/s expected for an S-wave in rock. ✅ consistency check passed.

Try it yourself: a P-wave from the same quake, at the same station, arrives after only 3 minutes. Find its speed in km/min and km/s, and confirm it's consistent with ~11 km/s for a P-wave in rock.
