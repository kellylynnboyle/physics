## The three boundary types

| Boundary type | Plates | What happens | M-E-F in one line | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Divergent** | Move **apart** | Magma rises to fill the gap; **new (basaltic) crust forms**, per Lesson 9 | Matter: new basalt created. Energy: mantle heat (L7) driving upwelling. Forces: convection's rising limb (L6) pulls plates apart. | Mid-ocean ridges; the **Afar region, Ethiopia** — this unit's own anchoring phenomenon, where a rift is actively splitting a continent apart |
| **Convergent** | Move **together** | Depends on crust type — see the three cases below | Matter: crust destroyed (subducted) or crumpled. Energy: gravitational PE converts to kinetic energy/heat as denser plate sinks. Forces: density (L9) decides which plate is forced down; convection's sinking limb (L6) pulls it under. | See cases below |
| **Transform** | Slide **past** each other | No crust created or destroyed; **friction locks the fault** while plates keep moving, stress builds, then releases suddenly | Matter/Energy/Forces: this is Lesson 4's foam model, at full scale — elastic energy loads in the locked rock until the elastic limit is exceeded, then it slips. | **San Andreas Fault**, California |

### The three cases of convergent boundaries

1. **Oceanic–continental:** the denser oceanic (basaltic) plate subducts beneath the less-dense continental (granitic) plate (Lesson 9's density prediction). The subducting slab carries water down, lowering the mantle's melting point above it — melted rock rises as magma. Produces volcanic arcs, deep ocean trenches, and — if the magma is silica-rich and viscous — explosive eruptions like **Krakatoa/Anak Krakatau** (Lesson 4). Also produces the Andes.
2. **Oceanic–oceanic:** both plates are basaltic and similar in density, but the older, cooler, denser one subducts beneath the younger one. Produces volcanic island arcs and deep trenches. **Japan** sits above this kind of boundary — hence frequent earthquakes, volcanoes, and tsunamis (referenced back in Lesson 6's notes).
3. **Continental–continental:** neither plate is dense enough to subduct — both are too buoyant (Lesson 9's isostasy). Instead, the crust crumples, thickens, and pushes upward. Produces the tallest mountain ranges on Earth, with **no** volcanic activity. **The Himalayas** — India colliding with Asia — are the textbook example, and match Lesson 5's tomography reading of "cold at the surface, mountains" (no rising magma involved).

## Cue / Question | Notes

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| Why don't transform boundaries create volcanoes? | No crust is created or destroyed, and no plate is forced down into the mantle where it could melt — the plates just grind past each other horizontally. All the action is mechanical (friction, stress, sudden slip), which is exactly why transform boundaries are earthquake factories but not volcano factories. |
| Why is Krakatoa (Lesson 4) explosive but Hawaii is not? | Krakatoa sits above a subduction zone (oceanic–continental convergent boundary) — silica-rich, viscous magma traps gas until it fails catastrophically. Hawaii sits above a mantle hot spot, **not** a plate boundary at all — its basaltic magma is runny and lets gas escape continuously, producing flows instead of explosions. Same planet, different boundary type (or no boundary at all), completely different eruption style. |
| How does the whole unit's chain read as one sentence? | Radioactive decay (L7) heats the mantle → convection (L6) circulates it → convection drags plates apart at ridges, creating new dense basaltic crust (L8, L9) → and pulls plates together elsewhere, where density (L9) decides which one subducts → stress builds at locked boundaries until bonds/faults reach their elastic limit (L4) → earthquake or eruption, releasing the stored energy at the surface (L5's waves carry the signal out). |
