## Learning objectives by topic

### 1. Kinematics
- [ ] Distinguish distance from displacement, and speed from velocity.
- [ ] Define acceleration and identify its sign relative to velocity's sign.
- [ ] Read and construct position–time, velocity–time, and acceleration–time graphs, including converting between them (slope and area under the curve).
- [ ] Select and apply the correct kinematic equation from the four standard equations for a given set of knowns/unknowns.
- [ ] Analyze free-fall motion, including objects thrown upward.
- [ ] Solve multi-stage motion problems (e.g., accelerate then constant velocity).

### 2. Projectile Motion
- [ ] State and apply the principle that horizontal and vertical motion are independent.
- [ ] Resolve an initial velocity into horizontal and vertical components using sine/cosine.
- [ ] Calculate time of flight, maximum height, and range for both level and elevated launches.
- [ ] Explain why horizontal velocity is constant (no air resistance) while vertical velocity changes at `g`.
- [ ] Analyze projectiles launched horizontally versus at an angle.
- [ ] Interpret a projectile's parabolic trajectory graphically.

### 3. Newton's Laws
- [ ] State all three of Newton's Laws in words and give a real example of each.
- [ ] Draw a correct free-body diagram for an object, including all forces acting on it.
- [ ] Apply `ΣF = ma` to solve for unknown forces, masses, or accelerations.
- [ ] Distinguish mass from weight and explain why weight changes with gravitational field but mass does not.
- [ ] Identify Newton's third-law force pairs correctly (same magnitude, opposite direction, acting on *different* objects).
- [ ] Differentiate static and kinetic friction, and explain why the friction force is not constant until motion begins.

### 4. Momentum
- [ ] Define momentum and impulse, and derive the impulse–momentum theorem.
- [ ] State the law of conservation of momentum and identify when it applies (isolated system, no net external force).
- [ ] Distinguish elastic, inelastic, and perfectly inelastic collisions, and know which quantities are conserved in each.
- [ ] Solve one-dimensional collision problems using conservation of momentum.
- [ ] Explain how impulse relates force and time (e.g., airbags, crumple zones).

### 5. Work & Energy
- [ ] Calculate work done by a force, including cases where force and displacement are not parallel.
- [ ] Apply the work–energy theorem to relate net work to change in kinetic energy.
- [ ] Calculate gravitational potential energy and elastic (spring) potential energy.
- [ ] Apply conservation of mechanical energy to systems without friction/air resistance, and explain where "lost" energy goes when it isn't conserved.
- [ ] Distinguish work, energy, and power, and calculate power from work or energy over time.
- [ ] Solve problems that combine energy methods with real systems (ramps, pendulums, springs).

## Assessment criteria (rubric)

Use this 4-level rubric for any written assessment, lab report, or open-response quiz question across all five topics.

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| **4 — Exemplary** | Correct answer with correct units and sig figs; work is fully shown; reasoning is explained in words, not just numbers; correctly identifies which principle/equation applies and why. |
| **3 — Proficient** | Correct final answer with correct units; work shown but reasoning not fully explained, or one minor arithmetic/unit slip that doesn't change the approach. |
| **2 — Developing** | Correct approach/equation selected, but execution error leads to wrong answer (sign error, wrong component, algebra mistake). |
| **1 — Beginning** | Incorrect approach — wrong principle or equation applied, or a fundamental misconception is present (see "commonly missed" lists in each topic's quiz). |

### Suggested grade weighting per topic
- Cornell notes completion / participation — 10%
- Practice problem set — 25%
- Quiz (`quiz.html`) — 25%
- Unit assessment (teacher-written, using rubric above) — 40%

## Cross-topic capstone check (end of unit)

Once all five topics are checked off, a strong final check is a single
multi-step problem that requires switching frameworks mid-problem, e.g.:

> *A ball is launched off a cliff (projectile motion) and its landing speed
> and angle are used to find the impulse delivered to the sand it lands in
> (momentum), and finally to verify the answer using energy conservation
> (work & energy).*

If a student can move fluidly between kinematics, forces, momentum, and
energy on the same problem, that's the clearest sign of mastery this
project is built to produce.
