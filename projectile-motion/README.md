# Unit 2: Projectile Motion

The study of objects launched into the air and moving under gravity alone —
applying kinematics (Unit 1) independently to two perpendicular directions
at once.

## Key concepts students need to master

- **Independence of horizontal and vertical motion** — the horizontal and
  vertical components of a projectile's motion don't affect each other.
  Gravity only acts vertically, so it never changes the horizontal
  velocity, and horizontal motion never influences how fast the object
  falls.
- **Horizontal motion is constant velocity** — with air resistance ignored,
  there is no horizontal acceleration, so vₓ stays the same from launch to
  landing (Δx = vₓt).
- **Vertical motion is free fall** — a = −g (≈ 9.8 m/s² downward) the
  entire flight, including at the peak. All four kinematic equations from
  Unit 1 apply to the vertical component alone.
- **Resolving the initial velocity into components** — v₀ₓ = v₀cosθ,
  v₀y = v₀sinθ, where θ is the launch angle above the horizontal. Every
  projectile problem starts here if velocity is given as a speed + angle.
- **Time of flight, max height, and range (level ground)** — for a
  projectile launched and landing at the same height:
  - t = 2v₀y/g (total time of flight)
  - h_max = v₀y²/(2g) (maximum height)
  - R = v₀ₓ·t = v₀²sin(2θ)/g (range)
- **Projectiles launched from a height** — off a cliff, table, or platform,
  the launch and landing heights differ, so the symmetric equations above
  don't apply. Instead, use Δy = v₀yt + ½at² (with Δy = −height, up
  positive) to solve for time, then vₓ·t for horizontal distance.
- **The trajectory is a parabola** — a direct result of constant horizontal
  velocity plus constant vertical acceleration; plotting x vs. y traces a
  parabolic arc.
- **Range is maximized at 45°** — for equal launch/landing height and no
  air resistance, sin(2θ) is maximized (= 1) when θ = 45°. Complementary
  angles (e.g., 30° and 60°) give equal ranges but different heights and
  flight times.
- **Symmetry of the parabola** — for level-ground launches, the trajectory
  is symmetric about the peak: time up equals time down, and the impact
  speed equals the launch speed.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

1. Decompose an initial velocity vector into horizontal and vertical
   components using v₀cosθ and v₀sinθ.
2. Apply kinematics equations separately to the horizontal axis (constant
   velocity) and vertical axis (constant acceleration = −g).
3. Solve for time of flight, maximum height, and range for projectiles
   launched and landing at the same height.
4. Solve projectile problems where launch and landing heights differ (e.g.,
   an object thrown horizontally off a cliff or launched from a table).
5. Explain why horizontal velocity stays constant through the flight while
   vertical velocity continuously changes.
6. Interpret and sketch a projectile's parabolic trajectory, including
   labeling the peak, range, and launch/landing points.

## Assessment criteria

- **Quiz target:** ≥ 80% on `quiz.html`, with correct reasoning on the
  "velocity at the peak" and "cliff-launch time of flight" questions
  specifically (these are the most commonly missed).
- **Problem sets:** Full given/find/equation/solve/units setup shown for
  each axis separately, not just a final number.
- **Mastery check:** Given a word problem (level ground or launched from a
  height, angle or horizontal launch), the student correctly identifies
  v₀ₓ and v₀y, chooses the right equation for each axis, and states
  whether the symmetric range/height/time-of-flight shortcuts apply before
  using them.
