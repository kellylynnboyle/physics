# Cornell Notes: Projectile Motion

**Topic/Date:** _____________

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| What are the two independent parts of projectile motion? | Horizontal motion (constant velocity) and vertical motion (free fall under gravity). They occur at the same time but do not affect each other; both share the same elapsed time, t. |
| Why does horizontal velocity stay constant during flight? | With no air resistance, there is no horizontal force acting on the projectile, so by Newton's first law vx never changes: vx(t) = v0x. |
| How do you find the initial horizontal and vertical velocity components? | v0x = v0 cos(θ), v0y = v0 sin(θ), where v0 is the initial speed and θ is the launch angle above the horizontal. |
| What equation gives vertical velocity at any time t? | vy(t) = v0y − g t, where g ≈ 9.8 m/s² is the acceleration due to gravity (downward). |
| What equations give horizontal and vertical position at time t? | x(t) = x0 + v0x t (horizontal); y(t) = y0 + v0y t − ½ g t² (vertical). |
| Why is the trajectory shaped like a parabola? | x grows linearly with t while y grows with t and t² together; substituting t = x/v0x into y(t) produces a quadratic relationship between y and x, i.e., a parabola. |
| What is true at the exact peak of the trajectory? | Vertical velocity vy = 0 for only an instant; the projectile is still moving horizontally at v0x. |
| What are the range and time-of-flight formulas, and when do they apply? | R = (v0² sin 2θ)/g and T = (2 v0 sin θ)/g — valid ONLY when launch height equals landing height (level ground). |
| What is special about a symmetric trajectory (equal launch/landing height)? | Time to rise to the peak equals time to fall back down; speed at a given height on the way up equals speed at that height on the way down. |
| Why do a dropped object and a horizontally launched object from the same height land at the same time? | Both start with the same y0 and the same v0y = 0 (a horizontal launch has zero initial vertical velocity), and both experience the same g. Since horizontal motion doesn't affect vertical motion, their vertical motions — and therefore fall times — are identical. |
| What must you do differently when launch height ≠ landing height? | You cannot use the simple T and R formulas above. Instead, set y(t) equal to the landing height in the full equation y(t) = y0 + v0y t − ½ g t² and solve the resulting quadratic for t. |

## Summary

Projectile motion is best understood by splitting a single curved path into two independent straight-line problems happening simultaneously: constant-velocity motion horizontally and constant-acceleration (free-fall) motion vertically. The launch angle θ determines how the initial speed v0 is divided between these two directions using v0x = v0 cos θ and v0y = v0 sin θ, and from there the standard position and velocity equations describe the entire flight. This independence explains several results that feel surprising at first, such as a dropped object and a horizontally launched object from the same height landing at the same time, and it also warns us that convenient shortcuts like the 45°-maximum-range rule and the symmetric time-of-flight formula only work when the launch and landing heights are equal. Mastering this topic means being comfortable resolving vectors into components, tracking a single shared time variable across two directions, and recognizing when a problem requires the full quadratic approach instead of a level-ground shortcut.
