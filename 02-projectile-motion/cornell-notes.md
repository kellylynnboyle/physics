# Cornell Notes — Projectile Motion

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is a projectile? | An object launched into the air that moves only under the influence of gravity after launch (no thrust, air resistance ignored). Its path is a parabola. |
| Why are horizontal and vertical motion independent? | Gravity acts only vertically, so it never changes the horizontal velocity. There is no horizontal force (no air resistance), so horizontal motion is unaffected by whatever is happening vertically. The two motions share only one thing: **time** — they happen simultaneously and take the same total time. |
| Classic demonstration of independence? | A ball dropped from a height and a ball fired horizontally from the same height at the same instant hit the ground at the **same time**, even though the fired ball travels much farther horizontally. Their vertical motions are identical because both start with vy = 0 and accelerate at g. |
| How do you find the velocity components at launch? | For launch speed v₀ at angle θ above horizontal: v₀x = v₀ cos θ, v₀y = v₀ sin θ. These come from treating v₀ as the hypotenuse of a right triangle, θ as the angle from the horizontal leg. |
| What stays constant during flight? | Horizontal velocity, vₓ = v₀x, is constant the entire flight (no horizontal acceleration). This is true at launch, at the peak, and at landing — it never becomes zero unless θ = 90°. |
| What changes during flight? | Vertical velocity, vy, changes continuously due to gravity: vy = v0y − gt (taking up as positive). It decreases on the way up, equals zero at the peak, and becomes increasingly negative (downward) on the way down. |
| What is the acceleration at every point of the flight, including the peak? | a = g = 9.8 m/s², directed straight down, at **every instant** — including exactly at the peak. Velocity being zero (vertically) at the peak does NOT mean acceleration is zero; the object is still speeding up in the downward direction at that instant. Common misconception: acceleration is NOT zero at the top. |
| Time of flight — symmetric case (launch height = landing height)? | t = 2v₀y / g = (2 v₀ sin θ) / g. This works only when the projectile lands at the same height it launched from, because the trip up and the trip down take equal time by symmetry. |
| Time of flight — general case (launch height ≠ landing height)? | Solve the full vertical displacement equation for t: Δy = v₀y t − ½ g t², where Δy = y_final − y_initial (negative if landing below launch). This is a quadratic in t — use the quadratic formula and keep only the positive root. Needed whenever launching from a cliff, roof, or table, or landing on a hill. |
| Maximum height formula? | h_max = v₀y² / (2g) = (v₀ sin θ)² / (2g), measured above the launch point. Derived by setting vy = 0 in v² = v₀y² − 2gΔy and solving for Δy. If launched from height h₀, the height above the ground is h₀ + h_max. |
| Range formula (and its one big assumption)? | R = vₓ · t. On level ground (launch height = landing height) this simplifies to the shortcut R = (v₀² sin 2θ) / g. **Assumption: this shortcut is valid only when launch and landing heights are equal.** For a projectile launched from a cliff or landing at a different elevation, you must instead compute t from the general vertical equation first, then multiply by vₓ — the shortcut formula will give a wrong answer. |
| Velocity (magnitude & direction) at any point in the trajectory? | vₓ is always v₀x (constant). vy = v0y − gt at time t. Speed = √(vₓ² + vy²). Direction = arctan(vy / vₓ) below/above horizontal. At landing (same height as launch), speed equals launch speed and the landing angle below horizontal equals the launch angle above horizontal (mirror symmetry). |
| Velocity at the peak specifically? | vy = 0 at the peak (by definition — that's what "peak" means), but vₓ is unchanged and nonzero. So total speed at the peak = |vₓ| = v₀ cos θ, **not zero**. Only a purely vertical launch (θ = 90°) has zero speed at the peak. |
| Special case: horizontal launch off a height (e.g., ball rolling off a table)? | Here θ = 0°, so v0y = 0 and v₀x = v₀ (the entire initial velocity is horizontal). Vertical motion becomes a pure "drop": Δy = −½ g t² (taking down as negative, launch as origin), giving t = √(2h / g) where h is the height of the table. Horizontal range is simply R = v₀ · t. Note vertical motion is identical to an object simply dropped from that height — horizontal launch speed has zero effect on fall time. |
| How does doubling launch speed affect range? | Range ∝ v₀² (from R = v₀² sin 2θ / g), so doubling v₀ **quadruples** the range (for the same angle and equal launch/landing height) — not doubles it. |
| What do complementary angles do? | Two launch angles that sum to 90° (e.g., 30° & 60°, 20° & 70°) produce the **same range** on level ground, because sin 2θ = sin(2(90° − θ)). However, they do **not** give the same max height or time of flight — the steeper angle (e.g., 60°) gives a greater height and longer flight time than the shallower complementary angle (30°). |
| What angle maximizes range on level ground? | 45°, because sin 2θ is maximized (sin 90° = 1) when θ = 45°. This is true only for equal launch/landing heights; the optimal angle is less than 45° when launching from a height and landing lower. |

## Summary

Projectile motion is solved by splitting one 2-D problem into two independent 1-D problems that share a common time variable: horizontal motion at constant velocity, and vertical motion at constant acceleration g downward. Initial velocity is decomposed into components v₀x = v₀ cos θ and v₀y = v₀ sin θ, and these components — not the original speed and angle — are what get plugged into the familiar kinematics equations. The symmetric shortcut formulas for time of flight, max height, and range are convenient but only valid when launch height equals landing height; any other scenario (cliffs, tables, hills) requires solving the general quadratic vertical displacement equation for time first. A critical, frequently-missed idea is that acceleration remains g downward at every instant of the flight, including the peak, even though vertical velocity is momentarily zero there — and that horizontal velocity never vanishes, so the object's speed at the peak equals its horizontal velocity component, not zero. Finally, range scales with v₀² and with sin 2θ, meaning doubling speed quadruples range, complementary angles share range but not height or flight time, and 45° maximizes range only on level ground.
