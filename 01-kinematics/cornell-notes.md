# Cornell Notes: Kinematics

**Topic/Date:** _____________

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is "position," and why does it need a reference point? | Position is the location of an object relative to a chosen origin (zero point) and a chosen positive direction. The same motion can have completely different position values depending on where you place the origin, so position is meaningless without stating the reference point. |
| What's the difference between distance and displacement? | Displacement is the vector change in position (x − x₀); it can be positive, negative, or zero, and depends only on start and end points. Distance is the scalar total length of the path actually traveled; it is always positive and never decreases. They are equal only for straight-line motion with no direction reversal. |
| How do average velocity and instantaneous velocity differ? | Average velocity = total displacement ÷ total time over an interval (a single number for the whole trip). Instantaneous velocity is the velocity at one specific moment, found as the slope of the position-time graph at that exact instant (or the limit of average velocity as the time interval shrinks to zero). |
| How do speed and velocity differ? | Speed is a scalar — just magnitude, always zero or positive. Velocity is a vector — magnitude and direction, so it can be negative depending on sign convention. Average speed can differ from the magnitude of average velocity whenever the path isn't a straight line in one direction. |
| What is acceleration? | Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity with respect to time: a = Δv/Δt. It is nonzero whenever velocity changes in magnitude, direction, or both — even if speed itself stays constant (e.g., turning at constant speed, in 2D) or momentarily reads zero (e.g., ball at the top of its arc). |
| Why do sign conventions matter? | Before solving any kinematics problem, you must pick a positive direction (e.g., "up is positive" or "right is positive"). Once chosen, every position, velocity, and acceleration value in that problem must be consistent with it — mixing conventions mid-problem causes sign errors and wrong answers. |
| What does the slope of a position-time graph tell you? What about a velocity-time graph? | On a position-time graph, slope = velocity (steeper = faster; flat = at rest; negative slope = moving in the negative direction). On a velocity-time graph, slope = acceleration, and the area between the curve and the time axis = displacement over that interval. |
| What are the four constant-acceleration kinematic equations? | (1) v = v₀ + a t. (2) x = x₀ + v₀ t + ½ a t². (3) v² = v₀² + 2 a (x − x₀). (4) x = x₀ + ((v₀ + v)/2) t. Each connects a different combination of x, v, a, and t — pick the one that already contains your known quantities and the one unknown you need. |
| What is free fall, and what value/sign does its acceleration have? | Free fall is motion under gravity alone (ignoring air resistance). Its acceleration has constant magnitude g ≈ 9.8 m/s², directed downward. If up is chosen as positive, a = −g for the entire flight — during the rise, at the peak, and during the fall. |
| Does zero velocity mean zero acceleration? | No. A ball thrown straight up has velocity = 0 at the exact top of its path, but its acceleration is still −g at that same instant (and throughout the whole flight) because gravity never stops acting. Zero velocity only means the object is momentarily at rest, not that its motion is unchanging. |
| Do heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones? | No, not in true free fall. Ignoring air resistance, all objects accelerate downward at the same rate g ≈ 9.8 m/s² regardless of mass. Everyday cases where heavy objects "fall faster" (a feather vs. a rock) are due to air resistance, not gravity treating masses differently. |

## Summary

Kinematics gives us a precise vocabulary — position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration — for describing motion along a line, and the key to using that vocabulary correctly is always fixing a sign convention before doing any calculation. Graphs are a second language for the same ideas: slope on a position-time graph is velocity, while on a velocity-time graph slope is acceleration and area is displacement. The four constant-acceleration equations let us move between position, velocity, acceleration, and time algebraically whenever acceleration doesn't change, and free fall is simply the special case where that constant acceleration is gravity, a = −g. Most of the mistakes students make in this unit come from mixing up scalar and vector quantities (distance vs. displacement, speed vs. velocity) or assuming that a zero value (velocity at the peak of a throw) means nothing else is happening (acceleration is still present). Keeping the vector/scalar distinction and the graph relationships straight is the single best way to avoid nearly every common kinematics misconception.
