# Cornell Notes: Kinematics

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is **position**? | The location of an object relative to a chosen reference point (origin), given a sign to show which side of the origin it's on. Symbol: x (or y for vertical motion). Units: meters (m). |
| **Distance** vs. **displacement** — how are they different? | Distance = total length of the path traveled; a scalar; always positive; can only increase or stay the same. Displacement (Δx) = final position − initial position; a vector; can be positive, negative, or zero even if the object moved a long path (e.g., walking 5 m east then 5 m west gives distance = 10 m but displacement = 0 m). |
| **Speed** vs. **velocity** — what's the difference? | Speed is a scalar: how fast, no direction (average speed = total distance / total time). Velocity is a vector: how fast AND which direction (average velocity = displacement / time). Because displacement can be less than distance, average velocity's magnitude can be less than average speed. |
| What is **instantaneous velocity**? | The velocity of an object at one exact moment in time. On a position-time graph, it equals the **slope of the tangent line** at that point (not the slope between two far-apart points, which gives average velocity instead). |
| What is **acceleration**? | The rate of change of velocity over time: a = Δv / Δt = (v − v₀) / t. Units: m/s². A vector — can be positive, negative, or zero. Important: an object can have a = 0 velocity at an instant but still be accelerating (e.g., a ball thrown straight up has v = 0 at the top of its path, but a = −9.8 m/s² the whole time). |
| Does negative acceleration always mean "slowing down"? | No. Negative acceleration means the acceleration vector points in the negative direction. Whether the object speeds up or slows down depends on whether velocity and acceleration point the same way or opposite ways: same sign → speeding up; opposite signs → slowing down. |
| List the **4 kinematic equations** (constant a). What does each solve for without needing?| 1) v = v₀ + at — no Δx. 2) Δx = v₀t + ½at² — no v (final velocity). 3) v² = v₀² + 2aΔx — no t. 4) Δx = ½(v₀ + v)t — no a. Variables: v₀ = initial velocity, v = final velocity, a = constant acceleration, t = time, Δx = displacement. |
| How do I know which kinematic equation to use? | List the known and unknown variables (from v₀, v, a, t, Δx). Pick the equation that contains all the knowns plus exactly the one unknown you need — i.e., the equation missing the variable you don't have and don't need. |
| What is **free fall**? What is g? | Motion under gravity alone (no air resistance). Near Earth's surface, g = 9.8 m/s², directed downward. All objects fall with the same acceleration regardless of mass (a heavy object and a light object dropped together, in a vacuum, hit the ground at the same time). Sign of g depends on chosen positive direction — often a = −9.8 m/s² if "up" is positive. |
| How do I read a **position-time graph**? | Slope of the line = velocity. Steep slope = fast; flat/horizontal = at rest; negative slope = moving in the negative direction; curved line = changing velocity (acceleration present). |
| How do I read a **velocity-time graph**? | Slope of the line = acceleration. Area between the line and the time axis = displacement (add areas above the axis, subtract areas below, since velocity below zero means displacement in the negative direction). |
| Why do sign conventions matter? | Before solving a problem, you must choose one positive direction (e.g., up = +, down = −, or right = +, left = −) and stay consistent for position, velocity, AND acceleration throughout the entire problem. Switching conventions mid-problem is one of the most common sources of error. |

## Summary

Kinematics gives us the tools to describe motion mathematically without needing to know what caused it. The key is keeping scalars (distance, speed) separate from vectors (displacement, velocity, acceleration), since direction changes everything — a round trip can have real distance traveled but zero net displacement. Acceleration is especially tricky: it's about how velocity is changing, so an object can be instantaneously at rest while still accelerating, and a negative acceleration only means "slowing down" when it's fighting against a positive velocity. Graphs are just another way to see these same ideas — slope tells you the next quantity up the chain (position's slope is velocity, velocity's slope is acceleration), while area under a velocity-time graph tells you the quantity below (displacement). The four kinematic equations are really just algebraic shortcuts for constant-acceleration situations, and choosing the right one is simply a matter of matching your knowns and unknowns. Free fall is a special, very common case of constant acceleration where g = 9.8 m/s² applies to every object equally, mass included. Underneath all of it, a single consistent sign convention is what keeps the math honest from the first line of a solution to the last.
