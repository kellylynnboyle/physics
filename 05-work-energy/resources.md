## Practice Problems

1. **Work with a horizontal force.** A worker pushes a 20 kg box 5 m across a floor with a constant horizontal force of 40 N. How much work does the worker do on the box?
   **Answer:** W = Fd cos θ = (40 N)(5 m) cos 0° = **200 J**.

2. **Work with an angled force.** A child pulls a 15 kg sled across level snow using a rope angled 30° above the horizontal, with a tension of 50 N, moving the sled 10 m. How much work does the tension force do?
   **Answer:** W = Fd cos θ = (50 N)(10 m) cos 30° = (500)(0.866) ≈ **433 J**.

3. **Kinetic energy.** A 1200 kg car is traveling at 20 m/s. (a) Find its kinetic energy. (b) If its speed doubles to 40 m/s, find its new kinetic energy and compare.
   **Answer:** (a) KE = ½mv² = ½(1200)(20²) = ½(1200)(400) = **240,000 J (240 kJ)**. (b) KE = ½(1200)(40²) = ½(1200)(1600) = **960,000 J (960 kJ)** — four times as much, confirming KE ∝ v².

4. **Gravitational potential energy.** A 0.5 kg book sits on a shelf 2 m above the floor. (a) Find its PE relative to the floor. (b) Find its PE relative to a tabletop 0.8 m above the floor. (Use g = 9.8 m/s².)
   **Answer:** (a) PE = mgh = (0.5)(9.8)(2) = **9.8 J**. (b) height above tabletop = 2 − 0.8 = 1.2 m, so PE = (0.5)(9.8)(1.2) = **5.88 J**. Both are valid — the reference point is a choice; what matters physically is the *change* in PE as the book moves.

5. **Work-energy theorem.** A 3 kg object is initially moving at 4 m/s. A net force does 60 J of work on it as it continues in the same direction. Find its final speed.
   **Answer:** W_net = ΔKE = KE_f − KE_i. KE_i = ½(3)(4²) = 24 J. KE_f = 24 + 60 = 84 J. 84 = ½(3)v_f² → v_f² = 56 → v_f ≈ **7.5 m/s**.

6. **Conservation of energy (no friction).** A 2 kg ball is dropped from rest from a height of 10 m. Ignoring air resistance, find its speed just before it hits the ground.
   **Answer:** PE_i = KE_f (taking the ground as h = 0, so PE_f = 0 and KE_i = 0). mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 10) = √196 = **14 m/s**.

7. **Conservation of energy (with friction).** A 5 kg block starts at rest at the top of a 3 m high ramp and slides to the bottom, arriving with a speed of 6 m/s. How much mechanical energy was lost to friction (converted to heat)?
   **Answer:** PE_i = mgh = (5)(9.8)(3) = 147 J. KE_f = ½(5)(6²) = 90 J. Energy lost = PE_i − KE_f = 147 − 90 = **57 J converted to heat** (mechanical energy is not conserved, but total energy still is).

8. **Power.** A 60 kg student runs up a flight of stairs, gaining 4 m in height, in 5 seconds. Find (a) the work done against gravity and (b) the average power output. (Use g = 9.8 m/s².)
   **Answer:** (a) W = mgh = (60)(9.8)(4) = **2352 J**. (b) P = W/t = 2352 / 5 = **≈ 470 W**.

## Real-World Applications

- **Roller coasters and energy conservation.** Roller coasters are a textbook example of mechanical energy trading between potential and kinetic forms: the first big hill converts the chain lift's work into gravitational PE, which converts into KE (speed) as the cars descend. Real coasters lose some mechanical energy to friction and air resistance at each hill, which is why later hills are always shorter than the first.
- **Hydroelectric dams.** Water held behind a dam has gravitational potential energy due to its height above the turbines below. As it falls, that PE converts to kinetic energy, which spins turbines to generate electricity — a large-scale, continuous application of PE-to-KE-to-electrical-energy conversion.
- **Braking systems and heat.** When a car brakes, friction between the brake pads and rotors does negative work on the car, removing its kinetic energy. That energy doesn't disappear — it converts into thermal energy, which is why brakes get noticeably hot after hard or repeated braking.
- **Pole vaulting and elastic potential energy.** A pole vaulter converts kinetic energy (running speed) into elastic potential energy by bending the pole, which then converts that stored energy back into gravitational potential energy as it straightens and launches the athlete upward — an efficient three-way energy conversion chain.
- **Human metabolism and power output.** The human body converts chemical energy from food into mechanical work and heat. During exercise, a person's power output (work done per second) can be measured in watts, similar to a machine; a trained cyclist might sustain several hundred watts, illustrating that power depends on how fast, not just how much, energy is converted.
