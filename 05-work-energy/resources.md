## Practice problems

1. A 50 N force is applied to push a box 6 m across a floor, at an angle of 30° above horizontal. Find the work done by the applied force.
2. A person holds a 20 kg box stationary at shoulder height for 30 seconds. How much work is done on the box? Explain your answer.
3. A 1200 kg car speeds up from 10 m/s to 25 m/s. Find the net work done on the car using the work-energy theorem.
4. A 3 kg ball is lifted 4 m at constant velocity. Find the work done against gravity and the resulting change in gravitational PE. Are these the same number? Why?
5. A 60 kg skier starts from rest at the top of a frictionless 40 m hill. Find their speed at the bottom using conservation of mechanical energy.
6. Repeat problem 5, but this time 5000 J is lost to friction on the way down. Find the new final speed.
7. A motor lifts a 500 N crate 10 m in 8 seconds. Find the work done and the power output of the motor. If a second motor did the same job in 4 seconds, compare the two motors' power.
8. **Conceptual:** A satellite orbits Earth in a circle at constant speed. Is gravity doing work on the satellite? Explain using the angle between force and displacement.
9. **Conceptual:** Explain why "power" and "energy" are not interchangeable, using the example of a weaker motor and a stronger motor lifting the same weight to the same height.

## Real-world applications

- **Roller coaster design** — engineers use conservation of mechanical energy (plus friction/air-resistance losses) to determine hill heights needed to reach target speeds at each point on the track.
- **Hybrid and electric vehicle regenerative braking** — converts a car's kinetic energy back into stored (electrical/chemical) energy instead of losing it all to friction heat, directly using the work-energy theorem.
- **Hydroelectric dams** — convert gravitational potential energy of stored water into kinetic energy (moving water), then into electrical energy via turbines.
- **Weightlifting and biomechanics** — "work" in the physics sense (force × distance an object moves) is used to calculate the energy cost of a lift, distinct from the physiological effort of just holding a weight still.
- **Engine/motor power ratings** — horsepower and wattage ratings on engines and appliances are literally the "rate of doing work" (power), which is why a low-power motor can still do the same total work as a high-power one — just more slowly.

## Suggested student task

Pick a moving system from daily life (a bicycle going downhill, a
pendulum, a thrown ball) and describe the energy transformations it goes
through (e.g., PE → KE, or KE → heat via friction) in 2–3 sentences,
noting anywhere energy "leaves" the mechanical system.
