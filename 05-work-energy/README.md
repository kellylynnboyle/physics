# Work and Energy

## Overview

Work and energy give us a second, often faster, way to analyze motion without tracking forces at every instant. Instead of asking "what is the acceleration right now?", we ask "how much energy was transferred into or out of the system?" This unit builds the definition of mechanical work, connects it to kinetic and potential energy through the work-energy theorem, and shows how energy is conserved even when friction seems to make it disappear. By the end, you should be able to move fluidly between force-based and energy-based problem solving, choosing whichever is simpler for a given situation.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Work (W):** the energy transferred to or from an object by a force acting through a displacement; only the component of force parallel to the displacement contributes.
- **Kinetic energy (KE):** the energy an object has because of its motion, depending on mass and the square of its speed.
- **Gravitational potential energy (PE):** stored energy due to an object's height above a chosen reference level.
- **Work-energy theorem:** the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy.
- **Conservation of mechanical energy:** in the absence of friction or air resistance, the sum of kinetic and potential energy stays constant as an object moves.
- **Conservation of total energy:** energy is never created or destroyed; friction converts mechanical energy into thermal energy (heat) and sound rather than eliminating it.
- **Power (P):** the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred, equal to work divided by time (or force times velocity for a constant force).

## Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

1. Calculate the work done by a constant force using W = F d cos(θ), including cases where the force is at an angle to the displacement.
2. Explain why a force perpendicular to displacement, or an object with zero displacement, results in zero work.
3. Calculate the kinetic energy of a moving object and explain why KE scales with the square of velocity.
4. Calculate gravitational potential energy relative to a stated reference height.
5. Apply the work-energy theorem to relate net work to a change in kinetic energy.
6. Apply conservation of mechanical energy to solve problems involving height and speed changes when friction is negligible.
7. Explain how total energy remains conserved even when mechanical energy decreases due to friction or air resistance.
8. Calculate power delivered by a force and distinguish power from work and energy.

## Key Equations

| Equation | Variables | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| W = F d cos(θ) | W = work (J); F = force magnitude (N); d = displacement magnitude (m); θ = angle between force and displacement | Finding the work done by a single constant force, especially when the force is not aligned with the motion |
| KE = (1/2) m v² | KE = kinetic energy (J); m = mass (kg); v = speed (m/s) | Finding the energy of motion of an object at a given speed |
| PE_grav = m g h | PE = gravitational potential energy (J); m = mass (kg); g = acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s² near Earth's surface); h = height above a chosen reference level (m) | Finding stored energy due to an object's position in a gravitational field |
| W_net = ΔKE | W_net = total (net) work done on an object (J); ΔKE = change in kinetic energy, KE_final − KE_initial (J) | Relating the combined effect of all forces acting on an object to how its speed changes |
| KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f | KE_i, PE_i = kinetic and potential energy at an initial point; KE_f, PE_f = kinetic and potential energy at a final point | Solving for an unknown speed or height when friction and air resistance are negligible (mechanical energy is conserved) |
| P = W / t = F v | P = power (W, watts); W = work done (J); t = time interval (s); F = force magnitude (N); v = speed (m/s) | Finding the rate of energy transfer, or comparing how quickly different agents (engines, people, machines) do the same amount of work |

## Common Misconceptions

1. **Misconception:** Work is done any time a force is applied or effort is felt, even without displacement, or when the force is perpendicular to the displacement.
   **Reality:** Work requires displacement in the direction of the force. Holding a heavy bag stationary does zero work because there is no displacement; carrying that same bag horizontally at constant height also does zero work by gravity, because gravity acts vertically while the motion is horizontal — the two directions are perpendicular, so cos(90°) = 0.

2. **Misconception:** Work, energy, and power are interchangeable ideas.
   **Reality:** Work is a transfer of energy via a force acting through a displacement; energy is the capacity stored in or possessed by a system; power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred. Two processes can involve the same work but very different power if they take different amounts of time.

3. **Misconception:** Negative work is impossible or represents some kind of error in the calculation.
   **Reality:** Negative work simply means the force has a component opposite to the displacement, so it removes energy from the object. Friction acting opposite to motion, or air resistance slowing a falling object, both do negative work and reduce the object's kinetic energy.

4. **Misconception:** Energy is only conserved in idealized, frictionless situations.
   **Reality:** Total energy is always conserved. Friction does not destroy energy; it converts mechanical energy (kinetic + potential) into thermal energy and sound. "Mechanical energy" appears to decrease only because we are not counting the heat generated as part of it.

5. **Misconception:** Potential energy is an absolute property of an object, independent of any reference point.
   **Reality:** Gravitational potential energy is always defined relative to a chosen reference height (h = 0). Changing that reference changes the numerical value of PE at every point, but it never changes physically meaningful quantities like the change in PE between two points.

6. **Misconception:** Doubling an object's speed doubles its kinetic energy.
   **Reality:** Because KE = (1/2) m v², doubling speed while keeping mass constant quadruples the kinetic energy, since v is squared in the formula.

7. **Misconception:** A heavier object always has more kinetic energy than a lighter one.
   **Reality:** Kinetic energy depends on both mass and the square of velocity. A small increase in speed can outweigh a large increase in mass — a much faster light object (like a thrown baseball) can have more kinetic energy than a slow heavy object (like a rolling boulder), depending on the specific values.

## Practice Problems

1. A person pushes a box with a horizontal force of 50 N, moving it 4 m across a floor in the direction of the push. How much work is done on the box?
2. A worker pulls a sled with a rope at an angle of 30° above the horizontal, applying 80 N of force while the sled moves 10 m horizontally. How much work does the worker do on the sled?
3. A 1200 kg car is traveling at 20 m/s. What is its kinetic energy?
4. A 5 kg book sits on a shelf 2 m above the floor. What is its gravitational potential energy relative to the floor? What is its potential energy relative to a tabletop 1 m above the floor?
5. A 0.5 kg ball is thrown and its speed increases from 4 m/s to 12 m/s due to a net force acting on it over a distance. Using the work-energy theorem, find the net work done on the ball.
6. A 2 kg object slides from rest down a frictionless ramp and reaches the bottom, which is 3 m lower than its starting point. Use conservation of mechanical energy to find its speed at the bottom.
7. A weightlifter raises a 40 kg barbell 2 m in 1.5 seconds, moving it at a roughly constant speed. Calculate (a) the work done against gravity and (b) the average power output.
8. A 3 kg cart moving at 6 m/s and a 12 kg cart moving at 2 m/s are compared. Which cart has more kinetic energy, and by how much? Explain why the answer might surprise someone who assumes "heavier always means more energy."

### Answer Key

1. W = F d cos(θ) = 50 N × 4 m × cos(0°) = 200 J.
2. W = F d cos(θ) = 80 N × 10 m × cos(30°) ≈ 80 × 10 × 0.866 ≈ 693 J.
3. KE = (1/2) m v² = 0.5 × 1200 × 20² = 0.5 × 1200 × 400 = 240,000 J.
4. Relative to the floor: PE = m g h = 5 × 9.8 × 2 = 98 J. Relative to the tabletop (h = 1 m, since the book is 1 m above the tabletop): PE = 5 × 9.8 × 1 = 49 J. This shows PE depends on the chosen reference height.
5. ΔKE = (1/2)(0.5)(12²) − (1/2)(0.5)(4²) = (0.5)(0.5)(144) − (0.5)(0.5)(16) = 36 − 4 = 32 J. By the work-energy theorem, W_net = ΔKE = 32 J.
6. Using KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f with KE_i = 0 and PE_f = 0 (taking the bottom as reference): m g h = (1/2) m v² → v = √(2 g h) = √(2 × 9.8 × 3) ≈ √58.8 ≈ 7.67 m/s (mass cancels out).
7. (a) W = m g h = 40 × 9.8 × 2 = 784 J. (b) P = W / t = 784 / 1.5 ≈ 523 W.
8. Cart A: KE = (1/2)(3)(6²) = 0.5 × 3 × 36 = 54 J. Cart B: KE = (1/2)(12)(2²) = 0.5 × 12 × 4 = 24 J. The lighter, faster cart (A) has more kinetic energy (54 J vs. 24 J) because kinetic energy depends on velocity squared, so speed has a much larger effect than mass on KE.

## Real-World Applications

- **Roller coasters** convert gravitational potential energy at the top of a hill into kinetic energy on the way down, and back again, illustrating conservation of mechanical energy (with some loss to friction and air resistance).
- **Hydroelectric dams** use the gravitational potential energy of water held at height to spin turbines, converting PE into kinetic energy and then into electrical energy.
- **Pendulums** continuously trade kinetic and potential energy as they swing, momentarily at rest (all PE) at the top of each swing and moving fastest (all KE) at the bottom.
- **Vehicle braking** converts a car's kinetic energy into thermal energy in the brake pads and rotors through friction, which is why brakes get hot and why stopping distance depends heavily on speed (since KE depends on v²).
- **Human-powered devices**, such as bicycles or hand-crank generators, let us calculate the power a person can sustainably output, connecting muscular work to measurable wattage.
- **Wind turbines** capture the kinetic energy of moving air and convert it into electrical energy, with power output depending on wind speed (and thus KE of the air) in a nonlinear way.
