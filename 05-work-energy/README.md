# 05 · Work & Energy

An alternative, often more powerful, lens for solving motion problems —
especially ones that would be painful with forces and kinematics alone.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Work**: W = Fd cos θ — only the force component *parallel* to displacement does work
- **Work-energy theorem**: net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, W_net = ΔKE
- **Kinetic energy**: KE = ½mv²
- **Gravitational potential energy**: PE_grav = mgh (relative to a chosen reference height)
- **Elastic potential energy**: PE_spring = ½kx² (x = displacement from equilibrium, not total spring length)
- **Conservation of mechanical energy**: KE + PE = constant, when only conservative forces (gravity, springs) act
- **Non-conservative forces** (friction, air resistance) remove mechanical energy, converting it to heat — must be accounted for as energy "lost" from the system
- **Power**: P = W/t (rate of doing work); units of Watts, distinct from Joules (energy)

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, students can:

- [ ] Calculate work done by a force, correctly applying the cos θ factor when force and displacement aren't parallel
- [ ] Apply the work-energy theorem to find a change in speed from work done
- [ ] Calculate gravitational and elastic potential energy for a given configuration
- [ ] Apply conservation of mechanical energy to solve problems with no friction (e.g., roller coasters, pendulums, falling objects)
- [ ] Correctly account for energy "lost" to friction in problems where mechanical energy is NOT conserved
- [ ] Distinguish power from work/energy and compute power in a given scenario

## Assessment Criteria

| Level | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Proficient | Applies conservation of energy fluently across gravity/spring/friction combinations; correctly reasons about when W = Fd cos θ reduces to zero or Fd |
| Developing | Solves basic conservation problems (no friction) but doesn't correctly account for energy loss when friction is present |
| Beginning | Confuses work/energy/power units, or forgets the cos θ factor for angled forces |

Suggested summative check: one frictionless energy-conservation problem, one with friction (energy loss), and one power calculation, 80% = mastery.

## Practice Problems

1. A person pushes a 20 kg box 5 m across a floor with a constant horizontal force of 40 N. How much work is done on the box?
2. A person carries a 10 kg bag horizontally at constant height for 15 m. How much work does gravity do on the bag? Explain why, even though the person is clearly exerting effort.
3. A 2 kg ball rolls off a table 1.2 m high. Use energy conservation to find its speed just before hitting the floor (ignore air resistance).
4. A spring with k = 200 N/m is compressed 0.15 m. How much elastic potential energy is stored?
5. A 60 kg skier starts from rest at the top of a 40 m frictionless hill. Find her speed at the bottom.
6. Redo problem 5, but this time friction does −8000 J of work on the way down. Find her speed at the bottom.
7. A motor lifts a 500 N crate 10 m in 20 seconds. Find the power output of the motor.
8. Explain, using the work-energy theorem, why it takes a much longer stopping distance to stop a car at 60 mph than at 30 mph (not just twice as long).

## Real-World Applications

- **Roller coasters**: converting gravitational PE at the top of a hill into KE at the bottom, and back again
- **Pendulum clocks and playground swings**: continuous KE ↔ PE exchange
- **Hydroelectric dams**: converting gravitational PE of stored water into electrical energy
- **Braking systems**: converting a car's KE into heat via friction (brake pads) — why stopping distance grows with the *square* of speed
- **Bouncing balls**: each bounce loses mechanical energy to heat and sound, which is why the bounce height decreases over time
