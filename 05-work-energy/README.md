# 05 — Work & Energy

Work and energy give you a third major problem-solving tool, often the fastest one when a problem asks about speed or height without needing time or detailed force information mid-motion. This section closes the mechanics unit by tying together everything before it: forces (Newton's Laws) doing work over a distance to change an object's energy.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Work** — $W = Fd\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the force and the displacement. Work is a scalar (can be positive, negative, or zero) — units: Joules (J).
- **Zero-work situations** — a force perpendicular to displacement does zero work (e.g., normal force on a horizontally moving object, or gravity on a horizontally moving object). Holding something stationary does zero work, no matter how tiring it feels.
- **Kinetic energy** — $KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, energy of motion, always $\geq 0$, scalar.
- **Gravitational potential energy** — $PE_g = mgh$, energy stored due to position in a gravitational field; $h$ requires a chosen reference point (only *changes* in height matter for $\Delta PE$).
- **Work-Energy Theorem** — $W_{net} = \Delta KE$. The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy — this connects forces directly to speed changes without needing time (unlike kinematics) or momentum.
- **Conservation of mechanical energy** — in the absence of non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance), $KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f$. When friction/air resistance IS present, mechanical energy is not conserved — the "lost" energy converts to thermal energy (still conserved overall, per the broader Law of Conservation of Energy).
- **Power** — $P = W/t = Fv$ (for constant force in the direction of motion), the *rate* of doing work or transferring energy. Units: Watts (W). Two objects can do the same work but at different power if the time differs.
- **Conservative vs. non-conservative forces** — gravity and spring forces are conservative (work done is path-independent, energy is fully recoverable); friction is non-conservative (work done depends on path length, energy is "lost" to heat).

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, a student should be able to:

1. Calculate work done by a force, correctly accounting for the angle between force and displacement, including recognizing when work is zero or negative.
2. Apply the work-energy theorem to find a final speed or force without using kinematics or momentum.
3. Use conservation of mechanical energy to solve problems (e.g., objects on ramps, pendulums, roller coasters) in the absence of friction.
4. Correctly modify the energy conservation equation to include energy lost to friction/air resistance when those forces are present.
5. Calculate power and distinguish it from work/energy — explain why "doing the same work faster" requires more power, not more energy.

## Practice Problems

1. A 15 kg box is pushed 4 m across a floor by a 30 N force applied horizontally. Find the work done by the applied force.
2. The same box in problem 1 experiences 10 N of kinetic friction over the same 4 m. Find the work done by friction, and the net work done on the box.
3. A 0.3 kg ball is dropped from a height of 8 m. Using energy conservation (ignore air resistance), find its speed just before hitting the ground. Verify this matches a kinematics-based answer from Section 01.
4. A 1000 kg roller coaster car starts at rest at the top of an 80 m hill (frictionless track). Find its speed at the bottom, and its speed at a point 30 m above the ground.
5. A 500 W motor lifts a 50 kg load. How long does it take to lift the load 10 m at constant speed? (Assume 100% efficiency.)
6. **Challenge:** A 2 kg block slides down a frictionless 5 m ramp from rest, then slides across a rough horizontal floor with $\mu_k = 0.25$. How far does it slide across the floor before stopping? (Combine energy conservation on the ramp with the work-energy theorem including friction on the floor.)

## Real-World Applications

- **Roller coaster design:** engineers use energy conservation (with friction/air resistance corrections) to ensure a coaster has enough energy at the top of the first hill to complete the entire track, including smaller hills and loops later on.
- **Power ratings of appliances and vehicles:** motors, engines, and electrical devices are rated in Watts specifically because power (not just total energy/work) determines performance — how *fast* a task can be done.
- **Renewable energy systems:** hydroelectric dams convert gravitational potential energy of stored water into kinetic energy (turbines) and then electrical energy — a direct, large-scale application of $PE_g = mgh$ and energy conservation.
- **Braking systems and regenerative braking:** conventional brakes convert kinetic energy to heat via friction (non-conservative force, energy "lost" to the system as useful mechanical energy); regenerative braking in electric/hybrid vehicles instead converts KE into stored electrical energy, improving efficiency.
