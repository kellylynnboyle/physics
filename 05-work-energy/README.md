# Unit 5 — Work & Energy

The connection between force applied over a distance and the energy an object has — plus the conservation law that ties every unit together.

## Key Concepts to Master

- Work: W = F d cos θ — work requires a force **and** displacement, and only the force component parallel to the displacement counts
- When work is zero: no displacement, or force perpendicular to displacement (e.g., a normal force during horizontal motion, or carrying a bag while walking horizontally)
- Kinetic energy: KE = ½mv²
- Work-energy theorem: W_net = ΔKE
- Gravitational potential energy: PE_g = mgh (relative to a chosen reference height)
- Elastic potential energy: PE_s = ½kx²
- Conservation of mechanical energy: KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f, **when no non-conservative forces (like friction) act**
- Non-conservative forces and energy transformation: mechanical energy isn't destroyed when friction acts — it converts to thermal energy (heat)
- Power: P = W/t (rate of doing work)

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

1. Calculate work done by a force, correctly accounting for the angle between force and displacement.
2. Identify situations where work done is zero despite an applied force or despite motion occurring.
3. Apply the work-energy theorem to relate net work to a change in kinetic energy.
4. Calculate gravitational and elastic potential energy, and apply conservation of mechanical energy to solve for an unknown speed or height.
5. Correctly analyze situations involving friction, explaining that energy converts to heat rather than "disappearing."
6. Calculate power and distinguish it from work and energy in both concept and units.

## Practice Problems

1. A 20 kg box is pushed 5.0 m across a floor by a 40 N horizontal force. How much work is done on the box?
2. A person carries a 10 kg bag at constant height, walking 15 m horizontally. How much work does the person do against gravity on the bag?
3. A 0.20 kg ball is dropped from a height of 8.0 m. Using conservation of energy, find its speed just before it hits the ground (ignore air resistance).
4. A roller coaster car (500 kg) starts at rest at the top of a 30 m hill. Ignoring friction, what is its speed at the bottom?
5. A motor lifts a 50 kg crate 4.0 m straight up in 5.0 s at constant velocity. Find the work done and the power output of the motor.

<details>
<summary><strong>Answer key</strong> (click to expand)</summary>

1. W = Fd cos θ = 40 × 5.0 × cos(0°) = 200 J (force and displacement are in the same direction)
2. Zero. The person's applied force on the bag is vertical (supporting its weight), but the bag's displacement is horizontal, so θ = 90° and cos(90°) = 0 — no work is done against gravity even though the person gets tired.
3. Energy conservation: mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 8.0) = √156.8 ≈ 12.5 m/s
4. mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 30) = √588 ≈ 24.2 m/s (mass cancels out — it doesn't affect the final speed)
5. W = Fd = mgh = 50 × 9.8 × 4.0 = 1960 J. P = W/t = 1960/5.0 = 392 W
</details>

## Real-World Applications

- **Roller coasters** — height converts to speed and back via conservation of mechanical energy, with friction/brakes removing energy as heat
- **Hydroelectric dams** — gravitational PE of stored water converts to kinetic energy, then electrical energy, as water falls through turbines
- **Pendulum clocks and swings** — continuous exchange between kinetic and gravitational potential energy
- **Regenerative braking in electric/hybrid cars** — converting a car's kinetic energy back into stored electrical energy instead of losing it all to heat
- **Rating light bulbs and appliances in watts** — power as the practical, everyday unit of energy use rate
