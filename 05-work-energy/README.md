# Unit 5 — Work & Energy

Work and energy give a second, often simpler, way to solve motion problems —
by tracking a scalar quantity (energy) instead of vector forces over time.
This unit ties together everything from Units 1–4 through the work-energy
theorem and conservation of energy.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Work**: `W = Fd cos θ` — force times displacement times the cosine of the angle between them. Work is a scalar and can be positive, negative, or zero.
- **Work is zero** when the force is perpendicular to displacement, or when there is no displacement at all
- **The work-energy theorem**: `W_net = ΔKE` — the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy
- **Kinetic energy**: `KE = ½mv²`
- **Gravitational potential energy**: `PE_g = mgh` (relative to a chosen reference height)
- **Elastic (spring) potential energy**: `PE_spring = ½kx²`
- **Conservative vs. non-conservative forces**: gravity and spring force are conservative (path-independent, energy is "storable" as PE); friction and air resistance are non-conservative (energy is lost as heat)
- **Conservation of mechanical energy**: `KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f`, valid ONLY when non-conservative forces do no work (i.e., no friction/air resistance)
- **When friction is present**: `KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + energy lost to friction`
- **Power**: `P = W/t` (rate of doing work), measured in watts

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students should be able to:

1. Calculate work done by a force, correctly accounting for the angle between force and displacement.
2. Apply the work-energy theorem to find a change in speed, or the work needed to produce a given speed change.
3. Calculate gravitational and elastic potential energy, and total mechanical energy.
4. Apply conservation of mechanical energy to solve motion problems on frictionless surfaces (ramps, pendulums, roller coasters).
5. Modify the energy conservation equation to account for energy lost to friction or other non-conservative forces.
6. Distinguish work, energy, and power, and calculate power for a given amount of work done over time.

## Practice Problems

1. A 10 kg box is pushed 5 m across a floor by a 30 N horizontal force. Find the work done by the applied force.
2. A person carries a 15 kg bag horizontally across a room at constant speed. How much work does the person's carrying force do on the bag vertically? Explain.
3. A 2 kg ball is dropped from 10 m. Use conservation of energy to find its speed just before hitting the ground (ignore air resistance).
4. A 0.5 kg block compresses a spring (`k = 200 N/m`) by 0.10 m. Find the elastic potential energy stored, and the block's speed when released on a frictionless surface.
5. A 1200 kg car's engine does `2.4 × 10⁵ J` of work to accelerate it in 8 s. Find the average power output in watts and horsepower.

## Real-World Applications

- **Roller coasters**: energy conversion between height (PE) and speed (KE) along the track
- **Hydroelectric power**: gravitational PE of stored water converted to electrical energy
- **Car engines and horsepower ratings**: power as the rate of doing work
- **Archery and slingshots**: elastic PE stored in a bent bow/stretched band converted to KE
- **Pendulum clocks and playground swings**: continuous KE ↔ PE exchange
