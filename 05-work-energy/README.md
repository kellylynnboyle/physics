# Work & Energy

Work and energy give physicists a second, often simpler way to analyze motion — one that sidesteps vectors and accelerations in favor of scalar quantities that are tracked before and after an event. This unit builds the definition of mechanical work, connects it to kinetic and potential energy through the work-energy theorem, and develops the conservation of mechanical energy as a problem-solving tool, including what happens when friction is present. It closes with power, the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Definition of work**: W = Fd cos θ, where θ is the angle between the applied force and the displacement; work is a scalar (measured in joules), not a vector.
- **Only the parallel component counts**: a force does work only through the component that lies along the direction of displacement — a force perpendicular to the motion (like a normal force or centripetal force) does zero work no matter how large it is.
- **Kinetic energy**: KE = ½mv², the energy of motion; because velocity is squared, doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy.
- **Gravitational potential energy**: PE = mgh; the height reference (h = 0) is arbitrary and chosen for convenience — only *changes* in PE have physical meaning.
- **Elastic potential energy**: PE = ½kx², where k is the spring constant and x is the displacement from the spring's natural (unstretched/uncompressed) length.
- **Work-energy theorem**: the *net* work done on an object (the sum of the work done by every force acting on it) equals its change in kinetic energy, W_net = ΔKE.
- **Conservation of mechanical energy**: when only conservative forces (like gravity or an ideal spring) act, total mechanical energy (KE + PE) stays constant, even as it converts between forms.
- **Non-conservative forces**: friction and air resistance remove mechanical energy from a system, converting it into thermal energy (heat) and sound; mechanical energy is *not* conserved when they act, but total energy (including heat) still is.
- **Power**: P = W/t = Fv, the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred, measured in watts; doing the same work in less time requires more power, not more work.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

1. **Define** work mathematically and **calculate** the work done by a constant force applied at an angle to displacement.
2. **Determine** whether the work done by a given force is positive, negative, or zero based on the angle between force and displacement.
3. **Calculate** the kinetic energy of a moving object and **explain** why kinetic energy scales with the square of velocity.
4. **Calculate** gravitational and elastic potential energy, and **justify** why the choice of reference height does not affect physical predictions.
5. **Apply** the work-energy theorem to relate net work to a change in an object's kinetic energy in single- and multi-force scenarios.
6. **Apply** conservation of mechanical energy to solve motion problems, and **distinguish** situations where it holds from those where friction or other non-conservative forces cause mechanical energy to decrease.
7. **Compute** the power delivered by a force or system, and **differentiate** power from work and energy in real-world contexts.
8. **Evaluate** multi-step problems (e.g., a skier or roller coaster on a track with friction) by combining work, energy, and power reasoning to find unknown quantities.

## Assessment Criteria

| Skill | Mastery Indicator |
|---|---|
| Work calculation | Correctly applies W = Fd cos θ, including cases with angled forces, and gets the sign right |
| Identifying zero/negative work | Correctly identifies when a force does zero work (perpendicular to motion, or no displacement) or negative work (opposes motion) |
| Kinetic energy | Correctly computes KE = ½mv² and reasons proportionally about how KE changes with velocity |
| Potential energy | Correctly computes PE = mgh and PE = ½kx², and explains that only ΔPE is physically meaningful |
| Work-energy theorem | Sets up W_net = ΔKE correctly, including problems with multiple forces or friction |
| Conservation of energy | Correctly applies KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f for conservative systems, and adjusts the equation to account for energy lost to friction when it is present |
| Power | Correctly computes P = W/t or P = Fv and distinguishes power from work/energy in explanations |
| Conceptual reasoning | Explains misconceptions correctly, e.g., holding a heavy object still does no work on it, even though it is tiring |

## Prerequisites

- Newton's Laws of Motion (especially the second law, F = ma, and force diagrams)
- Kinematics (displacement, velocity, acceleration, and the kinematic equations)
- Basic trigonometry (sine, cosine, and resolving vectors into components)
- Vectors (magnitude, direction, and vector components) versus scalars (like work and energy)
