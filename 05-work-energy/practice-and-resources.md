# Topic 5: Work & Energy — Practice, Resources & Applications

## Practice Problems

Use `g = 9.8 m/s²`.

1. A `10 kg` box is pushed `5 m` across a floor by a `40 N` horizontal force. Find the work done by the applied force.
2. A `70 kg` hiker carries a `15 kg` pack up a hill, gaining `120 m` of elevation. How much work is done against gravity? Does the horizontal distance traveled matter for this calculation?
3. A `2 kg` cart is pulled `8 m` by a rope at `30°` above horizontal with a tension of `25 N`, across a frictionless floor. Find the work done by the tension, and use the work–energy theorem to find the cart's final speed if it started at rest.
4. A `0.3 kg` ball is dropped from `10 m`. Using energy conservation (ignoring air resistance), find its speed just before hitting the ground. Verify it matches the kinematics answer.
5. A `1500 kg` roller coaster car starts at rest at the top of a `40 m` hill. Find its speed at the bottom, first ignoring friction, then assuming `15%` of its initial potential energy is lost to friction and air resistance.
6. A spring with `k = 200 N/m` is compressed `0.15 m` and used to launch a `0.5 kg` ball. Find the ball's launch speed (assume all spring PE converts to KE, no friction).
7. A `60 kg` person climbs a flight of stairs, rising `4 m`, in `8 seconds`. Find their average power output.
8. A crate is dragged `6 m` across a rough floor by a `50 N` force at a `20°` angle above horizontal, and friction does `-120 J` of work. Find the crate's change in kinetic energy.

### Answer Key
1. `W = Fd = 40 × 5 = 200 J` (force and displacement are parallel, `cos0°=1`)
2. `W = mgh = (85)(9.8)(120) = 99,960 J ≈ 1.0 × 10⁵ J`. No — horizontal distance is irrelevant to work done against gravity; only the VERTICAL height change matters, since gravity is vertical and only the component of displacement along the force direction counts.
3. `W = Fd cosθ = 25 × 8 × cos30° ≈ 173.2 J`. By work-energy theorem: `173.2 = ½(2)v² → v ≈ 13.2 m/s`
4. `mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh) = √(2×9.8×10) ≈ 14.0 m/s` — matches the kinematics result `v² = v₀² + 2gh` exactly, confirming the two methods agree.
5. Frictionless: `v = √(2gh) = √(2×9.8×40) ≈ 28.0 m/s`. With 15% loss: remaining KE fraction is 85% of `mgh`, so `v = √(2 × 0.85 × 9.8 × 40) ≈ 25.8 m/s`
6. `½kx² = ½mv² → v = x√(k/m) = 0.15 × √(200/0.5) = 0.15 × 20 = 3.0 m/s`
7. `W = mgh = 60 × 9.8 × 4 = 2352 J`; `P = W/t = 2352/8 = 294 W`
8. `W_applied = 50 × 6 × cos20° ≈ 281.9 J`. `ΔKE = W_net = W_applied + W_friction = 281.9 + (−120) = 161.9 J`

## Learning Resources
- **PhET Interactive Simulations** — *Energy Skate Park* — a skater on a track with adjustable friction; watch KE, PE, and thermal energy bars update in real time as the skater moves — excellent for visualizing "where energy goes."
- **The Physics Classroom** — [Work, Energy, and Power tutorial](https://www.physicsclassroom.com/class/energy) — includes the classic "Roller Coaster Physics" application pages.
- **Khan Academy** — [Work and energy](https://www.khanacademy.org/science/physics/work-and-energy) — video lessons plus practice problems on the work-energy theorem.
- **U.S. Department of Energy / EIA** — real power-plant and energy-efficiency data, useful for extending the power/efficiency discussion to real systems.

## Real-World Applications
- **Roller coasters**: designed almost entirely using energy conservation — the first hill must be the tallest point (ignoring losses) because it sets the total mechanical energy budget for the whole ride.
- **Hydroelectric dams**: convert gravitational PE of stored water directly into electrical energy via turbines — a large-scale, real application of `PE = mgh`.
- **Car engines and fuel efficiency**: power ratings (horsepower/watts) describe how fast an engine can do work, which is why a low-power engine can still eventually do the same total work as a high-power one, just more slowly.
- **Pole vaulting and archery**: both convert elastic PE (bent pole, drawn bowstring) into KE, directly analogous to the spring-launch problem above.
