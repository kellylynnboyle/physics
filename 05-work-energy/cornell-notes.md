## Cues / Questions | Notes

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is the formal definition of work? | `W = Fd cosθ`, where θ is the angle between the force vector and the displacement vector. Work is a **scalar**, but it can be positive, negative, or zero. |
| When is work exactly zero? | When force and displacement are perpendicular (θ = 90°, cosθ = 0) — e.g., normal force on a walking person, or centripetal force on an object in a circle. Also zero if displacement is zero, no matter how hard you push (e.g., pushing on a locked door, holding a heavy bag still). |
| Common myth to bust | "Holding something up takes work." Physically, holding a stationary object involves **zero work**, because displacement is zero — even though it's tiring for your muscles. |
| What does the work-energy theorem say? | `W_net = ΔKE = KE_f − KE_i`. The **net** work (from all forces combined) equals the change in kinetic energy — not the work from any single force. |
| What is kinetic energy? | `KE = ½mv²` — energy due to motion. Depends on speed squared, so doubling speed quadruples KE. |
| What is gravitational potential energy? | `PE = mgh`, measured relative to a chosen reference height (h = 0 is your choice — only *changes* in PE are physically meaningful). |
| When is mechanical energy (KE + PE) conserved? | Only when no non-conservative force (friction, air resistance, applied pushes) does work on the system. Gravity and ideal springs are conservative forces — they don't drain energy from the system. |
| What happens to energy when friction is present? | Mechanical energy (KE + PE) **decreases**, but **total** energy is still conserved — the "missing" mechanical energy converts into heat and sound. Never say energy "disappears." |
| What's the difference between work/energy and power? | Work and energy measure a total **amount** (Joules). Power measures a **rate**: `P = W/t` (Watts = Joules/second). Two motors can do the same total work, but the more powerful one does it faster. |
| How do I solve a "roller coaster" style energy problem? | Set up `KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f` (no friction) or `KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + W_friction` (with friction), choosing one consistent reference height for PE, then solve for the unknown speed or height. |

## Worked mini-example (conservation of mechanical energy)

A 2 kg cart starts at rest at the top of a frictionless 5 m tall hill.

1. At the top: `KE_i = 0`, `PE_i = mgh = 2(9.8)(5) = 98 J`
2. Total mechanical energy = 98 J (constant, no friction)
3. At the bottom (h = 0): `PE_f = 0`, so all 98 J is now kinetic: `½mv² = 98 → v = √(98/1) = 9.9 m/s`

If a 20 J energy loss to friction were introduced, step 3 would instead
read `KE_f = 98 − 20 = 78 J`, giving a smaller final speed — the "lost"
energy became heat, not nothing.

## Summary

Work connects force to a change in energy, but only the component of
force *along* the displacement counts — many "work is being done" gut
feelings (holding something, pushing against an immovable wall) are wrong
under the strict physics definition. The work-energy theorem and
conservation of mechanical energy are two views of the same idea:
energy shifts between kinetic and potential forms, and stays constant in
total unless a non-conservative force like friction drains some of it away
as heat. Power adds a time dimension on top of all of this — never confuse
"how much work total" with "how fast it's being done."
