## Cue Column | Notes

| Cue Column (questions/keywords) | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is **work** (physics definition)? | W = Fd cos θ, where θ is the angle between the force and the displacement direction. Work is a scalar (can be positive, negative, or zero) — units are Joules (J). |
| When is work **zero**, even if a force is applied? | When the force is perpendicular to the displacement (θ = 90°, cos 90° = 0) — e.g., normal force on a person walking horizontally, or tension in circular motion. Also zero if there's no displacement at all (holding a heavy box still does zero work on it, physics-wise). |
| When is work **negative**? | When the force has a component OPPOSITE to the displacement (θ between 90° and 180°) — e.g., friction opposing motion, or air resistance. Negative work removes kinetic energy from an object. |
| What is **kinetic energy (KE)**? | KE = ½mv². Always positive (or zero), scalar, depends on speed squared — doubling speed quadruples KE. |
| What is the **work-energy theorem**? | W_net = ΔKE. The NET work done on an object (by all forces combined) equals its change in kinetic energy. This lets you skip force/acceleration entirely and jump straight from work to speed change. |
| What is **gravitational potential energy (PE_grav)**? | PE_grav = mgh, measured relative to a chosen reference height (h = 0 is your choice — only CHANGES in PE matter physically). |
| What is **elastic potential energy**? | PE_elastic = ½kx², stored in a stretched/compressed spring (k = spring constant, x = displacement from natural length). |
| What is **conservation of mechanical energy**? | In a system with NO friction/air resistance (only "conservative" forces like gravity and springs acting), total mechanical energy is constant: KE₁ + PE₁ = KE₂ + PE₂. Energy converts between kinetic and potential but the total stays fixed. |
| What happens to mechanical energy when **friction** is present? | Mechanical energy is NOT conserved — some energy converts to heat (thermal energy). Use: KE₁ + PE₁ = KE₂ + PE₂ + E_lost_to_friction, where E_lost = f · d (friction force times distance over which it acts). |
| What is **power**? | P = W/t (rate of doing work) or equivalently P = Fv (force times velocity) for constant force. Units: Watts (W = J/s). Power tells you how FAST energy is transferred, not how much total energy. |
| Common trap: does a **normal force or tension** ever do work? | Only if it has a component along the direction of motion. Normal force on a flat surface = zero work (perpendicular to motion). Tension in a pendulum = zero work (always perpendicular to the circular path). But tension pulling something up a ramp WOULD do work, since it's not perpendicular to displacement. |
| Common trap: "energy is used up" — is that accurate? | No — energy is never destroyed, only converted between forms (kinetic ↔ potential ↔ thermal ↔ etc.), per conservation of energy. "Lost to friction" really means "converted to heat," not destroyed. |

---

## Summary

Work (W = Fd cos θ) is the bridge between force and energy — it can be positive, negative, or zero depending on the angle between force and motion, and the work-energy theorem (W_net = ΔKE) lets you connect work directly to speed changes without ever computing acceleration. Kinetic energy (½mv²) and potential energy (mgh for gravity, ½kx² for springs) trade off under conservation of mechanical energy when no friction is present; when friction acts, track the energy it converts to heat separately using KE₁+PE₁ = KE₂+PE₂+E_lost. The most common errors are forgetting that perpendicular forces do zero work, and treating energy as something that disappears rather than converts.
