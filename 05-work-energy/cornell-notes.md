# Cornell Notes — Unit 5: Work & Energy

> **How to use:** Fill in the **Notes** column during class/reading, write **Cues** afterward,
> and summarize in your own words at the bottom.

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| Define work | `W = Fd cos θ`, where θ is the angle between the force and the displacement. Work is a **scalar** (no direction), measured in joules (J). |
| When is work zero? | When `θ = 90°` (force perpendicular to displacement, e.g., carrying a bag horizontally — the carrying force is vertical, displacement is horizontal) OR when displacement is zero (pushing on a wall that doesn't move). |
| When is work negative? | When the force has a component opposite to the displacement (e.g., friction acting on a moving object, or a decelerating force). |
| State the work-energy theorem | `W_net = ΔKE = KE_f − KE_i`. The total (net) work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy — this connects force-based and energy-based problem solving. |
| Define kinetic energy | `KE = ½mv²`. Always positive (mass and v² are both non-negative). Depends on the square of speed — doubling speed quadruples KE. |
| Define gravitational potential energy | `PE_g = mgh`, measured relative to a chosen reference height (h = 0 level is your choice — only *changes* in PE matter physically). |
| Define elastic (spring) potential energy | `PE_spring = ½kx²`, where `k` is the spring constant and `x` is the displacement from the spring's natural (unstretched/uncompressed) length. |
| What's the difference between conservative and non-conservative forces? | Conservative forces (gravity, spring force) store energy that can be fully recovered — work done against them doesn't depend on path. Non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance) dissipate energy as heat/sound — it can't be recovered as mechanical energy. |
| State conservation of mechanical energy | `KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f`. **Only valid when no non-conservative forces do work** (i.e., frictionless, no air resistance). |
| How do you handle friction in an energy problem? | `KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + E_lost`, where `E_lost` is the energy converted to heat by friction, often calculated as `f × d` (friction force times distance). |
| Define power, and its formula | `P = W/t` — the *rate* at which work is done or energy is transferred, measured in watts (1 W = 1 J/s). Two machines can do the same total work but have very different power if one takes longer. |

## Quick Reference

| Quantity | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Work | `W = Fd cos θ` | Zero if F ⊥ d; scalar |
| Kinetic Energy | `KE = ½mv²` | Always ≥ 0 |
| Gravitational PE | `PE_g = mgh` | Reference height is your choice |
| Spring PE | `PE_spring = ½kx²` | x = displacement from natural length |
| Power | `P = W/t` | Rate of doing work, in watts |

## Common Mistakes to Flag in Your Own Work

- [ ] Did I forget the `cos θ` term and assume `W = Fd` even when force and displacement aren't parallel?
- [ ] Did I apply conservation of mechanical energy even though friction/air resistance was present?
- [ ] Did I confuse work, energy, and power — e.g., report a "power" answer in joules instead of watts?
- [ ] Did I forget that KE depends on `v²`, not `v` (so doubling speed quadruples KE, not doubles it)?
- [ ] Did I pick an inconsistent reference height for gravitational PE partway through a problem?
