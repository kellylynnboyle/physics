# Cornell Notes — Work & Energy

> **How to use:** Cover the "Notes" column and try to answer each cue from memory first.

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| Define work, with formula, and explain the role of $\theta$. | $W = Fd\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle BETWEEN the force vector and the displacement vector. Only the component of force parallel to the displacement does work. Scalar quantity — can be positive, negative, or zero. |
| Under what condition is the work done by a force exactly zero? | When the force is perpendicular to the displacement ($\theta = 90°$, $\cos\theta=0$) — e.g., normal force or gravity on something moving purely horizontally. Also zero if there's no displacement at all (e.g., holding a heavy box stationary does zero work, even though it's tiring). |
| When is work done by a force NEGATIVE? | When the force has a component opposite to the displacement ($\theta$ between 90° and 180°) — e.g., kinetic friction always does negative work on a moving object, since friction opposes motion. |
| Define kinetic energy and state the Work-Energy Theorem. | $KE = \tfrac12mv^2$. Work-Energy Theorem: $W_{net} = \Delta KE$ — the NET work (sum of work from all forces) done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy. Lets you find speed changes from forces without needing time or acceleration. |
| Define gravitational potential energy. What's tricky about "h"? | $PE_g = mgh$. $h$ is measured relative to a reference point YOU choose — only changes in height ($\Delta h$) actually matter physically; the reference point itself is arbitrary and can be set anywhere convenient (ground, table top, etc.), as long as you're consistent within one problem. |
| State conservation of mechanical energy, and the condition required for it to hold. | $KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f$ — holds ONLY when no non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance, applied pushes) do work on the system. If friction is present, mechanical energy is NOT conserved on its own — some converts to thermal energy. |
| What's the difference between a conservative and non-conservative force? | Conservative (e.g., gravity, spring force): work done depends only on start/end position, not the path taken; energy is fully recoverable (stored as potential energy). Non-conservative (e.g., friction, air resistance): work done depends on the path length; energy converts irreversibly to heat/sound, "lost" from the mechanical energy budget (though still conserved in the broader sense — total energy of the universe doesn't disappear). |
| How do you modify the energy equation when friction IS present? | $KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + |W_{friction}|$, where $W_{friction} = f \times d$ (using the friction force and distance traveled while it acts). The "lost" mechanical energy shows up as this added term, not as a violation of energy conservation overall. |
| Define power, with formula(s), and explain why it's different from work/energy. | $P = W/t$ (also $P = Fv$ for constant force along the direction of motion). Power is the RATE of doing work/transferring energy — two processes can involve the identical amount of work (energy) but require very different power if they happen over different amounts of time. Units: Watts (1 W = 1 J/s). |
| Why does "lifting something faster" require more power but not more work? | Work only depends on force and displacement ($W=Fd\cos\theta$) — lifting the same object the same height requires the same work regardless of speed. But doing that work in LESS time means a HIGHER power ($P=W/t$), since power scales inversely with time for fixed work. |

## Summary (write this in your own words first)

Work ($W=Fd\cos\theta$) is how a force transfers energy to or from an object over a displacement, and the work-energy theorem ($W_{net}=\Delta KE$) links net work directly to a change in speed — a shortcut around kinematics when time isn't given. Mechanical energy (KE + PE) is conserved only when no non-conservative force like friction does work; when friction is present, some mechanical energy irreversibly converts to heat, and that loss must be explicitly added into the energy equation rather than ignored. Power measures how fast work is done or energy is transferred ($P=W/t$), which is a completely different question from how much work is done — the same lift can be low-power (slow) or high-power (fast) for identical total work.
