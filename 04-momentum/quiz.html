1. A 0.5 kg ball moves at 4 m/s. Which statement correctly compares its momentum and kinetic energy?

Momentum (p = mv) and kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²) are two distinct physical quantities. Momentum is a vector measured in kg·m/s; kinetic energy is a scalar measured in Joules. They are related but not interchangeable, and it is a common misconception to treat them as the same thing.

2. In a collision inside an isolated system, which quantity is ALWAYS conserved, regardless of whether the collision is elastic or inelastic?

Momentum is conserved in ALL collisions within an isolated system — elastic, inelastic, and perfectly inelastic. Kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions. This is one of the most important distinctions in the momentum unit.

3. Car A (1000 kg) moves east at 10 m/s. Car B (1000 kg) moves west at 10 m/s. What is the total momentum of the two-car system?

Momentum is a vector, so direction (sign) matters. If east is positive: p_A = (1000)(+10) = +10,000 kg·m/s, and p_B = (1000)(−10) = −10,000 kg·m/s. Total = +10,000 + (−10,000) = 0 kg·m/s. Two objects moving toward each other have momenta of opposite sign, which can partially or fully cancel.

4. A hockey puck slides across ice and slowly comes to a stop due to friction with the ice surface. Is momentum conserved for the puck alone during this process?

Momentum conservation applies to an isolated system experiencing no net external force. The puck alone is not isolated — friction from the ice (part of the "outside world," not part of the puck-only system) is an external force that removes momentum from the puck over time. To use conservation of momentum validly, you must define a system where all significant forces are internal (or friction is negligible over the interaction).

5. Two lumps of clay collide head-on and stick together, moving off as one combined mass. This is an example of:

When objects stick together and move with one common final velocity, the collision is perfectly inelastic. Momentum is still conserved (it always is for an isolated system), but kinetic energy is NOT conserved — a significant amount converts to heat, sound, and deformation of the clay. Perfectly inelastic collisions lose the maximum possible kinetic energy allowed by momentum conservation.

6. In a perfectly elastic collision between two billiard balls, which statement is true?

Elastic collisions are defined as collisions in which BOTH total momentum and total kinetic energy are conserved. This is what distinguishes them from inelastic collisions, where momentum is still conserved but some kinetic energy is lost to other energy forms.

7. Object X has mass 20 kg and speed 1 m/s. Object Y has mass 0.1 kg and speed 300 m/s. Which has more momentum?

p = mv, so mass and velocity both matter equally. p_X = (20)(1) = 20 kg·m/s. p_Y = (0.1)(300) = 30 kg·m/s. Even though Object Y is far less massive, its much higher velocity gives it more momentum. Larger mass does not automatically mean larger momentum — always calculate both.

8. A car's airbag increases the time over which a passenger's momentum changes to zero during a crash. According to the impulse-momentum theorem, what effect does this have?

J = FΔt = Δp. The passenger's change in momentum (Δp) during the crash is fixed by their mass and change in velocity — the airbag doesn't change that. But by increasing the time (Δt) over which that Δp occurs, the airbag reduces the average force (F) needed, since F = Δp/Δt. This is the same principle behind crumple zones: a small force over a long time can produce the same impulse as a large force over a short time.

9. A 2 kg cart moving at +3 m/s collides and sticks with a 4 kg cart moving at −1 m/s (opposite direction). What is their common final velocity?

Total initial momentum = (2)(3) + (4)(−1) = 6 − 4 = 2 kg·m/s. For a perfectly inelastic collision, (m₁ + m₂)vf = 2, so vf = 2 / 6 ≈ 0.33 m/s. Note the negative sign on the second cart's velocity — forgetting it (using +1 instead of −1) is a very common error and gives a wrong answer of +1.67 m/s.

10. Which of the following best describes what happens to kinetic energy in a real (non-ideal) car crash where the cars crumple and come to rest tangled together?

This is a perfectly inelastic (or strongly inelastic) collision: momentum of the isolated system is conserved, but kinetic energy is not — it converts into heat, sound, and permanent deformation of the vehicles. Energy overall is still conserved (first law of thermodynamics), just not kinetic energy specifically.

11. Two ice skaters, initially at rest and facing each other, push off from one another. Which statement correctly applies conservation of momentum?

Initial total momentum is zero (both at rest). Since the ice is assumed frictionless (isolated system), total momentum after the push-off must still be zero: m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f = 0, so the two skaters move apart with momenta equal in magnitude but opposite in sign/direction. If they have different masses, they will have different speeds (the lighter skater moves faster), but their momenta remain equal and opposite.

12. A student sets up a 1D collision problem but forgets to assign a negative sign to an object moving in the "negative" direction. What is the most likely consequence?

Because momentum is a vector, every velocity must carry the correct sign relative to the chosen positive direction. Dropping or misassigning a sign changes the computed total momentum (a magnitude-only sum instead of a signed vector sum), which propagates into any solved final velocity — this is one of the most common errors in 1D collision problems.

13. A net force of 50 N acts on an object for 0.2 seconds. What is the impulse delivered, and what does it equal?

J = FΔt = (50 N)(0.2 s) = 10 N·s = 10 kg·m/s. By the impulse-momentum theorem, this impulse equals the object's change in momentum, Δp — not its velocity, kinetic energy, or mass directly.

14. Which pair of statements correctly separates momentum from kinetic energy?

Momentum (p = mv) is a vector, scales linearly with velocity, and is conserved in every collision within an isolated system. Kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²) is a scalar, scales with velocity squared, and is conserved only in elastic collisions. Confusing these two quantities is one of the most persistent misconceptions in this unit.
