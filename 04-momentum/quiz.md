# Unit 4 — Momentum — Self-Check Quiz

Built from the questions students miss most often on momentum tests. Try each question yourself before revealing the answer.

**How to use:** Get 80%+ (8/10) before moving on. Re-read the linked note section for anything you miss.

---

**1.** Why do airbags reduce injury in a car crash, in terms of impulse?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The change in momentum (Δp) of the passenger is fixed by the crash (going from some speed to zero). Since impulse J = FΔt = Δp, increasing the collision **time** (Δt) by cushioning the stop means the average **force** (F) must decrease to produce the same Δp.

*Most-missed reason:* students say "airbags absorb the force" without connecting it to the FΔt = Δp relationship — the key idea is trading time for force, not "absorbing" energy vaguely.
</details>

---

**2.** A 0.2 kg ball moving at 4 m/s bounces straight back at 4 m/s after hitting a wall. What is the change in momentum?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Δp = m(v_f − v_i) = 0.2(−4 − 4) = 0.2(−8) = **−1.6 kg·m/s** (magnitude 1.6 kg·m/s, direction reversed).

*Most-missed reason:* students compute 0.2(4 − 4) = 0 by forgetting the bounce reverses the sign of velocity — a full bounce-back has *double* the speed's worth of momentum change, not zero.
</details>

---

**3.** Is kinetic energy always conserved when momentum is conserved in a collision?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**No.** Momentum is conserved in *every* collision in a closed system, but kinetic energy is only conserved in **elastic** collisions. In inelastic collisions, some KE converts to heat, sound, or deformation even though momentum is still conserved.

*Most-missed reason:* students assume the two conservation laws always travel together, since both are "conserved quantities," without realizing they're independent conditions.
</details>

---

**4.** A 4 kg cart moving at 3 m/s (call this the positive direction) collides head-on with a 2 kg cart moving at 5 m/s in the opposite direction. They stick together. Find the common final velocity.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Using signs: v₂ = −5 m/s (opposite direction).
m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁+m₂)v' → (4)(3) + (2)(−5) = (6)v' → 12 − 10 = 6v' → v' = 2/6 = **0.33 m/s** in the original positive direction.

*Most-missed reason:* forgetting to assign a negative sign to the second cart's velocity since it moves opposite the first — treating both speeds as positive gives a wrong, too-large answer.
</details>

---

**5.** True or False: a heavy, slow-moving truck can have the same momentum as a light, fast-moving car.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**True.** p = mv — a large mass at low velocity can equal a small mass at high velocity (e.g., 2000 kg × 5 m/s = 10,000 kg·m/s = 1000 kg × 10 m/s).

*Most-missed reason:* students intuitively think "heavier = automatically more momentum," ignoring that momentum depends on *both* mass and velocity together.
</details>

---

**6.** A perfectly elastic collision occurs between two equal-mass objects, one moving and one at rest. After the collision, what happens (for a 1D head-on collision)?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The moving object **stops completely**, and the object that was at rest moves off with the *original* velocity of the first object. This is a special case (equal masses, elastic, head-on) often seen in Newton's cradle / billiards.

*Most-missed reason:* students assume both objects end up moving at some shared intermediate speed, which is actually the *inelastic* (sticking) case, not the elastic case.
</details>

---

**7.** A 0.15 kg baseball moving at 40 m/s is hit and leaves the bat at 50 m/s in the opposite direction. If contact time is 0.01 s, find the average force on the ball.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Δp = m(v_f − v_i) = 0.15(50 − (−40)) = 0.15(90) = 13.5 kg·m/s
F = Δp/Δt = 13.5/0.01 = **1350 N**

*Most-missed reason:* forgetting to treat the reversal as a sign change (v_i = −40, not +40 relative to the final direction), which nearly halves the calculated impulse and force if missed.
</details>

---

**8.** What's the difference between an "inelastic" and a "perfectly inelastic" collision?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Inelastic**: momentum is conserved, KE is not, but the objects don't necessarily stick together (they can separate at different final velocities). **Perfectly inelastic**: a special case of inelastic where the objects stick together and share one common final velocity.

*Most-missed reason:* students use "inelastic" and "perfectly inelastic" interchangeably, missing that perfectly inelastic is the specific "stick together" sub-case, not a synonym.
</details>

---

**9.** Two ice skaters, initially at rest, push off from each other. Skater A (50 kg) moves away at 3 m/s. If Skater B has a mass of 75 kg, how fast does Skater B move?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Total initial momentum = 0 (both at rest). So m_A v_A + m_B v_B = 0 → (50)(3) + 75v_B = 0 → v_B = −150/75 = **−2 m/s** (i.e., 2 m/s in the opposite direction from Skater A).

*Most-missed reason:* students don't recognize "initially at rest" means total momentum = 0, and instead try to guess a formula involving relative mass ratios without setting up the conservation equation properly.
</details>

---

**10.** Why does a longer follow-through in a golf swing (keeping the club in contact with the ball slightly longer) increase the ball's final speed for the same swing force?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

By the impulse-momentum theorem, J = FΔt = Δp. For the same average force F, a longer contact time Δt produces a larger impulse J, which means a larger change in the ball's momentum — and since the ball's mass is fixed, a bigger Δp means a bigger Δv (faster ball).

*Most-missed reason:* students focus only on the force of the swing and forget that contact *time* is an equally important factor in the impulse equation.
</details>
