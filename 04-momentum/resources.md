## Practice Problems

1. **Momentum calculation (basic).** A 1200 kg car travels north at 15 m/s. What is its momentum?
   *Answer:* p = mv = (1200 kg)(15 m/s) = **18,000 kg·m/s, north**.

2. **Momentum calculation (compare two objects).** A 0.145 kg baseball travels at 40 m/s. A 7.3 kg bowling ball rolls at 2 m/s. Which has more momentum?
   *Answer:* p_baseball = (0.145)(40) = 5.8 kg·m/s. p_bowling ball = (7.3)(2) = 14.6 kg·m/s. The **bowling ball** has more momentum, despite the baseball's much higher speed.

3. **Impulse.** A 0.15 kg baseball traveling at +30 m/s is struck by a bat and leaves at −40 m/s (reversed direction). What impulse did the bat deliver to the ball?
   *Answer:* J = Δp = m(vf − vi) = 0.15(−40 − 30) = 0.15(−70) = **−10.5 kg·m/s** (i.e., 10.5 kg·m/s in the direction the ball was hit).

4. **Force from impulse.** Using the previous problem, if the bat was in contact with the ball for 0.01 s, what average force did the bat exert on the ball?
   *Answer:* F = J/Δt = −10.5 / 0.01 = **−1050 N** (magnitude 1050 N, in the direction of the hit).

5. **Elastic collision (equal masses).** A 2 kg cart moving at +6 m/s collides elastically head-on with an identical stationary 2 kg cart. For an elastic collision between equal masses where the second is initially at rest, the first cart stops and the second moves off with the first cart's initial velocity. What are the final velocities?
   *Answer:* v1f = **0 m/s**, v2f = **+6 m/s** (a classic result for equal-mass elastic collisions, verifiable by checking both momentum and KE are conserved: initial p = 12 kg·m/s = final p = 0 + 12; initial KE = 36 J = final KE = 0 + 36).

6. **Perfectly inelastic collision.** A 1500 kg car moving at +20 m/s rear-ends a stationary 1500 kg car, and the two vehicles lock together. What is their common velocity immediately after the collision?
   *Answer:* (m1+m2)vf = m1v1i + m2v2i → vf = (1500)(20) / (3000) = **+10 m/s**.

7. **Perfectly inelastic collision with KE loss.** For the collision in problem 6, how much kinetic energy was lost?
   *Answer:* KE_initial = ½(1500)(20²) = 300,000 J. KE_final = ½(3000)(10²) = 150,000 J. KE lost = 300,000 − 150,000 = **150,000 J** (converted to heat, sound, and deformation).

8. **Two-object 1D collision with opposite directions (challenge).** A 3 kg object moving at +4 m/s collides with a 5 kg object moving at −2 m/s. They collide and stick together. Find the common final velocity, and state whether momentum and/or kinetic energy were conserved.
   *Answer:* Total initial p = (3)(4) + (5)(−2) = 12 − 10 = 2 kg·m/s. vf = 2 / (3+5) = **+0.25 m/s**. Momentum is conserved (2 kg·m/s before and after); kinetic energy is NOT conserved, since this is a perfectly inelastic collision (KE_initial = 24+10 = 34 J; KE_final = ½(8)(0.25²) = 0.25 J — most of the kinetic energy was lost).

## Real-World Applications

- **Car crash safety design (crumple zones and airbags).** Modern cars are engineered with crumple zones that deform during a collision, extending the time (Δt) over which the car's momentum changes to zero. By the impulse-momentum theorem (J = FΔt = Δp), a longer Δt for the same Δp means a smaller average force on the occupants, reducing injury. Airbags work the same way, cushioning the occupant and further extending the stopping time.

- **Rocket propulsion.** Rockets obey conservation of momentum: hot gas is expelled backward at high speed, and by conservation of momentum for the isolated rocket-plus-fuel system, the rocket itself gains momentum in the forward direction. No air or ground contact is needed — this is why rockets work in the vacuum of space, unlike propeller- or wheel-based propulsion.

- **Billiards and pool.** When a cue ball strikes another ball nearly head-on, the collision is close to elastic, and much of the cue ball's momentum and kinetic energy transfer to the struck ball. Skilled players use conservation of momentum intuitively, predicting how struck balls will move based on the cue ball's speed, angle, and mass (all pool balls have equal mass).

- **Sports collisions (e.g., football tackles).** When a tackler collides with and grips a ball carrier, the collision is approximately perfectly inelastic — both players move together (briefly) at a common velocity afterward. Momentum is conserved between the two players, but a large amount of kinetic energy is lost to sound, heat, and the deformation/impact absorbed by pads, muscle, and tissue — which is part of why padding and proper tackling technique matter for reducing injury.

- **Newton's cradle.** The classic desktop toy demonstrates near-elastic collisions between identical steel balls: lifting and releasing one ball transfers momentum and kinetic energy almost perfectly through the row, launching a ball off the opposite end at nearly the same speed, while the middle balls stay essentially motionless. Small energy losses to sound and heat cause the swinging balls to gradually lose amplitude over time.
