# Cornell Notes: Momentum and Collisions

**Topic/Date:** _____________

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is momentum, and how is it calculated? | Momentum (p) is a vector quantity equal to mass times velocity: p = m v. Units are kg·m/s. Because it's a vector, direction matters — momentum in one direction is often treated as positive and the opposite direction as negative. |
| Why is momentum described as a "vector quantity"? | Because it has both magnitude and direction. Two objects moving toward each other have momenta with opposite signs, so their momenta partially cancel rather than simply add. Forgetting this causes sign errors in collision problems. |
| What is impulse, and how does it relate to momentum? | Impulse (J) is the product of an average force and the time interval it acts: J = F Δt. The impulse-momentum theorem states J = Δp — the impulse on an object equals its change in momentum. |
| Why does a longer contact time reduce force? | Since J = F Δt = Δp is fixed for a given change in momentum, increasing Δt (contact/interaction time) must decrease the average force F needed to produce that same Δp. This is why airbags, crumple zones, and bent knees reduce injury. |
| What does it mean for momentum to be "conserved"? | In an isolated system (no net external force), the total momentum before an event equals the total momentum after it: m1v1i + m2v2i = m1v1f + m2v2f. |
| Why is momentum conserved during a collision even though friction exists? | During the brief moment of collision, the internal forces between colliding objects are far larger than external forces like friction, so external forces can be treated as negligible over that short time — momentum is effectively conserved. |
| What distinguishes an elastic collision from an inelastic one? | In an elastic collision, kinetic energy is conserved along with momentum. In an inelastic collision, kinetic energy is NOT conserved (some converts to heat, sound, deformation) — but momentum is still conserved in both cases. |
| What is a perfectly inelastic collision, and what equation describes it? | A perfectly inelastic collision is one where the objects stick together and move with one common final velocity: m1v1i + m2v2i = (m1 + m2) v_f. Kinetic energy is usually NOT zero afterward — only true if v_f itself equals zero. |
| Does a heavier object always dominate a collision? | No. The outcome depends on the product of mass AND velocity for each object, not mass alone. A light, fast object can transfer significant momentum. Direction (sign) must always be included in the calculation. |
| What's a common mistake with kinetic energy in collisions? | Assuming KE is always conserved. It is only conserved in elastic collisions. Students should always check whether a problem specifies "elastic" before assuming KE in equals KE out. |

## Summary

Momentum (p = m v) is a vector quantity that combines an object's mass and velocity, and it is connected to force and time through the impulse-momentum theorem, J = F Δt = Δp. In any isolated system, total momentum is conserved during a collision because the internal forces between colliding objects vastly outweigh external forces like friction during that brief interval. Collisions are classified by what happens to kinetic energy: elastic collisions conserve it, while inelastic collisions — including the special case of perfectly inelastic collisions where objects stick together — do not, even though momentum is conserved either way. The most common student errors involve forgetting that momentum has direction, assuming heavier objects automatically "win," assuming kinetic energy is always conserved, and misunderstanding how a longer collision time reduces force rather than increasing it. Mastering this unit means being able to set up and solve conservation-of-momentum equations correctly, with proper signs, for both elastic and inelastic scenarios.
