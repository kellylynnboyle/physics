## Cue Column | Notes

| Cue Column (questions/keywords) | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is **momentum**? | p = mv. A vector quantity (units: kg·m/s) — direction matters, and momentum can be positive or negative depending on your sign convention, just like velocity. |
| What is **impulse**? | J = FΔt = Δp. Impulse is the change in momentum, caused by a force acting over a time interval. A small force over a long time can produce the same impulse (and Δp) as a large force over a short time. |
| What is the **impulse-momentum theorem**? | FΔt = mΔv (equivalent to Δp). This connects force/time (how a collision happens) to momentum change (what results). It's why airbags and padding work: increasing Δt for the same Δp reduces the average force F. |
| What is **conservation of momentum**? | For an isolated system (no external net force), total momentum before an event equals total momentum after: p_total,before = p_total,after, i.e., Σm₁v₁ + m₂v₂ (before) = Σm₁v₁' + m₂v₂' (after). This holds in **every** collision type — elastic, inelastic, explosions — as long as the system is isolated. |
| What's the difference between an **elastic** and **inelastic** collision? | **Elastic:** both momentum AND kinetic energy are conserved (objects bounce apart, e.g., billiard balls). **Inelastic:** momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is NOT (some KE converts to heat/sound/deformation). |
| What is a **perfectly inelastic collision**? | A special inelastic case where the objects stick together after colliding and move with one common final velocity. Solve using: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v_final. |
| Is kinetic energy ALWAYS conserved in collisions? | **No** — only in elastic collisions. Momentum, however, is conserved in essentially every collision (as long as no significant external force like friction with the ground acts during the brief collision time). Don't assume KE conservation unless the problem specifies an elastic collision. |
| How do you handle **momentum in 2D**? | Treat x and y momentum components separately, each conserved independently: Σpₓ,before = Σpₓ,after and Σpᵧ,before = Σpᵧ,after. Solve like two separate 1D conservation problems. |
| Common trap: what about **direction/sign** in a collision? | Momentum is a vector — objects moving in opposite directions have momenta with opposite signs, and these must be added algebraically (not just magnitudes). A head-on collision where one cart moves +3 m/s and another moves −2 m/s requires you to keep those signs through the whole calculation. |

---

## Summary

Momentum (p = mv) and impulse (J = FΔt = Δp) connect force and time to changes in motion, and the impulse-momentum theorem explains why extending collision time (airbags, crumple zones, padding) reduces the force experienced. The unit's central tool is conservation of momentum, which holds for any isolated system regardless of collision type — but conservation of kinetic energy only holds for elastic collisions. The most common errors are forgetting momentum's vector/signed nature and incorrectly assuming kinetic energy is conserved when it isn't specified as elastic.
