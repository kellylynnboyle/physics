# Cornell Notes: Momentum

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is momentum? | Momentum (**p**) is a vector quantity describing an object's "quantity of motion." **p = mv**, where m is mass (kg) and v is velocity (m/s). Units: kg·m/s. Because it depends on velocity, momentum points in the same direction as the object's motion — direction (sign) always matters. |
| Is momentum a scalar or a vector? | Vector. Two objects can have equal mass and equal speed but opposite momenta if they move in opposite directions. In 1D problems, one direction must be chosen as positive and the other as negative; a velocity in the negative direction contributes negative momentum to the total. |
| What is impulse? | Impulse (**J**) is the product of a net force and the time interval it acts over: **J = FΔt**. Impulse is also a vector, with the same direction as the net force. |
| What is the impulse-momentum theorem? | **J = FΔt = Δp = mΔv = mvf − mvᵢ**. The net impulse delivered to an object equals its change in momentum. This means the *same* change in momentum (Δp) can be produced by a large force over a short time OR a small force over a long time. Example: airbags and crumple zones increase the collision time Δt, which reduces the peak force F needed to produce the same Δp, protecting occupants. |
| When is momentum conserved? | Momentum is conserved for an **isolated system** — one with **no net external force** acting on it. Internal forces (objects pushing/pulling on each other, like during a collision) do not change the system's total momentum because they occur in equal-and-opposite Newton's-third-law pairs. If an external force (e.g., friction from the ground, gravity along the direction of motion) acts on the objects being analyzed, momentum conservation does *not* automatically apply to just those objects — the external force must be small/negligible over the interaction time, or included in the analysis. |
| Momentum conservation equation (1D, two objects) | **m₁v₁ᵢ + m₂v₂ᵢ = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f**. Every velocity term must carry the correct sign based on the chosen positive direction. A common error is dropping or misassigning a negative sign for an object moving in the "negative" direction — this changes the total momentum and gives a wrong answer. |
| What is an elastic collision? | A collision in which **both total momentum and total kinetic energy are conserved**. Objects bounce off each other and separate afterward. Idealized examples: collisions between hard, non-deforming objects (e.g., billiard balls, idealized atomic/gas particle collisions). |
| What is an inelastic collision? | A collision in which **total momentum is conserved but total kinetic energy is NOT conserved**. Some kinetic energy converts to other forms of energy — heat, sound, and permanent deformation of the colliding objects. Most real-world macroscopic collisions are inelastic to some degree. |
| What is a perfectly inelastic collision? | A special case of inelastic collision where the colliding objects **stick together** and move afterward with **one common final velocity**. Momentum is still conserved: m₁v₁ᵢ + m₂v₂ᵢ = (m₁ + m₂)vf. This type of collision loses the maximum possible kinetic energy consistent with momentum conservation. |
| How do I set up a 1D collision problem? | 1. Choose and state a positive direction. 2. Assign signed velocities to each object based on that direction. 3. Identify collision type (elastic / inelastic / perfectly inelastic) to know what quantities are conserved. 4. Write the momentum conservation equation. 5. For perfectly inelastic collisions, combine masses and solve for one common vf. For elastic collisions, momentum AND kinetic energy equations may both be needed. 6. Solve algebraically, then check that the sign of the answer makes physical sense. |
| Is momentum the same as kinetic energy? | No. **Momentum (p = mv)** is a vector, is conserved in *every* collision within an isolated system (elastic or inelastic), and depends linearly on velocity. **Kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²)** is a scalar, is conserved *only* in elastic collisions, and depends on velocity squared. A common misconception is treating these as interchangeable "the more momentum, the more energy" — they are different quantities governed by different conservation rules. |
| Does a bigger mass always mean bigger momentum? | No. Momentum depends equally on mass and velocity (p = mv). A small, fast-moving object (e.g., a bullet) can have more momentum than a large, slow-moving object (e.g., a slowly rolling truck), depending on the actual numbers. Always calculate — don't assume based on size alone. |

## Summary

Momentum (p = mv) is a vector quantity that captures both how much mass is moving and in which direction, and it is changed only by an impulse (J = FΔt = Δp) — a fact used in real safety design to trade force for time. For any isolated system, free of net external force, total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after, which makes 1D collision problems solvable once a sign convention is fixed and each velocity is entered with its correct positive or negative value. Collisions are classified by what else is conserved besides momentum: elastic collisions conserve kinetic energy too, inelastic collisions lose some kinetic energy to heat, sound, or deformation, and perfectly inelastic collisions are the extreme case where the objects stick together and share one final velocity while losing the maximum possible kinetic energy. Throughout, momentum and kinetic energy must be kept conceptually separate — one is a vector conserved in all collisions of an isolated system, the other is a scalar conserved only when the collision is elastic. Mastery of this section means being able to correctly sign every velocity, correctly classify a collision, and correctly choose which conservation law(s) apply before solving.
