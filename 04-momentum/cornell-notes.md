## Topic: Momentum and Impulse

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| Definition of momentum | p = mv — a vector, so direction matters (a 2 kg object moving left and a 2 kg object moving right at the same speed have opposite-sign momentum). |
| Definition of impulse | J = FΔt — force applied over a time interval. |
| Impulse-momentum theorem | J = Δp, i.e., FΔt = m(v_f − v_i). Impulse causes a change in momentum. |
| Why does extending collision time matter? | For a given Δp (fixed change in momentum), a longer Δt means a smaller average force F. This is why airbags, crumple zones, and "giving" when catching a ball all reduce injury/damage. |

## Topic: Conservation of Momentum

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| Statement | In an isolated system (no net external force), total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after. |
| Is momentum conserved for each object individually? | No — only the **total** momentum of the whole system is conserved. Individual objects can gain or lose momentum during a collision. |
| Two-object collision equation | m₁v₁ᵢ + m₂v₂ᵢ = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f |

## Topic: Types of Collisions

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| Elastic collision | Momentum conserved AND kinetic energy conserved. (Rare in the real world — closest examples: billiard balls, some subatomic particle collisions.) |
| Inelastic collision | Momentum conserved, but kinetic energy is NOT conserved (some KE converts to heat, sound, or deformation). |
| Perfectly inelastic collision | A special case of inelastic: the two objects stick together and move with one common final velocity after the collision. |
| Is momentum ever "not conserved" in a collision? | Only if there's a significant *external* force on the system (e.g., friction from the ground, or an outside push) — for idealized collision problems, we treat the system as isolated. |

## Topic: The Momentum vs. Kinetic Energy Trap

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| Which quantity is ALWAYS conserved in a collision? | Momentum — always, for an isolated system, no matter what type of collision. |
| Which quantity is SOMETIMES conserved? | Kinetic energy — only in elastic collisions. |
| Common mistake to avoid | Never assume KE is conserved unless the problem specifically states (or you can prove) the collision is elastic. Default to using momentum conservation first. |
