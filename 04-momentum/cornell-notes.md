## Notes page

| Cues | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is momentum? | p = mv. Vector quantity — units kg·m/s. Direction matters just as much as magnitude. |
| What is impulse? | J = FΔt. Also equals the change in momentum: J = Δp (impulse-momentum theorem). |
| Why does increasing Δt matter? | For a fixed Δp, a longer contact time (Δt) means a smaller average force. This is why airbags, crumple zones, and bending your knees on landing reduce injury. |
| Conservation of momentum — when does it apply? | In an ISOLATED system (no net external force). Total momentum before a collision/interaction = total momentum after. Always true for isolated systems, regardless of collision type. |
| Elastic collision | Momentum conserved AND kinetic energy conserved. Objects bounce apart without permanent deformation. |
| Inelastic collision | Momentum conserved, but KE is NOT conserved (converted to heat/sound/deformation). |
| Perfectly inelastic collision | A special inelastic case: objects stick together, move with one common final velocity. Maximum possible KE loss for that momentum. |
| Explosion | Reverse of perfectly inelastic: one object splits into pieces. Momentum still conserved (often starts at 0 if initially at rest); KE increases from stored energy. |
| Sign convention in 1-D collisions | Pick one direction as positive at the start. A velocity in the opposite direction is negative — this affects momentum sums directly. |
| Most common trap | Assuming "conserved" applies to kinetic energy the same way it does to momentum — it doesn't, except specifically in elastic collisions. |

*Reference summary: Momentum (p = mv) is a vector quantity that is always
conserved in an isolated system, no matter what kind of collision occurs.
Impulse (J = FΔt = Δp) connects force, time, and the resulting change in
momentum — spreading a collision over more time reduces the peak force.
Kinetic energy, unlike momentum, is only conserved in elastic collisions;
inelastic collisions conserve momentum but lose kinetic energy to heat,
sound, or deformation, with perfectly inelastic collisions (objects
sticking together) losing the most. Correctly assigning direction/sign to
each object's velocity is essential, since momentum is a vector.*
