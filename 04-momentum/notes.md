# Unit 4: Momentum — Cornell Notes

**NGSS alignment:** HS-PS2-2 (conservation of momentum), HS-PS2-3 (impulse and collision forces). CA Physics course model, Semester 1, Week 7–8.

**Essential question:** What quantity stays constant when objects collide or push apart, and how can we use that to predict outcomes?

---

## Cornell Notes

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Momentum** definition | p = mv. A vector — direction matters. Units: kg·m/s. |
| **Impulse** definition | J = FΔt = Δp. Impulse is the change in momentum, caused by a net force acting over a time interval. |
| Why does a longer collision time reduce force? | Since J = FΔt is fixed by the needed Δp, increasing Δt (spreading the collision out) decreases the average force F needed. This is why airbags, crumple zones, and bending your knees when landing all work. |
| **Law of Conservation of Momentum** | In a closed system with no external net force, total momentum before = total momentum after: Σp_before = Σp_after. This holds even though individual objects' momenta change. |
| Is momentum conserved for EACH object, or the SYSTEM? | The system. Individual objects' momentum changes (that's the whole point of a collision) — it's the total (vector sum) that stays constant. |
| **Elastic collision** | Both momentum AND kinetic energy are conserved. Objects bounce apart (e.g., billiard balls, ideal springs). |
| **Inelastic collision** | Momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is NOT (some converts to heat/sound/deformation). Objects may or may not stick together. |
| **Perfectly inelastic collision** | A special inelastic case: objects stick together and move with a common final velocity after. Maximum kinetic energy is lost (relative to other outcomes with the same momentum). |
| Common misconception: "momentum is always conserved AND kinetic energy is always conserved" | False — momentum conservation applies to essentially all closed-system collisions; kinetic energy conservation is the special/stricter condition that only elastic collisions satisfy. |
| Sign convention in 1D collisions | Assign a positive direction before starting. An object moving in the negative direction has negative momentum — this matters heavily in "before and after" sums. |
| **Explosions / recoil** (e.g., a gun firing, ice skaters pushing apart) | Momentum starts at zero (system at rest) and stays at zero — the two resulting momenta must be equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, meaning the lighter object moves faster. |
| Does momentum depend on kinetic energy? | No — they're related but distinct: p = mv (linear in v), KE = ½mv² (quadratic in v). Two objects can have equal momentum but different KE, or vice versa. |

---

## Summary

Momentum (p = mv) is a bookkeeping tool for "how much motion" a system has, and impulse (J = FΔt) connects force and time to a change in momentum. The single most powerful idea in this unit is conservation: as long as no external force acts on a system, total momentum before a collision or explosion equals total momentum after — even though kinetic energy is only conserved in the special case of elastic collisions.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Momentum (p) | Mass × velocity, a vector quantity |
| Impulse (J) | Force × time interval; equals the change in momentum |
| Elastic collision | Collision where both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved |
| Inelastic collision | Collision where momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not |
| Perfectly inelastic collision | Objects stick together after colliding |
| Closed system | A system with no external net force acting on it |

## Practice Problems

1. A 1200 kg car traveling at 15 m/s collides with a stationary 1500 kg car, and they stick together. Find their common velocity after collision.
2. A 0.15 kg baseball traveling at 40 m/s is hit by a bat and leaves at 50 m/s in the opposite direction. If the bat is in contact for 0.01 s, find the average force on the ball.
3. A 60 kg skater at rest pushes off a 90 kg skater, also at rest. After the push, the 60 kg skater moves backward at 3 m/s. Find the 90 kg skater's velocity.

**Worked example (#1):** Conservation of momentum: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁+m₂)v_f → (1200)(15) + (1500)(0) = (2700)v_f → 18000 = 2700v_f → v_f = 6.67 m/s.

## Real-World Applications

- Car safety design: crumple zones and airbags increase collision time to reduce force (impulse-momentum theorem)
- Rocket propulsion: exhaust gas expelled backward gives the rocket forward momentum (conservation + Newton's 3rd Law)
- Sports: follow-through in swinging a bat or golf club increases contact time and force transfer
- Ballistic pendulum: classic lab/forensic method for finding a projectile's speed using momentum conservation

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:
- Calculate momentum and impulse for objects and systems (**DOK 2**)
- Apply the impulse-momentum theorem (J = FΔt = Δp) to real scenarios involving collision time and force (**DOK 3**)
- Apply conservation of momentum to solve 1D elastic, inelastic, and perfectly inelastic collision problems (**DOK 3**)
- Distinguish which quantities (momentum, kinetic energy) are conserved in each collision type (**DOK 2**)
- Analyze explosion/recoil scenarios using conservation of momentum (**DOK 3**)
