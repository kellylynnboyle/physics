# Unit 4 — Momentum — Cornell Notes

**Essential question:** What quantity is conserved in a collision, and how can we use that to predict the outcome without knowing the forces involved?

**NGSS connection:** HS-PS2-2 (mathematically support conservation of momentum) and HS-PS2-3 (design a device that minimizes force during a collision).

---

## Cue column | Notes

| Cue | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is **momentum**? | p = mv. A vector — has the direction of velocity. Units: kg·m/s. A large truck moving slowly can have the same momentum as a small car moving fast. |
| What is **impulse**? | J = FΔt (force × time interval) OR J = Δp (change in momentum). These are equal — the **impulse-momentum theorem**: FΔt = mΔv. |
| Why does impulse matter for safety design? | For a *fixed* Δp (same change in momentum, e.g., stopping a falling object), increasing Δt (time of impact) *decreases* the average force. This is why airbags, crumple zones, and padded landings work — they extend collision time to reduce peak force. |
| **Conservation of momentum** (law) | In a closed system with no external net force, total momentum before a collision equals total momentum after: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂' (all velocities as signed vectors). |
| **Elastic vs. inelastic collisions** | Elastic: both momentum AND kinetic energy are conserved (objects bounce apart, e.g., billiard balls). Inelastic: momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is NOT (some KE converts to heat/sound/deformation). **Perfectly inelastic**: objects stick together and move with a common final velocity. |
| Common trap: "momentum is always conserved, but KE is only sometimes conserved" | Total momentum of an isolated system is conserved in *every* collision type. Kinetic energy is only conserved in elastic collisions — this is the key distinguishing test. |
| How to solve a perfectly inelastic collision | m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v', solve directly for the single final velocity v'. |
| Sign convention trap | Velocities are vectors — a ball moving left at 3 m/s must be entered as **−3 m/s**, not 3 m/s, or the conservation equation will give the wrong answer. Always define a positive direction first and stick with it. |
| Why doesn't momentum conservation apply to *every* real-world collision perfectly? | External forces (friction with the ground, air resistance) act during real collisions and slightly reduce total system momentum — the law strictly applies to *closed/isolated* systems. |
| What's the difference between an object "bouncing" and "sticking" in terms of momentum change? | A bouncing object reverses direction, so its momentum change (and the impulse delivered to it) is *larger* than an object that just stops, because Δp = m(v_final − v_initial) is bigger when v_final is negative relative to v_initial. |

## Key vocabulary
momentum (p) · impulse (J) · impulse-momentum theorem · conservation of momentum · elastic collision · inelastic collision · perfectly inelastic collision · closed/isolated system

## Key formulas
- p = mv
- J = FΔt = Δp = m(v_f − v_i)
- Conservation: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂'
- Perfectly inelastic: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v'

---

## Summary (write in your own words after class)
Momentum (mass × velocity) is always conserved in an isolated system's collisions, giving us a powerful tool to solve for unknown velocities without needing to know the forces involved. Impulse links force, time, and momentum change — stretching out collision time reduces force, which is the physics behind most crash-safety engineering. Kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions, which is how we classify collision types.

## Real-world applications
- Car crumple zones and airbags (impulse-momentum theorem)
- Rocket propulsion (momentum conservation with changing mass)
- Billiards / pool (near-elastic collisions)
- Car crash forensics (using momentum conservation to reconstruct speeds)
- Boxing gloves and padded flooring in gymnastics

## Practice problems (by difficulty)
**Level 1 — plug and chug**
1. A 1200 kg car moves at 15 m/s. Find its momentum.
2. A 0.5 kg ball experiences a 20 N force for 0.1 s. Find the impulse and the resulting change in velocity.

**Level 2 — collisions**
3. A 3 kg cart moving at 4 m/s collides and sticks to a stationary 5 kg cart. Find their common final velocity.
4. A 2 kg ball moving at 6 m/s hits a stationary 2 kg ball elastically and stops completely. What is the second ball's velocity?

**Level 3 — design / conceptual**
5. Explain, using the impulse-momentum theorem, why a boxer "rolls with the punch" to reduce injury.
6. Design (in words) an egg-drop container that minimizes the force on the egg during landing. Explain your reasoning using impulse.
