| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is the formula for work, and what does θ mean? | W = Fd·cosθ, where θ is the angle between the force vector and the displacement vector. Only the force component *parallel* to displacement does work. Units: joules (J) = N·m. |
| When is work exactly zero? | When θ = 90° (cosθ = 0) — force perpendicular to displacement. Example: normal force on an object sliding horizontally does zero work; gravity does zero work on something moving purely horizontally; centripetal force in circular motion is always ⊥ to velocity, so it does zero work. |
| What does the work-energy theorem say? | W_net = ΔKE — the *net* (total, all forces combined) work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy. Work by a single force (like gravity alone) does NOT equal ΔKE unless it's the only force acting. |
| What is kinetic energy, and how does it scale with speed? | KE = ½mv². Depends on v², so doubling speed quadruples KE (not doubles it). Always positive (or zero), scalar, units of joules. |
| What is gravitational PE, and does the reference height matter? | PE = mgh, h measured from a chosen reference (zero) height. The PE *value* changes if you pick a different reference — but the *change* in PE (ΔPE) between two points is the same no matter what reference you chose. Physically, only ΔPE matters. |
| What is spring (elastic) PE? | PE_spring = ½kx², from Hooke's Law F = −kx (k = spring constant, x = displacement from natural/rest length). Like gravitational PE, it's stored energy that can convert to KE. |
| When is mechanical energy conserved? | When only conservative forces act (gravity, ideal springs) — no friction or air resistance. Then KE + PE = constant: KE gained equals PE lost, and vice versa, at every point along the motion. |
| What happens when friction/air resistance is present? | Mechanical energy (KE + PE) is NOT conserved — some is converted to heat. Correct equation: KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + energy lost to friction. Total energy (mechanical + heat) is still conserved, just not mechanical energy alone. |
| Is work done by friction positive or negative, and what does that mean? | Negative — friction opposes displacement, so it removes energy from the mechanical system (converts it to heat). It is not a force that adds energy. |
| What is power, and how is it different from work? | P = W/t = Fv. Power is the *rate* of doing work (watts, J/s), while work is the total amount of energy transferred (joules). Two objects can do identical work but have very different power if the time (or speed) differs — e.g., climbing the same stairs slowly vs. quickly. |
| Path independence of gravitational PE | The speed at the bottom of a frictionless drop depends only on the height dropped, not the shape/path taken to get there. Two differently-shaped frictionless ramps starting at the same height give the same final speed at the bottom. |
| Common unit mix-up | Joules (J) = work/energy. Watts (W) = power = J/s. Newtons (N) = force. Don't label a work value in watts, and don't divide by distance when you meant to divide by time (or vice versa) when converting between work and power. |

## Summary (write in your own words after studying)

Work transfers energy: W = Fd·cosθ, and only the force component along
the displacement counts (perpendicular forces do zero work). The
work-energy theorem ties *net* work to the change in kinetic energy,
W_net = ΔKE. Energy comes in forms — kinetic (½mv²), gravitational
potential (mgh, reference-dependent in value but not in change), and
elastic potential (½kx²) — and in a frictionless system their sum,
mechanical energy, stays constant: KE + PE = constant. Friction and air
resistance break that conservation by converting mechanical energy to
heat, so a loss term must be added: KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + losses.
Power is the *rate* of energy transfer, P = W/t = Fv, measured in watts
and distinct from both work (joules) and force (newtons).
