# Unit 5: Work & Energy

Work and energy give an alternative (often simpler) way to solve motion
problems, by tracking a scalar quantity — energy — instead of vector forces
and accelerations directly.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

1. Calculate work done by a force, including when force and displacement are
   not in the same direction (W = Fd cos θ).
2. Calculate kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²) and gravitational potential energy
   (PE = mgh).
3. Apply the work-energy theorem (W_net = ΔKE).
4. Apply conservation of mechanical energy correctly, identifying when
   non-conservative forces (like friction) require modifying the equation.
5. Calculate and interpret power (P = W/t = Fv).

## Key concepts to master

- **Work**: W = Fd cos θ, where θ is the angle **between the force and the
  displacement**. Work is zero if the force is perpendicular to displacement,
  or if there's no displacement at all — even if the force is large (e.g.,
  holding a heavy bag stationary does zero work on the bag).
- **Normal force and static friction often do zero work** on an object because
  they act perpendicular to the direction of the object's motion along a
  surface (e.g., normal force does no work on a person walking on flat ground).
- **Kinetic energy**: KE = ½mv², always ≥ 0, depends on **speed²**, not on
  direction — a car moving north and one moving south at the same speed have
  the same KE.
- **Gravitational potential energy**: PE = mgh, defined **relative to a chosen
  reference height** — only *changes* in PE are physically meaningful.
- **Work-energy theorem**: the **net** work done on an object equals its
  change in kinetic energy: W_net = ΔKE. This uses the net force, just like
  Newton's 2nd Law.
- **Conservation of mechanical energy** (KE + PE = constant) holds **only**
  when no non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance, applied pushes)
  do work on the system. If friction is present, mechanical energy is not
  conserved — it converts to heat: KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + energy lost to friction.
- **Power**: P = W/t = Fv (instantaneous). Power measures the *rate* of doing
  work — the same work done faster requires more power, not more energy.

## Cornell notes

### Cues / Questions
- Does carrying a box at constant height across a room require work on the box?
- When is work done by a force equal to zero?
- Why doesn't kinetic energy care about direction?
- Why is potential energy only meaningful as a *difference*?
- When can you use KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f, and when can't you?
- What's the difference between work and power?

### Notes
- **W = Fd cos θ**: always identify the angle between the *force in question*
  and the displacement — not the angle of an incline or some other reference,
  unless that happens to be the same angle.
- Carrying a box horizontally at constant velocity: the **applied vertical
  support force does zero work** because it's perpendicular to the horizontal
  displacement (even though you get tired — physics "work" is not the same as
  everyday effort).
- **Normal force does zero work** whenever motion is along the surface (force
  ⟂ displacement); it can do nonzero work when there's a component along the
  motion direction (e.g., an object riding up a moving incline).
- KE depends on **v²**, so speed direction is irrelevant, but the *sign* of
  work done (positive/negative) depends on whether the force has a component
  in the direction of motion (speeding up = positive work; slowing down =
  negative work).
- PE = mgh needs a **reference point** (often the ground, or the lowest point
  in the problem) — always state your reference height, since only Δh (and
  therefore ΔPE) has physical meaning for energy transfer.
- **W_net = ΔKE** — this is different from "any one force's work equals ΔKE."
  You must sum the work from *all* forces (or equivalently use the net force)
  to apply the theorem correctly.
- **Energy is conserved only when non-conservative forces do zero work.** With
  friction, use: KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + |W_friction|, where
  |W_friction| = f·d (energy "lost" to heat, not truly destroyed).
- **Power ≠ energy.** Two machines can do the same amount of work, but the one
  that does it faster has greater power (e.g., climbing the same stairs faster
  requires more power for the same total work/energy change).

### Summary (write in your own words)
Work only "counts" when a force has a component along the direction of
motion. Mechanical energy is conserved only in the absence of friction/other
non-conservative forces — otherwise, track the "lost" energy explicitly.
Power is about *how fast* energy is transferred, not how much.

## Commonly missed question types (what the quiz targets)

1. Calculating work without considering the angle between force and displacement.
2. Assuming holding/carrying something stationary or at constant height always
   means zero work is done by every force, when actually it means specific
   forces (like an upward support force during horizontal motion) do zero work.
3. Believing KE depends on velocity direction, not just speed.
4. Applying conservation of mechanical energy in a problem that includes friction.
5. Confusing "no work done by force A" with "net work is zero."
6. Confusing work (energy) with power (rate of energy transfer).

## Practice problems

1. A 10 kg box is pushed 5 m across a frictionless floor by a horizontal 20 N
   force. Find the work done and the box's final speed if it started at rest.
   *(Answer: W = 100 J, v_f ≈ 4.47 m/s)*
2. A 60 kg skier starts from rest at the top of a 40 m frictionless slope.
   Find her speed at the bottom using energy conservation.
   *(Answer: v ≈ 28.0 m/s, from mgh = ½mv²)*
3. The same skier's slope instead has friction that removes 8,000 J of energy
   by the bottom. Find her speed at the bottom.
   *(Answer: mgh − 8000 = ½mv² → v ≈ 24.4 m/s)*
4. A 5 kg block is pulled 3 m across a floor by a rope at 30° above
   horizontal with 15 N tension, with 5 N of kinetic friction opposing motion.
   Find the net work done on the block.
   *(Answer: W_tension ≈ 39.0 J, W_friction = −15 J, W_net ≈ 24.0 J)*
5. A motor lifts a 200 kg elevator 10 m in 8 s at constant speed. Find the
   work done and the average power output.
   *(Answer: W = mgh = 19,600 J, P = W/t = 2,450 W)*

## Real-world applications

- **Roller coasters**: energy conservation (with friction/air-resistance
  losses) predicts speeds at each point on the track.
- **Vehicle braking**: kinetic energy converted to heat via friction in brake
  pads.
- **Hydroelectric power**: gravitational PE of stored water converted to
  electrical energy — power output depends on flow rate (rate of energy transfer).
- **Human/animal locomotion**: metabolic power output vs. mechanical work
  done climbing stairs or running uphill.
