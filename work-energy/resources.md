# Work & Energy — Practice Problems, Resources & Applications

## Practice problems

### Level 1 — Direct substitution
1. A person pushes a box 12 m across a floor with a constant horizontal
   force of 40 N. Find the work done by the person.
2. A 5.0 kg object moves at 6.0 m/s. Find its kinetic energy. If its
   speed doubles to 12.0 m/s, find its new kinetic energy — by what
   factor did KE change?
3. A 2.0 kg book sits on a shelf 1.5 m above the floor. Find its
   gravitational PE relative to the floor. Then find its PE relative to
   a tabletop 0.8 m above the floor.

### Level 2 — Multi-step / choose your equation
4. A 1000 kg car traveling at 20 m/s brakes to a stop over a distance of
   50 m. Use the work-energy theorem to find the net braking force
   (assumed constant).
5. A 0.5 kg ball is dropped from rest from a height of 20 m. Using
   conservation of mechanical energy (ignore air resistance), find its
   speed just before hitting the ground. Then check your answer using a
   kinematics equation from Unit 1.
6. A worker carries a 15 kg toolbox at a constant height while walking
   20 m horizontally at constant velocity. How much work does the worker
   do against gravity on the toolbox? Explain why, in terms of the angle
   between force and displacement.
7. A crate slides 8.0 m across a rough floor, starting at 4.0 m/s and
   ending at rest. If its mass is 10 kg, how much energy was lost to
   friction? Find the average friction force.

### Level 3 — Power and combined concepts
8. An elevator motor lifts a 600 kg elevator car 30 m in 25 s at constant
   speed. Find the work done against gravity and the average power
   output of the motor.
9. Two students, A and B, each weigh 600 N and climb the same 4.0 m
   staircase. Student A takes 8.0 s; Student B takes 20.0 s. Compare
   the work done and the power output of each student.

### Challenge
10. A roller coaster car (mass 400 kg) starts from rest at the top of a
    45 m hill. It reaches the bottom of the hill, where a frictional
    section then brings it to a stop over the next 60 m of track. (a)
    Find its speed at the bottom of the hill, assuming the hill itself is
    frictionless. (b) Find the average friction force over the 60 m
    braking section. (c) How much total mechanical energy was converted
    to heat by the time the car stops?

for any PE calculation, and check answers with an instructor or the quiz

## Real-world applications

- **Roller coasters**: designers use energy conservation (KE + PE =
  constant, adjusted for track friction and air resistance) to determine
  hill heights, loop sizes, and required launch speeds.
- **Hydroelectric dams**: gravitational PE of stored water converts to
  KE as it falls, which turns turbines to generate electrical energy.
- **Pile drivers / hammers**: a heavy mass is raised (gaining PE), then
  released to convert that PE into KE, doing work on the pile or nail on
  impact.
- **Regenerative braking** (electric/hybrid cars): instead of wasting the
  car's KE as heat through friction brakes, motors run in reverse to
  convert KE back into stored electrical energy.
- **Human metabolism and exercise**: power output (watts) during exercise
  measures the rate at which the body converts chemical energy into
  mechanical work — the same work done faster means higher power and
  higher exertion.
- **Springs in pogo sticks, trampolines, and car suspensions**: elastic
  PE (½kx²) stores energy on compression and releases it as KE on
  rebound (or absorbs shock, in the case of suspensions).

## Study tips for the most commonly missed question types

- **"Does this force do work?"** — always check the angle between force
  and displacement first. If it's 90° (normal force on horizontal motion,
  centripetal force, carrying something at constant height while walking
  horizontally), the work is zero, no matter how large the force is.
- **"Net work" vs. "work by one force"** — the work-energy theorem only
  works with the *total* net work. If multiple forces act (e.g., gravity
  and friction), you must add up all the works (respecting sign) before
  setting the sum equal to ΔKE.
- **Reference height for PE** — always state which height you're calling
  h = 0 before plugging numbers in. It won't change your final answer for
  speed or energy transferred, but using two different references
  inconsistently within the same problem will.
- **"Where did the energy go?"** — whenever mechanical energy seems to
  disappear, look for friction, air resistance, or another
  non-conservative force; add an explicit loss term rather than assuming
  KE + PE stays constant.
- **KE and v²** — before calculating, ask "if speed doubles, does my
  answer for KE quadruple?" as a sanity check; if not, you may have
  written v instead of v².
