# Topic 4: Momentum

Momentum gives a second, often more powerful lens for analyzing interactions — especially collisions — where forces act over very short, hard-to-measure time intervals.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Momentum:** $p = mv$ (vector, same direction as velocity)
- **Impulse:** $J = F\Delta t = \Delta p$ — the impulse-momentum theorem links force applied over time to a change in momentum
- **Conservation of momentum:** in an isolated system (no external net force), total momentum before = total momentum after
- **Elastic vs. inelastic collisions:**
  - Elastic: both momentum *and* kinetic energy conserved (objects bounce apart)
  - Inelastic: momentum conserved, kinetic energy is *not* (some converts to heat/sound/deformation)
  - Perfectly inelastic: objects stick together and move with a common final velocity
- **Explosions** as the reverse case: objects start together (or at rest) and separate, with total momentum before = total momentum after (often zero)
- **Vector nature of momentum** in 2D collisions — must conserve momentum component-by-component (x and y separately)

## Learning Objectives (SWBAT)

1. Calculate momentum and impulse, and apply the impulse-momentum theorem to force/time problems (e.g., airbags, padding).
2. Set up and solve conservation-of-momentum equations for 1D collisions, correctly assigning signs by direction.
3. Distinguish elastic, inelastic, and perfectly inelastic collisions, and identify which quantities are/aren't conserved in each.
4. Solve perfectly inelastic collision problems (objects combine into one final velocity).
5. Explain why momentum is conserved in a collision even when kinetic energy is not (energy isn't destroyed — it converts to other forms).

## Common Pitfalls (why this topic gets missed)

- Forgetting momentum is a **vector** — objects moving in opposite directions have momenta with opposite signs, and these must be added algebraically, not just magnitudes summed.
- Confusing "momentum is always conserved in a collision" (true, for an isolated system) with "kinetic energy is always conserved in a collision" (only true for elastic collisions).
- In perfectly inelastic collisions, trying to keep the objects' velocities separate after they've combined — after sticking, there is only **one** final velocity for the combined mass.
- Applying impulse-momentum theorem with the wrong $\Delta t$ (confusing total trip time with contact/collision time).
- In explosion problems, forgetting that initial momentum can be zero (system starts at rest), which still constrains the final momenta of the pieces to be equal and opposite.

## Practice Problems

1. A 0.15 kg baseball traveling at 40 m/s is hit back at 50 m/s in the opposite direction. If the bat is in contact with the ball for 0.7 ms, find the average force exerted by the bat.
2. A 1200 kg car moving at 20 m/s rear-ends a stationary 1000 kg car, and they lock together. Find their common velocity immediately after the collision. Is kinetic energy conserved? Show the numbers.
3. Two ice skaters, 50 kg and 70 kg, push off from rest against each other. If the 50 kg skater moves away at 3 m/s, find the 70 kg skater's velocity.
4. A 2 kg cart moving at 3 m/s collides elastically with a stationary 2 kg cart. Predict (and explain using symmetry, not just plug-and-chug) what happens to each cart's velocity after.
5. A firework at rest explodes into two fragments of equal mass. Explain why they must fly apart in exactly opposite directions with equal speeds.

## Real-World Applications

- **Car safety design:** crumple zones and airbags increase $\Delta t$ during a collision, reducing the force for the same change in momentum (impulse-momentum theorem).
- **Rocket propulsion:** momentum conservation between expelled fuel and the rocket.
- **Sports:** follow-through in swinging a bat/racket increases contact time and thus force/energy transfer; boxing gloves increase impact time to reduce peak force.
- **Ballistic pendulum:** classic method for measuring a bullet's velocity using conservation of momentum (perfectly inelastic) then energy conservation.
- **Rocket/jet propulsion and Newton's cradle** as visual, intuitive momentum-transfer demonstrations.

Student work is assessed on:
- Correct treatment of momentum as a signed/vector quantity
- Correct setup of conservation of momentum equations (system before = system after)
- Correct classification of collision type and correct claim about what is/isn't conserved
- Correct use of the impulse-momentum theorem with the correct time interval
- Sanity-checking final velocities/directions against physical intuition
