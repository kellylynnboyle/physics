# Topic 5: Work & Energy

The energy approach is often the fastest way to solve problems that would be difficult with kinematics or forces alone — especially when forces vary or paths are curved.

## Key Concepts to Master

- **Work:** $W = F d \cos\theta$ — only the force component *parallel* to displacement does work; a force perpendicular to motion (e.g., normal force, centripetal force) does zero work.
- **Work-Energy Theorem:** $W_{net} = \Delta KE$ — the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy.
- **Kinetic energy:** $KE = \tfrac12 mv^2$
- **Gravitational potential energy:** $PE_g = mgh$ (relative to a chosen reference height)
- **Elastic potential energy:** $PE_s = \tfrac12 kx^2$ (springs, Hooke's Law $F = -kx$)
- **Conservation of mechanical energy:** $KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f$ when only conservative forces (gravity, springs) act — no friction/air resistance
- **Non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance):** remove mechanical energy from the system, converting it to heat: $KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f + W_{friction}$
- **Power:** $P = W/t = Fv$ — the *rate* of doing work/transferring energy

## Learning Objectives (SWBAT)

1. Calculate work done by a force, correctly accounting for the angle between force and displacement (including recognizing zero-work cases).
2. Apply the work-energy theorem to find a final speed or stopping distance without using kinematics equations directly.
3. Apply conservation of mechanical energy to solve problems involving height changes (ramps, pendulums, roller coasters) and springs.
4. Correctly account for energy "lost" to friction in non-ideal systems, and describe it as converted (not destroyed).
5. Calculate power and apply it to real-world rate-of-energy-transfer scenarios.
6. Choose energy methods over kinematics/force methods when appropriate (e.g., curved paths, varying forces) and justify the choice.

## Common Pitfalls (why this topic gets missed)

- Forgetting the $\cos\theta$ in the work formula — assuming any force acting during a displacement does positive work (e.g., carrying a bag horizontally does *zero* work against gravity, since the force of your arms is vertical while displacement is horizontal... more precisely, gravity does zero work since it's perpendicular to horizontal displacement).
- Believing potential energy has one "true" value rather than being relative to a chosen reference height — only *changes* in PE are physically meaningful.
- Forgetting that normal force and tension (when perpendicular to velocity, e.g., circular motion) do zero work, so they don't appear in the energy equation even though they appear in force analysis.
- Treating friction as if it can be handled with the same conservation equation as gravity/springs, without the extra $W_{friction}$ term.
- Confusing energy (a scalar, in Joules) with momentum (a vector, in kg·m/s) — a common error is trying to conserve energy as if it had direction/sign the way momentum does.
- Power vs. energy confusion — mixing up units (Watts vs. Joules) and what each measures (rate vs. total amount).

## Practice Problems

1. A 60 kg skier starts from rest at the top of a frictionless 40 m tall hill. Find her speed at the bottom using energy conservation, then check your answer using kinematics.
2. A 1000 kg roller coaster car is 30 m above the ground at the top of the first hill (from rest) and 5 m above the ground at the top of a second hill. Find its speed at the top of the second hill (ignore friction).
3. A spring with $k = 200\ \text{N/m}$ is compressed 0.3 m and used to launch a 0.5 kg block. Find the block's launch speed (assume the spring's PE fully converts to KE).
4. A 1500 kg car traveling at 25 m/s brakes to a stop over 60 m. Find the average friction force using the work-energy theorem.
5. A 70 kg person climbs a flight of stairs rising 4 m in 8 seconds. Find the power output.
6. A box is pushed 5 m across a floor by a 40 N force applied at 30° above horizontal, with a 10 N friction force opposing motion. Find the net work done on the box.

## Real-World Applications

- **Roller coaster design:** engineers use energy conservation (accounting for friction/air resistance losses) to ensure each subsequent hill is lower than the last.
- **Regenerative braking (electric/hybrid vehicles):** converts a car's kinetic energy back into stored electrical energy instead of losing it all to heat via friction brakes.
- **Hydroelectric dams:** gravitational PE of stored water converted to kinetic energy, then electrical energy.
- **Human metabolism and exercise science:** power output (Watts) measured in cycling and rowing performance.
- **Pile drivers and hammers:** using PE→KE conversion to deliver large impact forces.
