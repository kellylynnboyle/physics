# Unit 5: Work & Energy

**NGSS alignment:** HS-PS3-1 (create a computational model to calculate energy change), HS-PS3-2 (develop/use models to illustrate energy at the macroscopic scale), HS-PS3-3 (design/evaluate a device that converts energy from one form to another).

**Suggested pacing:** 2 weeks (10 class periods), including 1 lab day (energy conservation ramp/pendulum lab) and 1 review/assessment day. Serves as the capstone/synthesis unit for semester 1, tying together kinematics, forces, and momentum.

## 1. Key Concepts Students Must Master

| # | Concept | Why it matters |
|---|---------|-----------------|
| 1 | Work | `W = Fd cos θ`, where θ is the angle between the force and displacement. Work is a scalar (can be positive, negative, or zero). |
| 2 | When is work zero? | When force is perpendicular to displacement (θ = 90°), or when there is no displacement at all. |
| 3 | Kinetic energy | `KE = ½mv²`. Energy of motion; always positive (or zero). |
| 4 | Work-Energy Theorem | `W_net = ΔKE`. The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy. |
| 5 | Gravitational potential energy | `PE_grav = mgh`, measured relative to a chosen reference height (reference point is arbitrary — only changes in PE matter physically). |
| 6 | Elastic potential energy | `PE_spring = ½kx²`, where k is the spring constant and x is displacement from equilibrium. |
| 7 | Conservation of mechanical energy | In the absence of non-conservative forces (like friction), `KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f`. |
| 8 | Non-conservative forces | Friction and air resistance remove mechanical energy from a system, converting it to heat/sound — mechanical energy is NOT conserved when these act. |
| 9 | Power | `P = W/t` (rate of doing work); units of Watts. |

## 5. Practice Problems (progression: easy → hard)

1. A person pushes a 20 kg box 5 m across a floor with a constant 40 N horizontal force. How much work is done?
2. A 60 kg hiker climbs a hill, gaining 100 m of elevation. How much gravitational PE did they gain?
3. A 1000 kg roller coaster car is at the top of a 30 m hill, starting from rest. Ignoring friction, what is its speed at the bottom?
4. A spring with k = 200 N/m is compressed 0.15 m. How much elastic potential energy is stored?
5. **Multi-step:** A 2 kg block slides down a frictionless 5 m tall ramp, then slides across a rough horizontal surface (μ_k = 0.25) before stopping. How far does it slide on the rough surface before stopping? (Use energy methods, not kinematics.)
6. **Challenge:** A 500 kg elevator is lifted 20 m in 25 seconds at constant speed. Find the work done and the average power output of the motor.

## 6. Real-World Applications

- **Roller coasters:** designed almost entirely using conservation of energy — the first hill must be the tallest point (ignoring energy added by motors) because it converts PE into all the KE for the rest of the ride.
- **Hydroelectric dams:** gravitational PE of stored water converts to KE (moving water) then to electrical energy via turbines.
- **Regenerative braking (hybrid/electric cars):** converts a car's KE back into stored electrical energy instead of wasting it as heat through friction brakes.
- **Bouncing balls & pogo sticks:** elastic PE storage and release, with some energy lost to non-conservative forces (which is why bounces get smaller over time).
- **Human metabolism/exercise science:** power output (Watts) is used to quantify athletic performance, e.g., cycling power meters.

## 7. Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

- [ ] Calculate work done by a constant force, including cases where the force is at an angle to displacement.
- [ ] Apply the work-energy theorem to relate net work and change in kinetic energy.
- [ ] Calculate gravitational and elastic potential energy, and correctly explain that only changes in PE are physically meaningful.
- [ ] Apply conservation of mechanical energy to solve problems in frictionless systems.
- [ ] Correctly incorporate non-conservative forces (friction) into an energy conservation equation to determine energy "lost" to heat.
- [ ] Calculate power as the rate of energy transfer or work done.

## 8. Assessment Criteria

| Level | Criteria |
|-------|----------|
| **Exceeds (4)** | Solves multi-stage problems combining frictionless and frictional sections using energy methods; correctly quantifies energy "lost" to non-conservative forces. |
| **Meets (3)** | Applies conservation of mechanical energy correctly in frictionless scenarios; calculates work, KE, and PE accurately. |
| **Approaching (2)** | Understands energy conservation conceptually but makes errors incorporating friction, or miscalculates work when force and displacement aren't aligned. |
| **Beginning (1)** | Cannot set up an energy conservation equation; confuses work, force, and energy as interchangeable quantities. |

**Suggested evidence:** unit test (including an energy bar chart / energy transformation diagram question), ramp or pendulum lab report comparing predicted vs. measured energy loss to friction, quiz retake after reviewing `quiz/index.html` missed-concept report. This unit's assessment also serves as the semester 1 cumulative checkpoint, since it draws on kinematics, Newton's Laws, and momentum.
