# Unit 5 — Work & Energy — Self-Check Quiz

Built from the questions students miss most often on work & energy tests. Try each question yourself before revealing the answer.

**How to use:** Get 80%+ (8/10) before moving on to the semester review.

---

**1.** A person carries a 10 kg bag at constant height across a 20 m flat hallway. How much work does the person do on the bag?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Zero joules.** The force holding the bag up is vertical, but the displacement is horizontal — θ = 90°, and cos(90°) = 0, so W = Fd·cos(90°) = 0.

*Most-missed reason:* students assume "effort" always equals "work" in the physics sense — but physics work requires a force component in the direction of motion.
</details>

---

**2.** A 2 kg ball has 16 J of kinetic energy. What is its speed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

KE = ½mv² → 16 = ½(2)v² → 16 = v² → **v = 4 m/s**

*Most-missed reason:* forgetting to take the square root at the end, or forgetting the ½ when solving backward for v².
</details>

---

**3.** True or False: gravitational potential energy has one fixed, absolute value for any object.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**False.** PE = mgh is always measured relative to a chosen reference height (h = 0 point) — only *changes* in PE are physically meaningful, not the absolute number.

*Most-missed reason:* students treat PE like a fixed property of an object (like mass), not realizing it depends entirely on where you define "h = 0."
</details>

---

**4.** A net force does −30 J of work on a moving object. What happens to its kinetic energy?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

KE **decreases by 30 J** — by the work-energy theorem, W_net = ΔKE, so negative net work means the object is slowing down.

*Most-missed reason:* students think "negative work" is a nonsensical or purely mathematical result rather than recognizing it directly describes an object losing kinetic energy (e.g., due to friction or a decelerating force).
</details>

---

**5.** Two students lift identical boxes to the same height. Student A takes 2 seconds; Student B takes 10 seconds. Compare their work and their power.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Work is the same** for both (same force, same displacement — time doesn't appear in W = Fd·cosθ). **Power is different**: Student A has *more* power (P = W/t) because they did the same work in less time.

*Most-missed reason:* students think the faster student "did more work," conflating work (total energy transferred) with power (rate of energy transfer).
</details>

---

**6.** A 1 kg ball is dropped from 5 m. Using energy conservation (no air resistance), find its speed just before landing. (g = 9.8 m/s²)

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

PE_i = KE_f → mgh = ½mv² → gh = ½v² → v² = 2gh = 2(9.8)(5) = 98 → **v ≈ 9.9 m/s**

*Most-missed reason:* students try to bring kinematics equations back in unnecessarily, or forget mass cancels out of the energy conservation equation entirely (so it isn't even needed).
</details>

---

**7.** A roller coaster car has more speed at the bottom of a hill than partway down. Explain using energy conservation why this must be true (assume no friction).

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

As height (h) decreases going downhill, gravitational PE (= mgh) decreases. Since total mechanical energy (KE + PE) is conserved with no friction, that lost PE must convert directly into KE, so KE — and therefore speed — increases continuously as the car descends.

*Most-missed reason:* students describe the *result* (it speeds up) without connecting it explicitly to the energy conservation equation (KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f) as the reasoning.
</details>

---

**8.** A real roller coaster (with friction/air resistance) doesn't return to its original starting height on a later hill, even a lower one. Why not, in energy terms?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Friction and air resistance are **non-conservative forces** that convert some mechanical energy into heat and sound. Total energy is still conserved overall, but usable *mechanical* energy (KE + PE) decreases, so the car can't regain its original total mechanical energy on a later hill.

*Most-missed reason:* students say "energy is lost," which sounds like it violates conservation of energy — the more precise statement is that mechanical energy converts to non-mechanical forms (heat/sound), not that energy disappears.
</details>

---

**9.** A motor lifts a 200 kg elevator 10 m in 20 s at constant speed. Find the power output. (g = 9.8 m/s²)

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

W = Fd = mgh = (200)(9.8)(10) = 19,600 J
P = W/t = 19,600/20 = **980 W**

*Most-missed reason:* forgetting to calculate the force as weight (mg) first before computing work — students sometimes plug mass directly into W = Fd without converting to a force in newtons.
</details>

---

**10.** A spring with spring constant k = 200 N/m is compressed 0.3 m. How much elastic potential energy is stored?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

PE_elastic = ½kx² = ½(200)(0.3²) = ½(200)(0.09) = **9 J**

*Most-missed reason:* forgetting to square x before multiplying, or forgetting the ½ in the formula (both common with the elastic PE formula specifically since it looks similar to KE = ½mv²).
</details>
