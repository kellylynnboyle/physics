# Unit 5: Work & Energy — Cornell Notes

**NGSS alignment:** HS-PS3-1, HS-PS3-2, HS-PS3-3 (conservation of energy, energy transfer/transformation). CA Physics course model, Semester 1, Week 9–10 (unit closes Semester 1).

**Essential question:** How does energy transform from one form to another while the total stays constant?

---

## Cornell Notes

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Work** definition | W = Fd·cos(θ), where θ is the angle between the force and displacement direction. Units: joules (J) = N·m. |
| When is work **zero**, even if a force is applied? | When there's no displacement (nothing moves), OR when the force is perpendicular to the displacement (cos90° = 0) — e.g., carrying a bag horizontally doesn't do work against gravity, since gravity (down) is perpendicular to horizontal motion. |
| Can work be **negative**? | Yes — when the force has a component opposite to the displacement (θ > 90°), like friction opposing motion. Negative work removes energy from the object. |
| **Kinetic energy** | KE = ½mv². Energy of motion. Always positive (or zero); depends on v², not v. |
| **Gravitational potential energy** | PE_grav = mgh, measured relative to a chosen reference height (h = 0 point is your choice — only *changes* in PE matter physically). |
| **Elastic potential energy** (spring) | PE_spring = ½kx², where k is the spring constant and x is displacement from equilibrium. |
| **Work-Energy Theorem** | W_net = ΔKE. The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy — this connects forces (Unit 3) to energy. |
| **Conservation of Mechanical Energy** | In the absence of non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance), KE + PE = constant. Energy converts between forms but the total stays the same. |
| What happens to energy when friction is present? | Mechanical energy (KE + PE) is NOT conserved — some converts to thermal energy (heat). TOTAL energy (including thermal) is still conserved; only mechanical energy "appears" to decrease. |
| Common misconception: "energy is used up" | Energy is never destroyed — it's transformed or transferred (often to less useful forms like heat/sound). "Lost" energy in a real system usually means "converted to a form we're not tracking," not "destroyed." |
| **Power** | P = W/t = energy transferred per unit time. Units: watts (W) = J/s. Two machines can do the same work but at different power if they take different times. |
| Roller coaster / pendulum energy conversion | At the highest point: max PE, min KE (often ≈0 if momentarily at rest). At the lowest point: max KE, min PE. Total mechanical energy stays constant if friction/air resistance is ignored. |
| Why do heavier objects NOT "have more energy to reach the ground faster" in free fall? | They have more PE and more KE (both scale with m), but since a = F/m and mass cancels in kinematics, they still hit the ground at the same time/speed as lighter objects — energy scaling with mass doesn't affect free-fall speed. |

---

## Summary

Work (W = Fd·cosθ) is the mechanism by which energy transfers into or out of a system. Kinetic energy (motion) and potential energy (stored, due to position or configuration) trade off constantly, but as long as no non-conservative force like friction acts, their sum — total mechanical energy — stays constant. When friction is present, mechanical energy still "leaks" into thermal energy, but total energy (all forms combined) is always conserved.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Work (W) | Energy transferred by a force acting through a displacement |
| Kinetic energy (KE) | Energy of motion, ½mv² |
| Potential energy (PE) | Stored energy due to position (gravitational) or configuration (elastic) |
| Mechanical energy | KE + PE |
| Conservative force | A force (like gravity, spring force) for which work done doesn't depend on path, only start/end position |
| Non-conservative force | A force (like friction) that removes mechanical energy from a system, converting it to other forms |
| Power | Rate of energy transfer, W/t |

## Practice Problems

1. A 20 kg box is pushed 5 m across a floor by a 40 N horizontal force. How much work is done on the box?
2. A 0.2 kg ball is dropped from 10 m. Using energy conservation, find its speed just before hitting the ground (ignore air resistance).
3. A roller coaster car (500 kg) starts at rest at the top of a 30 m hill. Ignoring friction, find its speed at the bottom.
4. A 60 kg worker climbs a 4 m ladder in 8 seconds. Find the work done against gravity and the average power output.

**Worked example (#2):** Energy conservation: PE_initial = KE_final (starting from rest) → mgh = ½mv² → gh = ½v² → v = √(2gh) = √(2×9.8×10) = √196 = 14 m/s. Notice mass cancels out.

## Real-World Applications

- Roller coasters: engineered almost entirely around converting PE ↔ KE, with the first hill always the tallest
- Hydroelectric dams: gravitational PE of stored water converts to KE (turbines) then electrical energy
- Car crumple zones and braking: kinetic energy converts to heat/sound/deformation via negative work from friction
- Pole vaulting / archery: elastic PE (bent pole, drawn bowstring) converts to KE

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:
- Calculate work done by a force, including cases with angles and zero/negative work (**DOK 2**)
- Apply the work-energy theorem to relate net work and change in kinetic energy (**DOK 3**)
- Apply conservation of mechanical energy to solve problems involving height, speed, and springs (**DOK 3**)
- Explain energy transformations in real systems, including where energy goes when friction is present (**DOK 3**)
- Calculate power and distinguish it from work/energy (**DOK 2**)
