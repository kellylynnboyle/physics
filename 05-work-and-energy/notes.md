# Unit 5 — Work & Energy — Cornell Notes

**Essential question:** How can tracking energy let us solve motion problems without ever knowing the forces or times involved?

**NGSS connection:** HS-PS3-1 (compute energy changes in a system), HS-PS3-2 (model energy as kinetic + potential at the macroscopic scale), HS-PS3-3 (design a device that converts energy from one form to another).

---

## Cue column | Notes

| Cue | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is **work**, physics-definition? | W = Fd·cos(θ), where θ is the angle between the force and the displacement. Work is a **scalar** (no direction), measured in joules (J). Only the force component *parallel* to the displacement does work. |
| Common trap: "if I'm holding something up, am I doing work on it?" | **No mechanical work is done on the object** if it isn't moving (d = 0), or if you're moving it horizontally while your force is purely vertical (θ = 90°, cos90° = 0) — e.g., carrying a bag while walking at constant height does zero work on the bag. |
| What is **kinetic energy (KE)**? | KE = ½mv². Energy of motion. Always positive (depends on v², not v). |
| What is **gravitational potential energy (PE_grav)**? | PE = mgh, where h is height above a chosen reference point. PE is relative — only *changes* in PE have physical meaning, not the absolute number. |
| What is **elastic potential energy**? | PE_elastic = ½kx², stored in a stretched/compressed spring, where k is the spring constant and x is displacement from natural length. |
| **Work-energy theorem** | The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: W_net = ΔKE = KE_f − KE_i. This lets you solve for final speed without ever finding acceleration or time. |
| **Conservation of mechanical energy** | In the absence of friction/air resistance (no non-conservative forces), total mechanical energy is conserved: KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f. This is often faster than kinematics for problems involving height and speed. |
| What happens to energy when friction/air resistance is present? | Mechanical energy is *not* conserved — some energy converts to heat/sound (non-mechanical forms). Total energy (including heat) is still conserved (Law of Conservation of Energy), but usable mechanical energy decreases. |
| What is **power**? | Rate of doing work: P = W/t = Fv (for constant force in the direction of motion). Units: watts (W) = J/s. Power tells you *how fast* energy is transferred, not how much total energy. |
| Common trap: confusing **work** and **power** | Two people can do the same amount of work (same energy transferred) but different power if one takes longer — power is about rate, work is about total amount. |
| Common trap: negative work | Work can be negative when the force opposes displacement (θ > 90°), e.g., friction does negative work on a sliding object, removing KE from it. |

## Key vocabulary
work (W) · kinetic energy (KE) · potential energy (PE) · gravitational PE · elastic PE · mechanical energy · work-energy theorem · conservation of mechanical energy · power (P) · non-conservative force (friction)

## Key formulas
- W = Fd·cos(θ)
- KE = ½mv²
- PE_grav = mgh
- PE_elastic = ½kx²
- Work-energy theorem: W_net = ΔKE
- Conservation (no friction): KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f
- Power: P = W/t = Fv

---

## Summary (write in your own words after class)
Work transfers energy into or out of a system; the work-energy theorem lets net work be traded directly for a change in kinetic energy. When only conservative forces (like gravity) act, total mechanical energy (KE + PE) stays constant, giving a shortcut to solve motion problems using energy bookkeeping instead of forces and time. Power measures how fast that energy transfer happens.

---

## Real-world applications
- Roller coasters (continuous KE ↔ PE conversion, no engine needed after the lift hill)
- Pendulum clocks and playground swings
- Hydroelectric dams (gravitational PE of water → KE → electrical energy)
- Car engines rated by horsepower (power output, not just total energy)
- Bouncing ball height loss (mechanical energy lost to heat/sound on each bounce)

## Practice problems (by difficulty)
**Level 1 — plug and chug**
1. A 10 N force pushes a box 5 m in the direction of the force. Find the work done.
2. A 2 kg ball moves at 6 m/s. Find its kinetic energy.

**Level 2 — work-energy theorem / conservation**
3. A 3 kg object starts at rest and a net force does 24 J of work on it. Find its final speed.
4. A 1.5 kg ball is dropped from 10 m. Use conservation of energy to find its speed just before hitting the ground (ignore air resistance).

**Level 3 — power / mixed / conceptual**
5. A 60 kg student climbs a 4 m staircase in 8 s. Find the power output.
6. A roller coaster car (500 kg) starts at rest at the top of a 30 m hill. Ignoring friction, find its speed at the bottom, then explain what would change if friction were included (qualitatively, no calculation needed).
