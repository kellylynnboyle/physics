| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is momentum, and is it a vector or scalar? | p = mv. A vector — same direction as velocity. Units: kg·m/s. A heavy, slow object and a light, fast object can have the same momentum. |
| What is impulse, and how does it relate to momentum? | J = FΔt (impulse), and by the impulse-momentum theorem, J = Δp. A net force applied over time changes momentum by exactly that impulse. Impulse is also the area under a force-time graph, same idea as area under a velocity-time graph giving displacement. |
| Why do airbags and padding reduce injury? | They increase the collision time Δt. For the SAME Δp (same change in momentum, e.g. stopping the same person from the same speed), a longer Δt means a smaller average force F = Δp/Δt. Longer time ≠ more force — it's the opposite. |
| What is conserved in an isolated system? | Total momentum, always — p_before(total) = p_after(total) — for BOTH elastic and inelastic collisions, as long as there's no net external force. This is separate from whether kinetic energy is conserved. |
| Elastic vs. inelastic — what's the real difference? | Elastic: momentum AND kinetic energy both conserved (objects bounce apart, no energy lost). Inelastic: momentum conserved, but kinetic energy is NOT — some converts to heat/sound/deformation. Don't use KE-conservation equations for an inelastic collision. |
| Perfectly inelastic collision equation? | Objects stick together: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v_f. The combined mass goes on the right side — a common mistake is forgetting to add the masses together. |
| Explosions/recoil — momentum or velocity equal and opposite? | Momentum, not necessarily velocity. If the system starts at rest, p_total = 0 before, so after, the two pieces' momenta must be equal and opposite (m₁v₁ = −m₂v₂). Only if the two masses are equal are the velocities also equal and opposite. |
| Why does sign matter so much in collision problems? | Momentum is a vector. In a head-on 1D collision, one object's velocity must be entered as NEGATIVE (opposite direction) relative to the other, not just added as magnitudes. Forgetting the sign is the #1 momentum error. |
| Does a resting object contribute to "before" momentum? | Yes — as zero, not as "nothing to include." A stationary target has p = 0, but it's still a term in the sum: p_total(before) = p_moving-object + 0. Skipping it is easy to do but wrong bookkeeping, not a shortcut. |
| Newton's Third Law connection: are the two objects' momentum changes the same? | During a collision, the two objects exert equal and opposite forces on each other for the same time (Newton's Third Law), so their impulses — and therefore their changes in momentum, Δp — are equal and opposite. Their changes in velocity, Δv, are NOT necessarily equal unless their masses are equal (Δv = Δp/m). |

## Summary (write in your own words after studying)

Momentum p = mv is a vector quantity conserved for any isolated system —
total momentum before an interaction equals total momentum after, whether
the collision is elastic (kinetic energy also conserved) or inelastic
(kinetic energy is not). Perfectly inelastic collisions combine both
objects' mass into one final velocity. Impulse J = FΔt = Δp connects
force and time to momentum change, explaining why extending collision
time lowers force. Explosions conserve momentum the same way collisions
do, just run in reverse, with equal-and-opposite momenta (not
necessarily velocities) for the resulting pieces. Careful sign
conventions for direction are essential throughout.
