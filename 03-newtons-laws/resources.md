## Practice problems

1. A 5.0 kg box sits on a frictionless table. A 15 N horizontal force is applied. Find the acceleration.
2. A 60 kg skater pushes off a wall with 200 N of force. What force does the wall exert on the skater, and in what direction? (Apply Newton's 3rd law.)
3. A 1000 kg elevator accelerates upward at 2.0 m/s². Find the tension in the supporting cable. Then find it again for the elevator accelerating *downward* at 2.0 m/s².
4. A 10 kg crate rests on a ramp inclined at 25°. The coefficient of static friction is 0.4. Does the crate slide? Show your work using the incline force-component method.
5. Two blocks (3 kg and 5 kg) are connected by a string over a frictionless pulley (a classic Atwood machine). Find the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string.
6. A 1200 kg car experiences 3500 N of forward force from its engine and 800 N of friction/air resistance opposing motion. Find its acceleration.
7. **Conceptual:** A book rests on a table. Identify the two forces acting on the book and explain why they are *not* a Newton's-3rd-law pair (hint: they act on the *same* object).
8. **Conceptual:** Explain, using Newton's 1st law, why passengers lurch forward when a car brakes suddenly, even though no force pushed them forward.

## Real-world applications

- **Seatbelts and airbags** — direct application of Newton's 1st law (inertia): your body keeps moving forward when the car stops unless a force (the belt) acts on you.
- **Rocket propulsion** — Newton's 3rd law: hot gas is pushed out the back of the rocket, and the gas pushes the rocket forward with equal and opposite force.
- **Vehicle traction and anti-lock brakes (ABS)** — engineered around the difference between static and kinetic friction; ABS prevents wheels from skidding (switching to the weaker kinetic friction) so tires keep the stronger static friction grip.
- **Elevator design** — cable tension calculations (practice problem 3) are literally how engineers size elevator cables for acceleration/deceleration phases, not just holding still.
- **Ramp and wheelchair-accessibility design** — incline angle vs. required pushing force trades off using the same `mg sinθ` / `mg cosθ` decomposition from this unit.

## Suggested student task

Choose a real or hypothetical vehicle collision or sports collision (e.g.,
a tackle in football). Identify the Newton's-3rd-law force pair involved
and explain in 2–3 sentences why the forces are equal and opposite but the
*effects* on each object can still look very different (hint: think about
Newton's 2nd law and each object's mass).
