# Unit 3: Newton's Laws — Cornell Notes

**NGSS alignment:** HS-PS2-1 (motion and force relationships, F=ma). CA Physics course model, Semester 1, Week 4–6.

**Essential question:** What determines whether and how an object's motion changes?

---

## Cornell Notes

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Newton's First Law** (Law of Inertia) | An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net (unbalanced) external force. "No net force" does NOT mean "no forces" — it means the forces balance. |
| What is **inertia**? | The tendency of an object to resist a change in its motion. Inertia depends only on mass — more mass = more inertia, not related to weight/gravity specifically. |
| **Newton's Second Law** | F_net = ma. The net (vector sum) of all forces on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Acceleration is in the same direction as the *net* force, not necessarily any single applied force. |
| Common F=ma trap | Students plug in a single applied force instead of the *net* force. Always draw a free-body diagram and sum forces first. |
| **Newton's Third Law** | For every action force, there is a reaction force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, acting on a *different* object. Action-reaction pairs never act on the same object and therefore never cancel each other out. |
| Why don't action-reaction pairs cancel? | Because they act on two different objects. Forces only cancel when they act on the SAME object and sum to zero — that's Newton's First Law territory, not Third Law. |
| **Mass vs. weight** | Mass (kg) = amount of matter, constant everywhere. Weight (N) = force of gravity on that mass, W = mg, changes with location (e.g., the Moon). |
| **Free-body diagrams (FBDs)** | Show ONLY the forces acting ON the object of interest (not forces it exerts on other things). Common forces: gravity (weight, mg, down), normal force (N, perpendicular to surface), friction (f, opposes relative motion/tendency to slide), applied force, tension. |
| **Normal force** on a flat surface, no other vertical forces | N = mg (equal and opposite to weight so vertical forces balance) — but this changes if there's an additional vertical force (e.g., pulling up at an angle, or on an incline). |
| **Static vs. kinetic friction** | Static friction acts on objects that are not sliding (opposes the tendency to move), up to a maximum f_s,max = μₛN. Kinetic friction acts once sliding starts: f_k = μₖN. Usually μₛ > μₖ, meaning it takes more force to start motion than to keep it going. |
| **Equilibrium** | Net force = 0. This includes both objects at rest AND objects moving at constant velocity — both have zero acceleration, so both are in equilibrium by the first law. |
| Object on an **incline** | Weight is resolved into components parallel (mg·sinθ, drives sliding) and perpendicular (mg·cosθ, balanced by normal force) to the incline surface. |
| Heavier objects fall at the **same rate** as lighter ones (no air resistance) — why doesn't F=ma contradict this? | F_gravity = mg, and a = F/m = mg/m = g. The mass cancels out, so acceleration due to gravity alone is independent of mass. |

---

## Summary

Newton's three laws form one connected idea: objects resist changes to their motion (1st law) unless a net force acts (2nd law: F=ma), and forces always come in equal-and-opposite pairs acting on different objects (3rd law). Every force problem starts with a free-body diagram, isolating one object and identifying every force acting *on it*.

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Net force | The vector sum of all forces acting on an object |
| Free-body diagram | A diagram showing all forces acting on a single isolated object |
| Normal force | The perpendicular contact force a surface exerts on an object |
| Friction | A force opposing relative sliding (or tendency to slide) between surfaces |
| Tension | Pulling force transmitted through a string, rope, or cable |
| Equilibrium | State of zero net force (zero acceleration) |

## Practice Problems

1. A 5.0 kg box sits on a frictionless table. A 15 N horizontal force is applied. Find the acceleration.
2. A 60 kg skater pushes off a wall with 200 N of force. What force does the wall exert on the skater? What is the skater's acceleration?
3. A 10 kg crate rests on a ramp inclined at 20°. Find the components of gravity parallel and perpendicular to the ramp (use g = 9.8 m/s²).
4. Two boxes (3 kg and 7 kg) are connected by a string and pulled by a 20 N horizontal force across a frictionless floor. Find the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string.

**Worked example (#1):** F_net = ma → 15 = 5.0 × a → a = 3.0 m/s².

## Real-World Applications

- Seatbelts and airbags: Newton's First Law (your body keeps moving at crash speed unless a force stops it)
- Rocket propulsion: Third Law (expelled exhaust gas pushes the rocket forward)
- Car traction and ABS brakes: static vs. kinetic friction
- Elevator "weightless" feeling during acceleration: apparent weight changes with net force

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:
- State and apply Newton's three laws to explain and predict motion (**DOK 2**)
- Draw accurate free-body diagrams for objects in varied scenarios (flat surface, incline, connected objects) (**DOK 3**)
- Calculate net force, acceleration, or mass using F = ma given a free-body diagram (**DOK 3**)
- Correctly identify action-reaction pairs and explain why they don't cancel (**DOK 3**)
- Distinguish mass from weight and static from kinetic friction (**DOK 2**)

## Assessment Criteria

| Level | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Exceeds | Solves multi-object/incline/friction combined problems; correctly explains conceptual "trick" questions (action-reaction, equilibrium at constant velocity) |
| Meets | Draws correct FBDs and applies F=ma correctly for single-object problems on flat surfaces and inclines |
| Approaching | Confuses net force with individual applied forces; incomplete or inaccurate FBDs |
| Beginning | Cannot identify forces acting on an object or apply F=ma |
