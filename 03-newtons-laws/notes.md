# Unit 3 — Newton's Laws — Cornell Notes

**Essential question:** What is the relationship between force, mass, and acceleration — and why do objects move the way they do?

**NGSS connection:** HS-PS2-1 — analyze data to support the claim that Newton's second law describes the mathematical relationship among net force, mass, and acceleration.

---

## Cue column | Notes

| Cue | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Newton's First Law** (Law of Inertia) | An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, *unless* acted on by a net (unbalanced) force. Inertia = an object's resistance to a change in motion; depends only on mass. |
| **Newton's Second Law** | Net force = mass × acceleration: ΣF = ma. Acceleration is directly proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass. Acceleration points in the same direction as the *net* force. |
| **Newton's Third Law** | For every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force, acting on the *other* object. Action-reaction pairs act on **different objects** — they never cancel each other out on the same object. |
| What is a **free-body diagram (FBD)**? | A diagram showing *only* the forces acting **on one object**, drawn as arrows from the object's center, with length roughly proportional to magnitude. Never include the object's own weight-on-the-ground reaction or forces it exerts on other things. |
| **Mass vs. weight** | Mass (kg) = amount of matter, doesn't change with location. Weight (N) = force of gravity on an object = mg, changes with local gravitational field (weighs less on the Moon, same mass). |
| **Normal force (N)** | The support force perpendicular to a surface. On a flat surface with no other vertical forces, N = mg. On an incline or with an applied vertical force, N ≠ mg — you must solve for it from the FBD. |
| **Friction** | Opposes relative motion (or attempted motion) between surfaces. Kinetic friction: f_k = μ_k·N (object already sliding). Static friction: f_s ≤ μ_s·N (prevents motion from starting; can take any value up to its max). |
| **Tension** | Pulling force transmitted through a string/rope/cable, directed along the rope, away from the object. In an ideal (massless, frictionless-pulley) system, tension is the same throughout a single rope. |
| What does "net force = 0" actually mean? | The object is in **equilibrium** — either at rest or moving at constant velocity (Newton's 1st Law). It does *not* mean no forces are acting, just that they balance. |
| Common trap: "heavier objects fall faster" | False in the absence of air resistance — all objects near Earth's surface accelerate at g = 9.8 m/s² regardless of mass, because F = mg and a = F/m = mg/m = g (mass cancels). |
| Common trap: confusing an action-reaction pair with balanced forces on one object | A book resting on a table: the table pushes up on the book (normal force) and gravity pulls the book down (weight) — these balance **but are not a 3rd-law pair** (they act on the same object and are different force types). The true 3rd-law pair to "table pushes up on book" is "book pushes down on table." |

## Key vocabulary
inertia · net force · equilibrium · free-body diagram (FBD) · normal force (N) · friction (static/kinetic) · coefficient of friction (μ) · tension · action-reaction pair · weight vs. mass

## Key formulas
- ΣF = ma (Newton's 2nd Law)
- Weight: W = mg
- Kinetic friction: f_k = μ_k N
- Static friction (max): f_s(max) = μ_s N
- On an incline (frictionless): a = g sin(θ), N = mg cos(θ)

## Summary (write in your own words after class)
Newton's three laws connect force, mass, and motion: objects resist changes in motion (1st law) in proportion to their mass (2nd law: F = ma), and every force has an equal-and-opposite partner acting on a different object (3rd law). Free-body diagrams are the essential tool for turning a real scenario into a solvable F = ma equation.

## Real-world applications
- Seatbelts and airbags (inertia in a collision)
- Rocket propulsion (3rd law: exhaust pushed down, rocket pushed up)
- Car tires and braking distance (friction coefficients)
- Elevator "weightlessness" feeling during acceleration
- Rock climbing anchors and tension in ropes

## Practice problems (by difficulty)
**Level 1 — plug and chug**
1. A 5 kg object experiences a net force of 20 N. Find its acceleration.
2. A 1000 kg car needs to accelerate at 3 m/s². What net force is required?

**Level 2 — multi-force FBD**
3. A 10 kg box sits on a table. A person pushes horizontally with 15 N; the coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.2. Find the box's acceleration.
4. A 2 kg block hangs from a rope. Find the tension in the rope if the block is (a) at rest, (b) accelerating upward at 2 m/s².

**Level 3 — inclines / systems / conceptual**
5. A 4 kg block slides down a frictionless 30° incline. Find its acceleration and the normal force.
6. Explain, using Newton's 3rd law, why you can walk forward: what pushes on what?

## Learning objectives
- I can state and apply Newton's first, second, and third laws to real scenarios.
- I can draw a correct free-body diagram for an object in equilibrium or accelerating.
- I can calculate net force, acceleration, and mass using F = ma.
- I can distinguish mass from weight and explain the role of friction and normal force.
