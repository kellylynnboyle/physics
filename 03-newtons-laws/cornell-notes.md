## Topic: Newton's First Law

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| Statement | An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force. |
| What is "inertia"? | The tendency of an object to resist a change in its velocity. More mass = more inertia. |
| Does "no net force" mean "no forces at all"? | No — it means the forces are balanced (equilibrium). A book on a table has gravity and a normal force acting on it, but they cancel, so it stays at rest. |

## Topic: Newton's Second Law

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| Equation | ΣF = ma (net force, not any single force) |
| Does a constant force always mean constant velocity? | No — a constant *net* force produces a constant *acceleration*, not constant velocity. |
| Why "net" force matters | If multiple forces act on an object, you must add them as vectors first; only the resultant (net) force determines acceleration. |
| Does more force always mean an object needs to keep moving? | No — force is required to *change* velocity (accelerate), not to *maintain* it. An object moving at constant velocity has zero net force acting on it. |

## Topic: Newton's Third Law

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| Statement | For every action force on object A from object B, there is an equal and opposite reaction force on object B from object A. |
| Do action-reaction pairs cancel out? | No — they act on **different objects**, so they never cancel. (Forces that cancel to give equilibrium act on the *same* object.) |
| Example | You push on a wall (action); the wall pushes back on you (reaction) — equal in size, opposite in direction, but one acts on the wall and one acts on you. |

## Topic: Free Body Diagrams & Common Forces

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What goes in a free body diagram? | Every force acting **on** the object of interest — never forces the object exerts on something else. |
| Weight | F_g = mg, always points toward the center of the Earth (down). |
| Normal force | Perpendicular to the contact surface; adjusts to prevent objects from passing through each other — not always equal to weight (e.g., on an incline, or with an additional vertical force). |
| Static vs. kinetic friction | Static friction resists the start of sliding, up to a maximum (f_s ≤ μ_s F_N); kinetic friction acts once sliding starts (f_k = μ_k F_N), and is usually smaller than max static friction. |
