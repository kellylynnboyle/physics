## 1. Forces and free-body diagrams

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| **What is a force?** | A push or pull on an object caused by an **interaction with another object**. It is a vector, measured in newtons (N). If you cannot name the object doing the pushing or pulling, the force is not real. |
| **Which forces might act on an object?** | **F_grav** (weight; Earth pulls down), **F_norm** (a surface pushes perpendicular to itself), **F_frict** (a surface resists sliding, parallel to the surface), **F_tens** (a rope pulls), **F_app** (a person or machine pushes or pulls), **F_air** (air resistance, opposite the motion). |
| **How do I draw a free-body diagram?** | (1) Draw the one object as a dot or box. (2) Draw an arrow from it for each force **on** that object. (3) Label each arrow by type. (4) Make longer arrows for bigger forces. Do not draw velocity, and do not draw forces the object exerts on other things. |
| **What forces act on a ball after it leaves the thrower's hand?** | Only **gravity** (plus air resistance if it is not ignored). The hand's force ends when contact ends. The ball keeps moving because of inertia, not because of a "force of the throw". |
| **What is net force?** | The vector sum of all forces on the object. Same direction: add. Opposite directions: subtract. 30 N right and 10 N left → F_net = 20 N right. |

> **IXL practice (goal: SmartScore 80+):** Physics **F.1** Newton's laws of motion. For free-body diagrams use The Physics Classroom Concept Builders (no IXL match).

## 2. Newton's first law (inertia)

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| **What does Newton's first law say?** | An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving at constant velocity, **unless a net force acts on it**. |
| **What is inertia?** | An object's resistance to changes in its motion. More mass = more inertia. Inertia is a property, not a force. |
| **If an object moves at constant velocity, what is the net force on it?** | **Zero.** The forces are balanced. Forces are needed to change motion, not to keep it going. |
| **You push a box at constant velocity with 40 N. How big is friction?** | Constant velocity → F_net = 0 → friction = **40 N**, exactly balancing the push. A push bigger than friction would make the box speed up. |

> **IXL practice:** Physics **F.2** Newton's first law of motion

## 3. Newton's second law

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| **What does Newton's second law say?** | **a = F_net / m** (or F_net = ma). Acceleration is in the direction of the net force, grows with the net force, and shrinks with more mass. |
| **What happens to acceleration if the net force doubles? If the mass doubles?** | Force doubles → acceleration doubles. Mass doubles → acceleration halves. Both double → acceleration unchanged. |
| **What steps solve a second-law problem?** | (1) Draw the free-body diagram. (2) Find F_net in the direction of motion. (3) Use a = F_net / m. (4) Check units and direction. |
| **A 5.0 kg box is pushed with 30 N against 10 N of friction. What is its acceleration?** | Object: the box. Horizontal forces: 30 N forward, 10 N backward. F_net = 30 − 10 = 20 N.<br>a = 20 ÷ 5.0 = **4.0 m/s²** forward. (Vertical forces balance.) |
| **What does a scale read for a 70 kg person in an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s²?** | Forces on the person: F_norm up (the scale reading), F_grav = mg down.<br>F_norm − mg = ma → F_norm = m(g + a) = 70 × 11.8 = **826 N** (more than the 686 N weight).<br>At constant speed, up or down, a = 0 and the scale reads the true weight. |

> **IXL practice:** Physics **F.3** Newton's second law of motion

## 4. Mass and weight

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| **What is the difference between mass and weight?** | **Mass** is the amount of matter, in kg; it is the same everywhere. **Weight** is the force of gravity on that mass, in N; it depends on location. |
| **How do I calculate weight?** | F_grav = mg, with g = 9.8 N/kg on Earth.<br>Worked: 60 kg person → (60)(9.8) = **588 N** on Earth; on the Moon (g = 1.6) → **96 N**. The mass is 60 kg in both places. |
| **Which object exerts the normal force, and when does it equal the weight?** | The **surface** the object touches. It equals the weight only when the surface is level, nothing else pushes or pulls vertically, and there is no vertical acceleration. |

> **IXL practice:** Physics **F.3** Newton's second law of motion (weight is the second law applied to gravity) · extension: **I.4** Newton's law of universal gravitation

## 5. Newton's third law

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| **What does Newton's third law say?** | When object A pushes on object B, B pushes back on A with a force **equal in size and opposite in direction**, at the same time. |
| **How do I name the partner of a force?** | Swap the two objects: "Earth pulls book down" ↔ "book pulls Earth up". "Foot pushes ground backward" ↔ "ground pushes foot forward". |
| **Why don't the two forces in a third-law pair cancel?** | They act on **different objects**. Only forces on the same object can cancel. |
| **Is the normal force the third-law partner of weight?** | **No.** Both act on the same object (the book), and they are different types of force. Weight's partner is the book pulling up on Earth. |
| **A truck hits a small car. Which one feels the bigger force?** | **Neither.** The forces are equal. The car has the bigger acceleration (and damage) because its mass is smaller: a = F/m. |

> **IXL practice:** Physics **F.1** Newton's laws of motion

## 6. Friction

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| **What is the difference between static and kinetic friction?** | **Static friction** acts on surfaces that are not sliding; it matches the push up to a maximum. **Kinetic friction** acts on sliding surfaces and is usually smaller than the static maximum. |
| **How do I calculate friction?** | F_frict = μ F_norm, where μ depends on the two surfaces. |
| **A 10 kg crate is pulled with 50 N across a floor with μ = 0.30. What is its acceleration?** | F_norm = mg = (10)(9.8) = 98 N. F_frict = (0.30)(98) = 29.4 N.<br>F_net = 50 − 29.4 = 20.6 N. a = 20.6 ÷ 10 = **2.1 m/s²**. |

## 7. Labs and measurement

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| **How does a cart lab show that a = F_net / m?** | Part A: keep the total mass constant, change the pulling force, measure acceleration. The a vs. F graph is a straight line through the origin with slope 1/m. Part B: keep the force constant, change the mass. Acceleration falls as mass rises; a vs. 1/m is a straight line. |
| **What are the variables in part A?** | Independent: net force. Dependent: acceleration. Held constant: total mass of the system. |

## Memory tricks

- **First law = law of inertia. Second law = F_net = ma. Third law = force pairs.**
- **"A on B, B on A"** names any third-law pair.
- **Constant velocity means F_net = 0**, the same as at rest.
- **Mass is matter (kg); weight is a force (N).**
- **Draw the free-body diagram before any equation.**

## Summary

Forces come from interactions between objects, and a free-body diagram shows every force on one object. If those forces balance, the velocity stays constant (first law). If they do not, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force with a = F_net / m (second law). Weight is the force of gravity, mg, and is different from mass. Every force has an equal and opposite partner acting on the other object in the interaction (third law), so the pair never cancels.
