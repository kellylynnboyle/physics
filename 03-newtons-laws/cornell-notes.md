# Cornell Notes: Newton's Laws

Name: ______________________  Period: ______  Date: ____________

**Learning targets:** I can draw a free-body diagram. I can use Newton's three laws to explain and predict motion.

**Essential question:** What does a force do to an object's motion, and what happens when there is no net force?

**How to use these notes:** cover the Notes column, answer each cue aloud, then check.

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| **1. Forces and free-body diagrams** | |
| What is a force? | A push or pull that comes from an **interaction between two objects**. Unit: newton (N). 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². A force is not something an object carries with it. |
| Which forces show up most often, and which object exerts each? | **F_grav** (weight): Earth pulls down. **F_norm**: a surface pushes perpendicular to itself. **F_tens**: a rope pulls along its length. **F_frict**: a surface pushes parallel to itself, against sliding. **F_app**: a person or object pushes or pulls. **F_air**: air pushes against motion. |
| How do you draw a free-body diagram? | 1. Pick **one** object and draw it as a dot or box.<br>2. Draw an arrow for each force **on** that object, starting at the object.<br>3. Label each arrow and make longer arrows for larger forces.<br>4. Do not draw forces the object exerts on other things, and do not draw velocity or "force of motion." |
| What is net force? | The vector sum of all forces on one object. Forces in the same direction add; opposite forces subtract. |
| **2. First law (inertia)** | |
| What does Newton's first law say? | An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving at **constant velocity**, unless a net force acts on it. |
| How much net force is needed to keep an object moving at constant velocity? | **Zero.** If you push a crate at constant velocity with 50 N, friction must be 50 N the other way. Forces are balanced. |
| What is inertia, and what measures it? | The tendency of an object to resist changes in its motion. **Mass** measures it. Inertia is not a force. |
| **3. Second law** | |
| What does Newton's second law say? | **a = F_net ÷ m**, or F_net = ma. Acceleration is in the direction of the net force, grows with net force, and shrinks with mass. |
| A 4 kg box is pushed with 20 N while friction is 8 N. What is its acceleration? | Object: the box. Horizontal forces: 20 N forward, 8 N backward.<br>F_net = 20 − 8 = 12 N. a = 12 N ÷ 4 kg = **3 m/s²** forward.<br>Sense check: less than 20 ÷ 4 = 5 m/s², because friction opposes the push. |
| What is the difference between mass and weight? | **Mass** (kg) is the amount of matter and does not change with location. **Weight** (N) is the force of gravity, F_grav = mg. On the Moon your mass is the same and your weight is about one sixth. |
| What does a scale read in an elevator? | The normal force. Constant speed (up or down): N = mg. Accelerating up: N = m(g + a), reads more. Accelerating down: N = m(g − a), reads less. |
| **4. Third law** | |
| What does Newton's third law say? | When object A pushes on object B, B pushes on A with a force **equal in size and opposite in direction**. Both forces are the same type and exist at the same time. |
| A truck hits a small car. Which one feels the larger force? | **Neither.** The forces are equal. The car has a larger *acceleration* because its mass is smaller (a = F ÷ m). |
| Why don't third-law pairs cancel? | They act on **different objects**. Only forces on the same object can cancel. |
| What is the third-law partner of Earth's pull on a book? | The **book's gravitational pull on Earth**. It is not the normal force from the table: that force is a different type and acts on the same object. |
| **5. Friction** | |
| How do you calculate friction? | f = μN. **Static** friction prevents sliding and adjusts up to a maximum of μ_s N. **Kinetic** friction acts during sliding, f_k = μ_k N. Usually μ_k < μ_s. |
| **6. Labs and measurement** | |
| In the cart and hanging-mass lab, what do the graphs show? | Acceleration vs. net force (constant mass) is a straight line through the origin: a ∝ F. Acceleration vs. mass (constant force) is a curve: a ∝ 1/m. Independent variable: force or mass. Dependent variable: acceleration. |

**Memory tricks**

- First law: "an object keeps doing what it is doing."
- Third-law pairs: say "A on B, B on A." If you cannot swap the two nouns, it is not a pair.
- Free-body diagrams: one object, only forces *on* it.

**Summary**

Forces are interactions between two objects. If the forces on an object balance, its velocity stays the same, whether that velocity is zero or not. If they do not balance, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force with a = F_net ÷ m. Every force has an equal and opposite partner that acts on the other object in the interaction, which is why the pair never cancels.
