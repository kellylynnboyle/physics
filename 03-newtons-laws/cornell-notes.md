# Topic 3: Newton's Laws — Cornell Notes

## Learning Objectives
- State all three of Newton's Laws in words and give a real example of each.
- Draw a correct free-body diagram for an object.
- Apply `ΣF = ma` to solve for unknown forces, masses, or accelerations.
- Distinguish mass from weight.
- Identify Newton's third-law force pairs correctly.
- Differentiate static and kinetic friction.

---

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is **Newton's First Law**? | An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force. Also called the **law of inertia**. Motion does not require a continuing force — only a *change* in motion does. |
| What is **Newton's Second Law**? | `ΣF = ma` — the net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Acceleration is proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass. Always use the **net** (total) force, not any single force. |
| What is **Newton's Third Law**? | For every action force, there is a reaction force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. Critically: the two forces act on **two different objects**, so they never cancel each other out for the same object. |
| What goes into a **free-body diagram (FBD)**? | Every force acting *on* the object of interest — weight (`mg`, down), normal force (perpendicular to surface), applied forces, tension, friction (opposing relative motion or attempted motion) — drawn as arrows from a single point representing the object. Forces the object exerts on *other* things do NOT belong on its FBD. |
| What's the difference between **mass** and **weight**? | Mass (`kg`) is the amount of matter in an object — constant everywhere. Weight (`N`) is the gravitational force on that mass, `W = mg` — changes with the local gravitational field (e.g., an object weighs less on the Moon but has the same mass). |
| What is the **normal force**? | The perpendicular contact force a surface exerts on an object, adjusting in magnitude to prevent the object from passing through the surface. It is **not** always equal to `mg` — only on a flat, unaccelerated horizontal surface with no other vertical forces. |
| What's the difference between **static** and **kinetic friction**? | Static friction resists the *start* of relative motion and adjusts its magnitude (up to a maximum, `f_s,max = μ_s N`) to exactly match the applied force until the object starts to move. Kinetic friction acts once the object is already sliding, at a roughly constant value `f_k = μ_k N`, and is usually slightly less than max static friction. |
| How do you handle **equilibrium** problems (`a = 0`)? | Net force is zero, so all forces balance in every direction: `ΣFₓ = 0` and `ΣFᵧ = 0` separately. This doesn't mean no forces are acting — it means they cancel. |
| How does **tension** work in a rope/string? | For an ideal (massless, inextensible) rope over a frictionless pulley, tension is the same throughout the rope. Tension can only *pull*, never push. |
| How do you solve a problem with **two connected objects** (e.g., blocks connected by a string)? | Draw a separate FBD for each object. Write `ΣF = ma` for each, using the same acceleration magnitude for both (since they're connected) and Newton's third law to relate the tension/contact forces between them. |

---

## Key Vocabulary
**Inertia** (resistance to a change in motion, proportional to mass) · **Net force** (vector sum of all forces) · **Normal force** · **Static/kinetic friction coefficients** (`μ_s`, `μ_k`) · **Tension** · **Equilibrium** (`ΣF = 0`) · **Apparent weight** (what a scale reads, which can differ from true weight during acceleration, e.g., in an elevator)

## Summary
Newton's Laws explain *why* motion changes, connecting force, mass, and
acceleration. The most persistent misconceptions are believing that motion
itself requires a force (First Law), forgetting to use the *net* force
(Second Law), and pairing action-reaction forces on the *same* object
instead of different ones (Third Law). A correct, complete free-body
diagram is the single most reliable tool for avoiding all three mistakes.
