# Cornell Notes — Newton's Laws

**Topic:** Forces and Motion | **Date:** ___________ | **Name:** ___________

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| Newton's 1st Law — what does it really say? | An object's velocity (not just position) stays constant unless a **net** force acts on it. This includes objects already moving — they don't need a force to *keep* moving at constant velocity, only to change that velocity. |
| Newton's 2nd Law? | F_net = ma. Acceleration is proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass. Always use **net** force — add up all forces as vectors first. |
| Newton's 3rd Law? | Every force has an equal, opposite reaction force acting on a **different** object. They never cancel each other because they act on two separate bodies, not the same one. |
| Why don't action-reaction pairs cancel out? | Because F_net = ma is computed per object. The "reaction" force acts on the *other* object's FBD, not the one you're analyzing. |
| Mass vs weight — what's the difference? | Mass (kg) = amount of matter, constant everywhere. Weight (N) = force of gravity = mg, and changes if you go to the Moon, Mars, etc. |
| What forces show up in a free-body diagram? | Gravity (mg, always straight down), Normal force (perpendicular to the surface), Applied force, Tension (along a rope/string, pulling), Friction (opposes relative sliding, parallel to surface). Draw ALL forces acting **on** the object — never forces the object exerts on something else. |
| Static vs kinetic friction? | **Static friction** adjusts (0 up to μₛN) to prevent an object from starting to slide. **Kinetic friction** = μₖN, roughly constant once sliding. μₖ is usually less than μₛ — it's "easier" to keep something sliding than to start it. |
| Common incline mistake? | On an incline, the normal force is **not** equal to mg — it's mg cos θ. Gravity's component along the incline (mg sin θ) is what tends to cause sliding. |
| What does "equilibrium" mean? | Net force = 0, so acceleration = 0. The object could be sitting still OR cruising at constant velocity — both are equilibrium. |

## Summary

Newton's Laws connect force to motion. The First Law defines the "default"
(constant velocity, including zero) that a force must fight to change. The
Second Law, F_net = ma, quantifies that relationship — always the *net*
force, found by correctly summing every force in a free-body diagram. The
Third Law is the most commonly misunderstood: action-reaction pairs act on
two different objects and never cancel within a single object's FBD.
Friction requires knowing whether an object is already sliding (kinetic,
roughly constant) or still static (adjusts up to a maximum) — and on an
incline, remember normal force is reduced to mg cos θ, not the full weight.
