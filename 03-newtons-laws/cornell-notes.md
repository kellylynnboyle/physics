# Cornell Notes: Newton's Laws of Motion

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is Newton's First Law? | An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion remains in motion at constant velocity in a straight line, unless acted upon by a **net external force**. Also called the Law of Inertia. |
| What is inertia? | The tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion. Inertia depends only on **mass** — more mass means more inertia, meaning more resistance to acceleration. |
| Common misconception about motion? | Aristotle believed a constant force was needed to keep something moving at constant velocity. This is **false**. A constant velocity means net force = 0. Once moving, an object needs *zero* net force to keep moving at the same speed in the same direction — friction and air resistance are what make it *seem* like force is required to sustain motion in everyday life. |
| What is Newton's Second Law? | F_net = ma. The net (total) force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Acceleration points in the same direction as net force. |
| Units of force? | Newtons (N) = kg·m/s². One Newton accelerates a 1 kg mass at 1 m/s². |
| Do heavier objects fall faster? | No (ignoring air resistance). All objects in free fall near Earth's surface accelerate at g ≈ 9.8 m/s², **regardless of mass**. Although F = ma tempts students to think a heavier object needs more force and thus "more acceleration," the force of gravity itself scales with mass (F = mg), so mass cancels: a = F/m = mg/m = g. Heavier and lighter objects hit the ground at the same time when dropped from the same height (no air resistance). |
| What is Newton's Third Law? | For every action force object A exerts on object B, object B exerts an equal-magnitude, opposite-direction force back on object A. |
| Do action-reaction pairs cancel out? | No — a critical misconception to avoid. The two forces in an action-reaction pair act on **two different objects**, so they can never cancel each other for the motion of a single object. (Forces that *do* cancel for one object are different forces acting on that *same* object, e.g., gravity and normal force.) |
| Example of Third Law pair? | You push on a wall (action); the wall pushes back on you with equal force (reaction). A rocket expels gas backward (action); the gas pushes the rocket forward (reaction) — this is how rocket propulsion works even in the vacuum of space. |
| What is a free-body diagram (FBD)? | A diagram isolating a single object, showing every force acting **on** that object as an arrow (vector) with correct direction and relative magnitude. Forces the object exerts on *other* things are not included. |
| What forces are commonly forgotten in an FBD? | Normal force (from any contact surface), friction (if surfaces are in contact and there's relative motion or tendency to move), and any applied or tension forces. Students often draw gravity but forget the normal force or a relevant friction force. |
| What is normal force? | The perpendicular contact force a surface exerts on an object pressing against it. It adjusts to prevent objects from passing through the surface. |
| Does normal force always equal weight? | **No.** N = mg only on a flat, horizontal surface with no other vertical forces and no vertical acceleration. |
| Normal force on an incline? | N = mg cos θ, where θ is the incline angle from horizontal. As θ increases, N decreases because more of gravity's component acts along the incline surface (mg sin θ) rather than into it. |
| Normal force in an accelerating elevator? | If accelerating upward: N = m(g + a) — you feel heavier. If accelerating downward: N = m(g − a) — you feel lighter. If in free fall (a = g downward): N = 0 — apparent weightlessness. At constant velocity (a = 0): N = mg, same as at rest. |
| Normal force with an applied vertical force? | If a force F is pushed down on an object: N = mg + F. If a force F pulls up on an object: N = mg − F (as long as N ≥ 0). |
| What is static friction? | The friction force that prevents an object from starting to slide. It is a **variable, self-adjusting** force: f_s can be anywhere from 0 up to a maximum value f_s,max = μs·N, matching whatever is needed to prevent sliding, up to that maximum. |
| Is static friction always equal to μs·N? | **No** — common misconception. f_s = μs·N only at the exact verge of slipping (the maximum). For any lesser applied force, f_s is smaller and exactly balances the applied force to keep the object stationary. |
| What is kinetic friction? | The friction force once an object is already sliding: f_k = μk·N, where μk is the coefficient of kinetic friction. This is treated as roughly constant regardless of sliding speed (for typical introductory problems). |
| How do static and kinetic friction compare? | μk is generally **less than** μs, so kinetic friction is generally less than the *maximum* static friction. This is why it takes more force to start an object sliding than to keep it sliding. |
| What is the coefficient of friction? | A unitless number (μ) describing how "rough" the interaction is between two specific surfaces. Different surface pairs have different μs and μk; both are typically between 0 and 1 (though can exceed 1 for very rough or sticky surfaces). |
| Difference between mass and weight? | **Mass** (kg) is the amount of matter in an object and a measure of its inertia — it does not change with location. **Weight** (N) = mg is the force of gravity on that mass and *does* change with the local gravitational field strength (e.g., less on the Moon, more on Jupiter). |
| Example: same object, different weight | A 10 kg object always has a mass of 10 kg. On Earth (g ≈ 9.8 m/s²) it weighs ≈ 98 N; on the Moon (g ≈ 1.6 m/s²) it weighs only ≈ 16 N. Its mass never changes. |
| What is equilibrium? | A state where the **net force on an object is zero** (ΣF = 0), so acceleration = 0. |
| Does equilibrium mean "at rest"? | **No** — a critical misconception. Equilibrium means constant velocity, which includes an object at rest (v = 0) **or** an object moving at a constant speed in a straight line (v = constant ≠ 0). Both are equilibrium states because both have zero acceleration. |
| Static vs. dynamic equilibrium? | Static equilibrium: object is at rest and stays at rest (v = 0). Dynamic equilibrium: object moves at constant velocity (v ≠ 0, constant). Both have F_net = 0. |
| What is tension? | The pulling force transmitted along a string, rope, cable, or chain. For an ideal (massless, inextensible) rope, tension is the same throughout the rope, and a rope can only pull, never push. |
| How do you solve connected-object (system) problems? | Two approaches: (1) Treat all connected objects as **one system** with combined mass to find the shared acceleration using the net external force on the whole system, or (2) analyze **each object separately** with its own FBD, using Newton's Third Law and the fact that connected objects share the same magnitude of acceleration (assuming an inextensible connector). |
| Example: two blocks connected by a string, pulled horizontally | System approach: a = F_applied / (m1 + m2). Then find tension by isolating just one block: T = m2 · a (for the trailing block, assuming no friction). |
| Example: masses over a pulley (Atwood machine) | For two hanging masses m1 > m2 connected over a frictionless, massless pulley: a = (m1 − m2)g / (m1 + m2), and both masses share this acceleration magnitude (one up, one down). Tension is found by applying F_net = ma to either mass individually. |

## Summary

Newton's three laws describe how forces govern motion: the First Law defines equilibrium (zero net force means constant velocity, not necessarily rest), the Second Law quantifies how net force produces acceleration (F_net = ma), and the Third Law explains that forces always come in equal-and-opposite pairs acting on two different objects, so they never cancel for a single object's motion. Correctly analyzing any scenario starts with a careful free-body diagram that includes every force acting on the object — commonly gravity, normal force, friction, tension, and applied forces. Normal force and friction are frequently misunderstood: normal force is not always equal to weight (inclines, accelerating elevators, and applied forces all change it), and static friction is a variable force up to a maximum, not a fixed value. Mass and weight are distinct: mass is invariant while weight depends on the local gravitational field. Multi-object systems connected by strings or in contact can be solved either as a combined system or object-by-object, always using a consistent shared acceleration and Newton's Third Law to relate internal forces like tension.
