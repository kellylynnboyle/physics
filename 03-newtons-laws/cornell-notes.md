## Cues / Questions | Notes

| Cues / Questions | Notes |
|---|---|
| What does Newton's 1st Law really say? | Objects resist changes to their velocity (inertia). Constant velocity (including zero velocity) happens when **net** force = 0 — individual forces can still be present and nonzero, as long as they balance. |
| Common myth to bust | "A moving object needs a constant force to keep moving." False — a hockey puck sliding on frictionless ice needs zero net force to keep its constant velocity forever. |
| What is F_net in F_net = ma? | The **vector sum** of every force acting on the object — not any single applied force. Always find F_net before using the 2nd law. |
| Which direction is acceleration? | Always the same direction as F_net (never the object's velocity direction unless F_net happens to point that way too). |
| What does Newton's 3rd Law actually pair up? | Every force is one half of a pair: Object A pushes on Object B ⇒ Object B pushes back on Object A, equal magnitude, opposite direction. The two forces act on **different objects**, so they can never add up to zero "on" one object. |
| Common myth to bust | "The action and reaction forces cancel out." They can't — they're on two different objects. What might cancel is a *different* pair of forces acting on the *same* object (e.g., gravity and normal force on a book on a table). |
| Is normal force always equal to weight? | No. Normal force adjusts to whatever is needed for equilibrium (or the correct net force) perpendicular to the surface. On an incline, in an elevator accelerating up/down, or with an added vertical force, N ≠ mg. |
| Mass vs. weight — what's the difference? | Mass (kg) = amount of matter, same everywhere. Weight (N) = mg = force of gravity on that mass; changes with location (e.g., 1/6 as much on the Moon) even though mass doesn't. |
| How do I handle an inclined plane? | Rotate your coordinate system so "x" is along the incline and "y" is perpendicular to it. Gravity splits into `mg sinθ` (down the slope) and `mg cosθ` (into the surface). Normal force balances `mg cosθ`, not the full weight. |
| Static vs. kinetic friction? | Static friction (up to `μₛN`) resists the *start* of sliding and matches the applied force until it's exceeded. Kinetic friction (`μₖN`, constant) acts once the object is already sliding. `μₛ` is usually greater than `μₖ`. |

## Free-Body Diagram checklist

1. Isolate **one** object — draw only forces acting *on* it, never forces it exerts on other things.
2. Include gravity (weight, always straight down) unless told to ignore it.
3. Include normal force wherever a surface touches the object (perpendicular to that surface).
4. Include friction (parallel to the surface, opposing relative motion or attempted motion) if surfaces are in contact and not frictionless.
5. Include tension along any ropes/strings, pulling *away* from the object.
6. Include any other applied/push/pull forces stated in the problem.
7. Sum forces along each axis separately; set equal to `ma` (or 0 for equilibrium) along each axis.

## Summary

Newton's laws connect force and motion: the 1st law defines when
nothing needs explaining (zero net force → constant velocity), the 2nd
law quantifies what happens when forces are unbalanced (`F_net = ma`),
and the 3rd law explains that forces always come in equal-and-opposite
pairs acting on two different objects — never a self-canceling pair on
one object. Free-body diagrams are the practical tool that turns a
word problem into a solvable one: isolate the object, list every real
force acting on it, and apply the 2nd law along each axis.
