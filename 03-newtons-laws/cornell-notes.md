# Cornell Notes: Newton's Laws of Motion

**Topic/Date:** _____________

| Cue / Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| What does Newton's First Law say? | An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a net external force. Also called the law of inertia. No net force is needed to *maintain* constant velocity — only to change it. |
| What is inertia? | The tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion. More mass means more inertia, so more massive objects are harder to speed up, slow down, or turn. |
| What does Newton's Second Law say, and what is the equation? | The net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration: F_net = m a. Acceleration increases with more net force and decreases with more mass. Direction of acceleration matches direction of net force. |
| What does Newton's Third Law say? | For every action force one object exerts on another, the second object exerts an equal and opposite reaction force back on the first. The two forces act on two *different* objects, so they never cancel each other. |
| How are mass and weight different? | Mass (kg) is the amount of matter in an object and stays the same everywhere. Weight (N) is the force of gravity acting on that mass, calculated as W = m g, and it changes depending on the local value of g (e.g., less on the Moon). |
| What is a free-body diagram? | A diagram of a single object showing every external force acting on it as a labeled arrow (gravity, normal, tension, friction, applied force). It is used to organize forces before applying F_net = m a. |
| What is the normal force, and does it always equal weight? | The perpendicular contact force a surface exerts on an object. It equals weight only in simple flat-surface cases with no other vertical forces — it differs on inclines, with additional vertical forces, or during vertical acceleration (like in an elevator). |
| How do static and kinetic friction differ? | Static friction (f_s ≤ μ_s N) resists the start of sliding and adjusts to match applied force up to a maximum. Kinetic friction (f_k = μ_k N) acts once sliding has begun and stays roughly constant for a given surface pair and normal force. |
| What is tension? | The pulling force transmitted through a rope, string, or cable, directed along the connector and pulling away from the object at each end. |
| What is equilibrium, and what condition defines it? | A state where the net force on an object is zero (ΣF = 0). This includes both an object at rest and an object moving at constant velocity — "no acceleration" does not mean "no forces." |

## Summary

Newton's three laws together describe a complete framework for analyzing motion: the first law establishes that objects resist changes in velocity unless pushed or pulled by a net force, the second law quantifies that relationship as F_net = m a, and the third law explains that every force comes paired with an equal and opposite force acting on a different object. Free-body diagrams are the practical tool that ties these ideas together, letting us organize forces like gravity, normal force, tension, and friction before solving for unknowns. A key distinction to keep straight is that mass is an unchanging amount of matter while weight is a location-dependent force of gravity, and similarly, static friction (which adjusts up to a maximum) behaves differently from kinetic friction (which stays roughly constant during sliding). Perhaps the most important shift in thinking this unit demands is recognizing that "no motion" or "constant velocity" both mean the net force is zero, not that forces are absent — objects in equilibrium can have several forces acting on them that simply balance out.
