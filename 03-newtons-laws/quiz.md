# Unit 3 — Newton's Laws — Self-Check Quiz

Built from the questions students miss most often on Newton's Laws tests. Try each question yourself before revealing the answer.

**How to use:** Get 80%+ (8/10) before moving on. Re-read the linked note section for anything you miss.

---

**1.** A book rests on a table. Identify the Newton's 3rd Law reaction pair to "the table pushes up on the book."

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**The book pushes down on the table.** (Not "gravity pulls the book down" — that's a different force entirely, and it pairs with "the book pulls up on the Earth.")

*Most-missed reason:* students pair the normal force with gravity because they balance each other on the book, but a true 3rd-law pair must be the *same type of force*, acting on *two different objects*, swapped.
</details>

---

**2.** A 2 kg ball and a 20 kg ball are dropped from the same height (ignore air resistance). Which hits the ground first?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**They hit at the same time.** F = mg, and a = F/m = mg/m = g — mass cancels out, so both accelerate at 9.8 m/s² regardless of mass.

*Most-missed reason:* students assume "more force = falls faster," forgetting that more mass also means more inertia resisting that force, and the two effects exactly cancel.
</details>

---

**3.** A car moves at constant velocity in a straight line. What can you conclude about the net force acting on it?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Net force = 0.** Constant velocity (including zero velocity) always means the object is in equilibrium — by Newton's 1st law.

*Most-missed reason:* students think "moving" automatically implies "a force is pushing it," not realizing constant velocity requires balanced forces, not zero forces.
</details>

---

**4.** A 10 kg box is pushed with 30 N horizontally; friction opposes with 12 N. Find the box's acceleration.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Net force = 30 − 12 = 18 N. a = F/m = 18/10 = **1.8 m/s²**

*Most-missed reason:* forgetting to subtract friction before dividing by mass — using the applied force alone instead of the *net* force.
</details>

---

**5.** True or False: heavier objects always require more force to start sliding than lighter objects (same surface).

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**True, generally** — because static friction depends on normal force (f_s(max) = μ_s N), and a heavier object has a larger normal force (N = mg on a flat surface), so it takes more force to overcome friction. But it is *not* because of mass/inertia directly — it's because of the larger normal force.

*Most-missed reason:* students correctly guess "true" but explain it with inertia instead of the friction formula, missing the actual mechanism being tested.
</details>

---

**6.** A rocket expels exhaust gas downward at high speed. Explain, using Newton's 3rd law, why the rocket accelerates upward.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The rocket pushes the exhaust gas **downward** (action); the exhaust gas pushes the rocket **upward** with equal magnitude (reaction). Since these act on *different objects* (gas vs. rocket), they don't cancel — the rocket experiences a net upward force.

*Most-missed reason:* students say "the gas pushes off the air/ground," but rockets work in the vacuum of space too — the reaction is against the expelled gas itself, not against the atmosphere.
</details>

---

**7.** A 5 kg block hangs at rest from a single rope. What is the tension in the rope?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

At rest → equilibrium → T = weight = mg = 5 × 9.8 = **49 N**

*Most-missed reason:* students forget that "at rest" means net force = 0, so tension must exactly equal weight, not some other value derived from the rope's properties.
</details>

---

**8.** Two forces act on an object: 30 N east and 30 N west. What is the object's acceleration?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Zero.** The forces are equal and opposite, so net force = 0, and by F = ma, a = 0 regardless of the object's mass.

*Most-missed reason:* students try to add the magnitudes (30 + 30 = 60 N) instead of treating force as a vector and recognizing the forces cancel.
</details>

---

**9.** A 6 kg block sits on a frictionless 30° incline. Find its acceleration down the ramp. (g = 9.8 m/s²)

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

a = g·sin(θ) = 9.8 × sin(30°) = 9.8 × 0.5 = **4.9 m/s²** down the incline.

*Most-missed reason:* students use the full g instead of the component along the incline (g·sinθ), or confuse sin and cos (cosθ is used for the normal force, not the acceleration along the slope).
</details>

---

**10.** An astronaut in deep space (far from any gravity) pushes off a space station wall. Will they continue moving forever at constant velocity?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Yes** (idealized) — with no net force acting (no friction, no gravity), Newton's 1st law says they continue at constant velocity indefinitely.

*Most-missed reason:* students assume motion must "run out" over time/distance the way it does on Earth, forgetting that this intuition comes from friction and air resistance, not some property of motion itself.
</details>
